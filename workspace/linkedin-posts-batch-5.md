## Post 1 — Budget Reality for AI Talent

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

The number studios budget for a senior AI production leadership role and the number that actually closes the search are usually different.

Not because studios are being deliberately low.

Because the benchmarks are wrong.

The natural reference point for hiring inside creative production is what equivalent roles in that industry pay. Head of Production. VFX Supervisor. Technical Director. Those bands are well understood.

AI production leadership doesn't come from that pool exclusively. The people who can lead meaningful AI transformation at senior level have often passed through technology companies, research organisations, or AI-native businesses before landing in creative contexts.

Their compensation expectations reflect that history.

Studios benchmarking against creative industry peers are competing on different numbers than the candidate across the table. The gap only becomes visible after three rounds of interviews.

The fix isn't simply paying more. It's building the brief with an honest picture of where this talent actually comes from, and setting compensation expectations before the process starts.

I have this conversation in every retained briefing. The studios that front-load it close faster and lose fewer offers at the final stage.

What's your process for compensation benchmarking when the role sits across two different talent markets?

P.S. The most expensive thing about getting this wrong isn't the increase at offer stage. It's the three months you lose starting the search again.

## Post 2 — Why Job Specs Get It Wrong

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

The job specs for senior AI production roles are usually written to describe the person who last held something similar.

That's the first problem.

When there's no clear predecessor, the brief defaults to a hybrid: half technical architecture requirement, half creative leadership expectation. It looks comprehensive and screens out the right candidates.

Here's what I see consistently across briefs in this space.

Tools listed as hard requirements that are actually context. "Proven experience with [specific platform]" when what the business actually needs is someone who can evaluate any platform and make the right call.

Responsibilities framed around process, not outcomes. Describing what the person will do day to day rather than what they'll be accountable for building or changing.

Seniority signals borrowed from the wrong market. Credentials and company-name recognition that belong to research hiring, not production leadership.

The spec shapes the shortlist before you speak to anyone.

Getting the brief wrong doesn't just slow the search. It closes off the best candidates at the first filter, before a single conversation has happened.

What would you change in a typical AI creative production brief if you started from outcomes rather than credentials?

P.S. The best brief I've worked from in this space was two pages. The one that produced the longest shortlist was eight.

## Post 3 — Build vs Hire Decisions

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

The build versus hire question for AI production leadership comes up in almost every retained conversation I have.

Studios are right to ask it.

The instinct to develop internally is genuine and usually well-intentioned. There's institutional knowledge, cultural alignment, and cost efficiency on the build side. It's not a wrong question.

But the timeline changes the answer.

Building a senior AI production leader from within a creative company takes two to three years minimum if the raw material is there. That assumes the person exists on the current team, wants the path, has a clear development programme, and the business can hold that space while they grow into it.

Hiring externally closes in three to five months.

If the business is under competitive pressure, losing clients to studios that already have this capability, or has AI integration milestones tied to a production slate, the timeline isn't flexible.

The studios getting this decision right aren't choosing between build and hire as philosophies.

They're asking what the business needs by when, and working backwards from there.

If you've faced this decision recently, what tipped it one way or the other?

P.S. Internal development and external hire don't have to be sequential. Some of the best outcomes I've seen involved bringing in external leadership specifically to accelerate the internal team.

## Post 4 — Speed to Hire in a Competitive Market

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

The gap between first interview and offer acceptance for senior AI production roles has shortened considerably.

The process timelines most studios operate on haven't moved with it.

Four-stage interview processes designed for 20-candidate shortlists don't work when the active pool for a senior AI production brief is eight people. By stage three, the person you want has either accepted something else or changed their position.

This isn't a new problem in executive search. It is a new problem for industries that haven't historically competed for this type of talent.

What actually closes searches at this level is a process built around the candidate's decision, not the committee's calendar. That means knowing what internal stakeholders need to see before interviews start, compressing the stages that exist for coordination rather than evaluation, and having a clear offer position before the final conversation.

Not skipping diligence.

Skipping the stages that only exist because nobody mapped the process to the actual hiring goal.

Studios that do this well move from brief to offer in six to eight weeks. The ones still running 14-week processes lose offers they would have closed.

What part of your hiring process would you restructure if you were redesigning it for today's market?

P.S. The fastest search I've closed at VP level took 28 working days. The brief was clear, the process was clean, and the right person was ready.
