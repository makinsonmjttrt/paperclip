## June overarching themes

### Theme 1 — Evidence

CreativAI is far enough into operation to speak from what the practice is actually seeing, not just predicting. The dominant June voice is observational and first-person: "here's what the inbound briefs are telling us," "here's what the early conversations revealed," "here's the pattern that keeps appearing."

This is the strongest authority-building mode available. Observation from practice is more credible than assertion from positioning. Posts under this theme draw on real brief patterns, candidate conversation insight, and what the supply side looks like in practice.

**Tone:** Direct, specific, grounded. No claims that require sourcing. First-person Martyn.

### Theme 2 — Event observation

Four events in June create four moments to position Martyn as someone who reads industry signals and translates them into talent implications. Not as a commentator, not as an AI expert — as a specialist recruiter who understands what technology shifts mean for who studios need to hire.

The approach: take what a session agenda, a keynote theme, or a product announcement signals about where production is heading, and map that to the capability gaps that will open over the next 12–18 months.

**Tone:** Curious, translating. Not "AI is changing everything" — that's noise. Instead: "this specific signal tells us this specific thing about where the talent brief is heading."

### Theme 3 — Community / dialogue

June is when the transition to two-way conversation starts. Engagement posts, direct questions, inviting the network to share perspective. The format from Batch 17 P1 (27 May) — the open question post — extends into June in a lighter form.

This theme is not about weakening authority. It is about using that authority to invite dialogue rather than just broadcast. The most credible senior voices on LinkedIn ask as well as tell.

**Tone:** Invitational. Confident enough to put a genuine question to the room. Not seeking validation — seeking perspective.

### Post P2 — Client-facing | Tue 3 Jun

**Working title:** Six weeks in: what the inbound briefs are telling us about where studios are getting stuck

**Target audience:** Studio heads, Heads of Production, Creative Directors, Founders — client side

**Pillar:** Market intelligence / Practice observation

**Angle:**
Six weeks of CreativAI briefs produce a pattern. The briefs that lead to strong shortlists share something in common: they define what the person needs to *deliver*, not which tools they need to *know*. The briefs that lead to short, frustrated searches are consistently tool-led — "proficiency with X," "experience of Y in production" — when the underlying need is something different.

This is not a client lecture. The voice is "here's what I'm observing across the briefs we're seeing." The insight for clients: a brief that leads with tool familiarity narrows the field to candidates who are fluent in today's capabilities but may not adapt when those tools change in eighteen months. A brief that leads with output capability and production judgment opens the field to a much stronger pool.

Post should not name clients or specific tools. Directional, observational, first-person.

**Structure guidance:** Opening observation from brief patterns → what separates briefs that work → why tool-led briefs underperform → what the alternative looks like → close that invites conversation (what are you putting in your briefs?).

**Word count target:** 190–230 words
**Schedule slot:** Tue 3 Jun (confirmed)

### Post P3 — Candidate-facing | Thu 5 Jun

**Working title:** The question that tells me within three minutes whether a candidate understands AI creative production

**Target audience:** Senior candidates — Heads of AI Production, Technical Directors, Creative Technologists, VPs of Production with AI scope

**Pillar:** Talent intelligence / Candidate advice

**Angle:**
There is one question that appears in almost every senior AI creative production conversation. Most candidates answer it as a tool competency question. The ones who land the roles answer it as a production philosophy question.

The question itself is not the reveal — what it surfaces is. Strong candidates describe a body of work that includes what the tooling couldn't do, what the brief forced them to work around, where the process failed and why they would do it differently. That level of honest complexity is a strong signal of real production experience. Candidates who give a clean success narrative with a tool list are signalling something different.

Martyn to write this as a specific, first-person observation. Should feel like a genuine insight from placement conversations, not a career advice post. No naming of specific candidates.

**Structure guidance:** Setup (the question exists, here's the context) → what most candidates do → what strong candidates do differently → the underlying reason it matters → close (what does your answer look like?).

**Word count target:** 180–210 words
**Schedule slot:** Thu 5 Jun (confirmed)

## LinkedIn article idea for June

**Title:** Why AI creative leadership hires fail in year one

**Format:** LinkedIn Article (long-form, not a post)
**Length:** 900–1,200 words
**Target publish:** w/c 16 June (during Cannes/AWE window — credibility anchor for senior audience)

**Angle:**
The candidates who land the right AI creative leadership roles and then struggle in year one are not usually the wrong people. They are usually in under-defined mandates.

When a studio hires an AI creative leader before resolving what that function should own, year one gets spent lobbying for scope rather than delivering against it. The hire becomes what the person can defend, not what the business actually needs.

This is not a failure of candidate quality. It is a failure of org readiness. Studios treating the AI leadership hire as a headcount decision rather than an org design decision are setting up both the hire and themselves for a difficult first twelve months.

**Structure:**
1. Opening: the pattern (placed someone strong, they struggled — not because of ability)
2. The root cause: mandate ambiguity and what it costs (candidate and studio)
3. What studios are getting wrong: confusing "who should we hire" with "what should this role own"
4. What org readiness looks like before the brief opens
5. Close: CreativAI conversations start before the brief, not after — that's the point

**Audience:** Studio heads, founders, COOs — senior client-facing authority piece
**Note:** No statistics requiring sourcing. All observations drawn from FPZ placement experience and inbound brief patterns.
