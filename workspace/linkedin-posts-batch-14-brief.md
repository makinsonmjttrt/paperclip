### Post 1 — CreativAI: one month in (Both / consolidation anchor)

**Publish:** Tue 5 May

**Brief:** CreativAI has been operational for roughly three weeks by the time this publishes. Post 1 is a reflection post — but not a PR one. The brief is to surface what the practice's first month has confirmed about the market: not what we hoped it would find, but what the actual conversations with studios and candidates have shown.

The angle that makes this different from Batch 12 P1 ("first week"): that was immediate reaction. This is pattern recognition. The first week produced curiosity. The first month has produced recurring questions.

**Core argument:** The conversations we expected coming into the practice are not quite the conversations we're having. Studios aren't asking "who should we hire." They're asking structural questions about what the function should own, who it should report to, and what success looks like in year one. Candidates aren't asking "is the market right for a move." They're asking whether a specific studio has genuinely thought through the mandate or whether they're hiring to say they've hired. Those are harder questions than title or compensation. They're also the right questions. CreativAI was built for exactly this conversation, and the first month confirms it's the one that needs to be had.

**Hook direction:** Open with the observation that the questions coming in are different from what you'd expect. Something like: "A month in, the questions coming through CreativAI are not what I assumed they'd be." Or lead with the structural question pattern directly.

**CTA:** Soft — one line noting that if this is the conversation you're in, CreativAI is set up for it. Not salesy. One sentence.

### Post 2 — FMX 2026: what the room reveals (Both / event anchor)

**Publish:** Wed 6 May (concurrent with FMX Stuttgart)

**Brief:** This is an FMX signal post — but different from Batch 10 P1, which was a general observation about conference narrative versus talent reality. That angle was broad and evergreen. This one is specific to FMX 2026 and what this year's event says about the shift in the industry's relationship with AI.

The angle: Two years ago, FMX conversations were about what AI would do. A year ago, they were about whether to invest. This year, they're about structure and hiring — who owns the AI function, what the role looks like, how to build the capability. The shift from aspiration to operations is measurable in the questions people are asking each other between sessions.

That shift matters for recruitment. It means studios are no longer in a research phase. They're in a hiring phase. The FMX room is the cleanest signal that the market has crossed that threshold.

**Hook direction:** Open on the shift in the conversation — something like: "FMX this year sounds different. Not in the sessions. In the corridors." Or lead with the observation that the event has moved from AI aspiration to AI infrastructure. Make it feel like Martyn is in that room.

**CTA:** Invitation — "Curious what others are taking from FMX this year. What's the conversation you keep having?" One line.

**Note for Content Specialist:** Keep this specific to this year's mood, not a general observation about conferences. The contrast with previous years is what gives it authority.

### Post 3 — The structural supply shortage (Client-facing)

**Publish:** Thu 7 May

**Brief:** This post addresses why the AI creative leadership market is genuinely hard to hire in — but from a different angle than the posts that have already covered it. B9-P2 ("great candidates aren't on market long") was about velocity: when a strong candidate appears, the window closes fast. B7-P1 ("cost of slow hiring") was about process speed. This post is about supply structure: why the pool is small in the first place.

The argument: The constraint in the AI creative leadership market isn't demand. Plenty of studios want to hire. The constraint is that genuine experience at scale only comes from having built and delivered AI production work over time. The people who have 3–5 years of real AI production delivery at commercial scale were the ones who started early — 2020, 2021 — when the tooling was rough and the projects were experimental. That cohort is small. It's growing, but slowly. Experience is not accelerated by demand.

What this means for studios: the candidate you're describing in your brief probably already has a role they find interesting. They're not on job boards. They're working. Getting in front of them requires a network built in those early years, not a search firm running keywords on a database.

**Hook direction:** Open on the supply constraint — not the competition. Something like: "The AI production leadership market is tight — but not because everyone is competing for the same candidate. It's tight because the pool was defined by who started early." Or: "Studios are competing to hire AI creative leaders. That's not the problem. The problem is that experience at this level takes years to develop, and most people started recently."

**CTA:** Implied authority — one line noting that this is why specialist network matters more than volume in this search. Not a direct sales line. Something that positions FPZ/CreativAI as having been in the room when the experience was being built.

### Post 4 — Before you accept an AI creative role (Candidate-facing)

**Publish:** Fri 8 May

**Brief:** This is candidate-facing placement intelligence. Different from Batch 10 P4 (which was about what actually motivates AI creative professionals to move — the internal drivers). This post is tactical: before you accept, ask these things.

The argument: Strong candidates take roles that look good on paper and underdeliver in the first year — not because the candidate was wrong, but because the structural conditions weren't right. The way to avoid this is to ask the right questions before the offer is accepted. These questions don't come from a careers blog. They come from post-placement conversations where the patterns of underdelivery are visible.

The questions (Content Specialist: build the post around these, don't list them as bullet points — weave them into paragraphs):
1. What does this function own, specifically? Not "what will you be involved in" — what is yours to deliver?
2. Does the tooling and infrastructure budget exist now, or will I spend year one lobbying for it?
3. Who is my internal champion, and how much political capital do they have?
4. What does successful year one look like — how will we both know it's worked?
5. Why does this role not exist yet — or, if someone was in it before, what happened?

The tone should be that of someone who has watched these hires go well and badly, and knows where the failure points are. Not a lecture. Practical intelligence from active placement experience.

**Hook direction:** Open on the gap between role on paper and role in practice. Something like: "Before you accept an AI creative production role, there are questions that will tell you more than the job description did." Or lead with the observation that strong candidates take the wrong roles — and the failure is structural, not personal.

**CTA:** Direct invite — "If you're evaluating something right now and want a candid view on what to ask before you decide, DM me." One sentence.
