## Post 1 — Monday 21 April 2026

Spatial computing has an industry.

It doesn't yet have a talent pipeline to match.

Studios and agencies building in XR, immersive, and location-based entertainment have grown fast enough that senior leadership roles now exist. The infrastructure, the funding, the projects — all there.

What isn't there yet: the small pool of people who can lead across spatial production, real-time rendering, and creative direction simultaneously. At the very senior level, that pool is genuinely small.

The companies that figured this out fastest aren't waiting for the pipeline to catch up.

They're not posting jobs.

They're having conversations now, before they have an open brief, with people who may not be actively considering a move.

That's the approach the CreativAI practice at FourPointZero takes across spatial computing, immersive design, and AI-integrated production. Not reactive search, not job board advertising. Conversations with the specific people who've actually delivered at this level, before there's urgency.

If you're building leadership in this space, the pipeline problem is real.

But it's solvable if you start early.

What's the hardest leadership gap to fill in your spatial or immersive team right now?

P.S. The most capable candidates in spatial computing are employed, not looking, and getting approached regularly. The first conversation that sounds genuinely informed is the one that gets a response.

## Post 2 — Tuesday 22 April 2026

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

You don't need to understand the model architecture.

You need to understand what shipped.

I speak to hiring panels at studios and agencies interviewing for AI-integrated production roles who aren't sure how to evaluate the candidates in front of them. The technical depth is genuinely difficult to assess without a technical co-interviewer.

Here's what I've found works, regardless of your own technical background.

𝗔𝘀𝗸 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘆 𝗰𝗵𝗼𝘀𝗲 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝘂𝘀𝗲.

Candidates who've actually led AI adoption at production scale can tell you which tools they evaluated and rejected, and why. That answer reveals judgment. A list of tools adopted reveals enthusiasm.

𝗔𝘀𝗸 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗯𝗿𝗼𝗸𝗲 𝗶𝗻 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻.

Real experience always has war stories. Anyone who has genuinely shipped AI-integrated work can tell you exactly what failed, what the downstream consequences were, and what changed. Smooth-sounding answers are a warning sign.

𝗔𝘀𝗸 𝗵𝗼𝘄 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘆 𝗲𝘅𝗽𝗹𝗮𝗶𝗻𝗲𝗱 𝗶𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗶𝗿 𝘁𝗲𝗮𝗺.

AI integration at senior level is fundamentally a communication and change management challenge. How they describe their approach to the people working under them tells you more than any tool list.

What's the hardest interview question you've found for this type of role?

P.S. The candidates who can answer all three clearly and specifically, without qualification or jargon, are extremely rare. When you find one, move quickly.

## Post 3 — Thursday 24 April 2026

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

VFX studios have defaulted to network hiring for 30 years.

That model breaks for one type of role.

The traditional approach in visual effects is well understood: tap the network, move people through the same handful of studios, work through relationships built on previous productions.

It's efficient when the candidate pool is large and the role definition is settled.

Neither is true for AI leadership.

The people who can lead AI transformation in a VFX production environment don't come from a single industry. Their careers span production technology, real-time pipelines, research contexts, and creative direction in ways that traditional VFX networks simply don't reach.

Posting on the usual boards and calling the usual contacts doesn't find them.

It never did.

Retained search, structured and relationship-driven, crossing the boundary between technical and creative talent markets, is how this work actually gets done.

Studios that understand this start the conversation before they have the brief.

Studios that don't wait until there's urgency.

What usually happens next is predictable.

Has the way your studio finds senior technical leadership changed in the last two or three years?

P.S. The first retained AI leadership search I ran in VFX identified the right candidate in week three. The role had been open for six months before we were brought in.

## Post 4 — Friday 25 April 2026

**[LINKEDIN COPY — PASTE BELOW THIS LINE]**

Ask senior AI leaders in creative production why they'd move.

Equity rarely leads the answer.

In the candidate conversations I have at this level, the motivators are consistent. They don't match what most studios assume when building the offer.

𝗔𝗰𝗰𝗲𝘀𝘀 𝘁𝗼 𝗿𝗲𝗮𝗹 𝘁𝗲𝗰𝗵𝗻𝗼𝗹𝗼𝗴𝘆 𝗱𝗲𝗰𝗶𝘀𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀. Not a seat in meetings. Actual authority to choose, reject, and shape tooling and pipeline architecture. The people who've built something significant want to build again, not manage what someone else built.

𝗧𝗲𝗮𝗺 𝗾𝘂𝗮𝗹𝗶𝘁𝘆 𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗿 𝘁𝗲𝗮𝗺 𝘀𝗶𝘇𝗲. A smaller, highly capable team working on interesting production problems is more compelling than a large organisation where talent is spread thin. What they're building with matters more than how many people are building it.

𝗖𝗿𝗲𝗮𝘁𝗶𝘃𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘁𝗲𝘅𝘁, 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗷𝘂𝘀𝘁 𝘁𝗲𝗰𝗵𝗻𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘁𝗲𝘅𝘁. The most sought-after candidates in this space didn't come from research labs or pure engineering backgrounds. They came from production. They want to stay connected to what's actually being made.

What they don't need persuading on: companies that already have AI production credentials and can demonstrate it in the first conversation. That's the fastest path to a yes.

What's missing from the way most briefs in this space describe what's on offer?

P.S. The best offer I've seen rejected recently wasn't missing money. It was missing a clear answer to "what will I actually be responsible for building?"
