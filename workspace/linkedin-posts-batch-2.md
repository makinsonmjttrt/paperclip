## Post 2 — Tuesday 15 April 2026
**Pillar 3 | Tactical | Five questions before rewriting a senior role brief**
**Hook type:** Fear/Warning
**Format:** Numbered list with bold headers

---

Before you repost that job spec, ask yourself five questions.

It'll save you three months.

**1. Is this brief actually new?**
Or is it a retitled version of the role that's been quietly open for ninety days? The title changes. The unspoken requirements that killed the last search usually don't.

**2. Who internally could do this job?**
If nobody, that's useful data. If someone could but isn't being considered, understand why before going external. The answer almost always shapes the brief.

**3. Does the spec describe what needs doing — or how the previous person did it?**
The two are rarely identical. Writing to the past creates briefs that filter out exactly the profile you actually need.

**4. Have you spoken to someone who turned this role down?**
Counteroffers, rejections, and near-misses carry intelligence no brief review captures. One honest conversation with someone who said no will tell you more than three rounds of internal alignment.

**5. Is compensation benchmarked against where this candidate is coming from?**
AI-creative talent doesn't sit neatly inside creative industry salary bands. Benchmarking against your existing team means you're competing with a different starting number than the candidate across the table.

Which of these is hardest to get honest about internally? Drop a number.

P.S. I ask these in every retained briefing. They've saved more searches than any sourcing tactic.

## Post 3 — Thursday 17 April 2026
**Pillar 1 | Contrarian | AI tools vs AI judgment in VFX**
**Hook type:** Contrarian/myth-bust
**Format:** Contrarian rant

---

The studios getting AI wrong aren't using too little of it.

They're using too much of the wrong kind, without the leadership to know when to stop.

The pattern is consistent.

Production teams experimenting with generative tools at the asset level, while the editorial pipeline, scheduling architecture, and delivery infrastructure stays entirely unchanged.

AI at the edges.

Analogue at the core.

That's not an AI pipeline.

It's decoration.

What studios need isn't another tool licence or a quarterly subscriptions audit.

It's a senior leader who can stand in front of a production workflow and say: this is where AI genuinely saves time, and this is where it introduces fragility that won't show up until post.

That judgment is rare.

Most of the senior people I work with who have actually delivered AI at production scale can tell you which tools they chose not to use as quickly as the ones they adopted.

The hard competency isn't adoption.

It's selection.

What's been your experience? I'm curious whether the studios getting it right look different internally.

P.S. The most common gap I see in AI production briefs: they list tools, not judgment. The hiring process usually reflects it.

## Post 4 — Friday 18 April 2026
**Pillar 2 | Personal story | What makes senior creative tech talent move**
**Hook type:** Time-stamped personal story
**Format:** Story-lesson-CTA

---

In six years of placing creative tech leadership, the most important hires rarely appeared on a job board.

They were already employed.

Doing exceptional work.

Not actively looking.

What moved them wasn't the role.

It was the first approach.

Senior AI-creative professionals at this level receive recruiter outreach every week.

Most of it goes unread.

The messages that get a response aren't better-written job ads.

They reference something specific: a project, a pipeline decision, a technical problem the person has actually written or spoken about publicly.

They signal: I understand what you do, and I understand why this might interest you specifically.

The consistent thread across every search I've run at VP level and above is not sourcing strategy or LinkedIn premium credits.

It's that the first conversation didn't sound like recruitment.

It sounded like two people who operate in the same industry, talking about what's changing in it.

What's your experience on the other side of this — what's made you respond to an approach you might otherwise have ignored?

P.S. The best responses I've ever had from candidates came from messages under 80 words that proved I'd read their work.
