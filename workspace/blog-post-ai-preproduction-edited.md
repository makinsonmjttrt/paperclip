The brief that lands in my inbox for a senior AI role in a spatial computing environment usually reads like a technical director specification. Clear on the stack, specific about the engines and the headset pipeline, with a line at the bottom about AI integration experience. When I call the studio to map it properly, the brief that emerges from the conversation is almost always different. They do not need someone to manage the tools. They need someone to own the decision about how those tools enter the creative process, and when they should not.

That distinction is where most spatial computing AI searches go wrong before they have started.

---

## The spatial computing AI gap is different from the VFX AI gap

In VFX, the AI hiring problem follows a recognisable shape. The pipeline had decades of established practice before generative tools arrived. Studios know what a VFX Supervisor or a Pipeline TD looks like. The AI question is: can this person who already understands the environment also apply new tools within it? The candidate pool is constrained, but the existing categories are legible.

Spatial computing does not work like that. The discipline itself was still maturing as a commercial field at the same time AI capability arrived. Enterprise AR, location-based XR, virtual production with spatial delivery: these workflows were being invented in parallel with the AI tools that are now expected to run inside them. The people who needed to develop deep spatial computing leadership experience and real AI production fluency had to do both at the same time, during years when neither field had a well-worn commercial path.

This is what I call the double frontier problem. Two emerging disciplines developing simultaneously. The candidate who has genuine depth in both is not just hard to find. They were built in circumstances that most studios cannot reverse-engineer from a CV.

---

## What these roles actually require

The brief for a senior AI role in a spatial computing production rarely maps cleanly onto an existing job category. The work sits at the intersection of the spatial layer and the AI layer, and both halves of that require real production literacy.

The spatial layer: understanding how real-time engines behave under production conditions, how headset pipeline constraints shape creative decisions, how spatial audio integrates with visual experience design, how enterprise AR environments differ from consumer XR in their content requirements. This is not theoretical. The person you need has shipped things in this environment, often before the commercial wave made it a commonly understood discipline.

The AI layer: how generative tools enter a production workflow, where ML-assisted content creation creates genuine efficiency and where it creates problems, how to evaluate output quality when the reference points for "correct" are themselves being established in real time. The best candidates in this space are not enthusiastic about the tools. They have formed opinions about their limits.

What studios often write when they try to describe this role looks like a technology leadership brief. What they are actually hiring is a creative technology leader: someone whose judgment can operate in the gap between disciplines, and whose credibility works on both sides of the table.

## Why the candidate pool is smaller than studios assume

The timing argument has a practical consequence that studios consistently underestimate.

The people who have both spatial computing depth and AI production fluency were building spatial experiences between roughly 2019 and 2022, during years when commercial appetite for the category was limited. They were not building CVs. They were building things, often at small studios, technology research labs, or early-stage companies working on enterprise AR or location-based XR projects that never had wide visibility.

By the time the commercial wave arrived (Apple Vision Pro, Meta Horizon OS expansion, enterprise AR deployment at scale), these people were already embedded. Senior roles, established relationships, ongoing projects. They are not on job boards. Most of them do not have polished LinkedIn profiles because they never needed them. The signal-to-noise problem in trying to reach this cohort through conventional means is severe. Outreach from a recruiter they have no reason to recognise gets filtered as noise.

Studios that have found the right candidate for this type of role have almost never found them through a job posting. They have found them through a connection that already existed, or through a recruiter who already had the connection.

The professional communities where spatial computing talent actually congregates are largely invisible to AI recruiters and generalist agencies. AIXR and VRARA are the two that matter most in this space. Between them, they represent the closest thing the spatial computing sector has to a practitioner community at leadership level. The events, the networks, the informal channels where senior people exchange information about what is working in production. These are where the relevant candidates can actually be found.

## What to do if you have this brief

If you are hiring a senior AI role in a spatial computing or XR production environment, three things are worth knowing before you formalise the mandate.

First: the brief definition matters more than usual. Roles written by a technology function and roles written by a creative production team can look entirely different on paper: different titles, different skill emphasis, different seniority signals, and yet describing the same need. Getting clarity on what the role actually requires, from someone who understands both sides, is worth doing before you open the search.

Second: the timeline is longer than studios typically expect. A qualified shortlist for a senior spatial computing AI role realistically takes ten to fourteen weeks from a cold start. That is not because the search is slow. It is because the pool is small and passive, and reaching the right candidates through the right channels takes time.

Third: starting the conversation early changes the outcome. The best candidates in this space are usually considering one or two opportunities by the time a formal mandate opens. If you anticipate this hire six months out rather than six weeks out, the options improve significantly.
