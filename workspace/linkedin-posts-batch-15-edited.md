## Post 1 — What a Strong AI Production Candidate Profile Actually Looks Like in 2026

**Pillar: Talent Intelligence | Candidate Advice | Audience: Senior AI creative production professionals | Schedule: Thu 14 May**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Observation from placement work → contrast → underlying reason → actionable close**

---

The candidates landing the strongest AI production roles in 2026 are not the ones with the longest tools list.

I keep seeing the same pattern in placement work.

Strong profiles don't lead with fluency. They lead with decisions made under constraint. What they prioritised. What they cut. What they'd do differently on the next brief.

Studios hiring at this level are not testing whether someone knows the platform. They're working out whether this person has built real production capability, or supervised workflows someone else designed.

The difference shows up most clearly in how candidates answer the AI experience question.

Strong candidates describe a body of work with honest complexity. What the tooling couldn't do. Where the brief pushed beyond what existed. Where the process failed and what they learned from it.

Weak candidates give a list of platforms and a clean success narrative.

The clean narrative is a red flag. Production at this level doesn't deliver cleanly. Everyone knows that. A candidate who describes only the wins hasn't yet built the thing.

If you're positioning for an AI production leadership role right now, the most useful thing you can do is audit your narrative. Not your tool list. Your decisions.

What did you cut, and why?

## Post 2 — The Org Design Question That Determines Whether an AI Production Hire Succeeds

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Strategic insight | Audience: Studio leaders, Heads of Production, COOs, Founders | Schedule: Flex — Week 2 or Week 3**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Pattern observation → why it happens → what it costs → alternative → close**

---

The most consistent pattern I'm seeing across inbound briefs right now: studios deciding to hire for AI production before they've resolved what the function should own.

It happens in this order.

Leadership sees a gap. The gap is real. The decision to hire is sound.

But the org design question (what this function owns, who it reports to, what authority it carries) gets deferred. The plan is to work that out once the right person is in seat.

That plan is expensive.

An AI production hire into an undefined mandate spends year one lobbying for scope rather than delivering against it. The role becomes whatever the person can defend inside the business. That's not a talent failure. That's a structural one.

This isn't criticism of studios. AI production doesn't map neatly onto existing org structures. They're working it out as they go. The thoughtful ones are treating the hire as an org design decision, not just a headcount decision.

What that looks like: the brief gets built before the search starts. What will this function own on day 90? What does the budget allocation look like? Who has historically resisted this kind of function, and how is that being managed?

It costs a few weeks upfront. It avoids a failed placement and a year of internal attrition.

If you're working through this right now, I'd be glad to talk it through.
