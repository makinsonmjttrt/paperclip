## Post 1 — What AI-Native Candidates Look Like in Creative Production

**Pillar 2 | Talent Market Intelligence | Candidate Signals**

---

Something I notice every time I look at a strong candidate profile in AI creative production: the AI skills don't appear in a cluster from 2022.

They're threaded through everything. A GitHub contribution from 2021. A SIGGRAPH talk from 2023 where it's obvious the presenter built the system rather than used it. A portfolio with visual consistency across generated assets, which only happens when someone has fine-tuned models on specific training data.

The CV tells you when someone arrived.

AI-native candidates were in this space before it was commercially fashionable. Many came through NeurIPS Creativity Workshop, Creative AI London, or open-source contributions to Stable Diffusion and ComfyUI repos before the mainstream wave.

In conversation, the difference is clear. AI-native candidates talk about training data constraints, inference latency, ControlNet conditioning, the gap between what a model can do and what it will do consistently at production scale.

AI-curious candidates describe results. AI-native candidates describe the process that made those results reliable.

The screening question I return to: describe a production problem you solved with AI that you could not have solved any other way.

Genuine depth surfaces in the answer.

P.S. Most of the strongest AI creative candidates I know were building in this space in 2019 or earlier. The mainstream arrived where they already were.

## Post 2 — Why Great AI Candidates Are Not on the Market Long

**Pillar 2 | Talent Market Intelligence | Urgency for Hiring Managers**

---

The search conversations I'm having with studios right now all end the same way: "we need to move quickly on this."

They're right. They just don't know how right they are.

The pool of senior candidates who combine genuine production delivery experience with real AI fluency is small. Not "hard to find" small. "We know most of them individually" small.

These candidates are not sitting idle waiting for the right brief.

The ones displaced by the 2023-2024 VFX contractions have mostly landed. Those still in role are fielding multiple approaches simultaneously. Big tech, Meta in particular, has been actively pulling experienced production operators out of the studio ecosystem for AI training work.

The clients who move fastest are the ones who start the search conversation before the role is formally approved. They come in knowing what they need. They decide in weeks.

The clients who arrive at the job spec stage are often searching a pool that is already smaller than it was.

Acting early in a thin market is not just good practice. It is the strategy.

P.S. The most competitive searches I've run were not about finding the candidate. They were about getting there before someone else did.

## Post 3 — The Career Trajectory Question AI Leaders Get Asked in Every Interview Right Now

**Pillar 3 | Leadership at the AI/Creative Intersection | Candidate Perspective**

---

There is one question that comes up in almost every senior AI creative leadership interview right now, in some form.

It is not about technical depth. Not about team size. Not about titles held.

It is: prove that you were in this space before it was obvious.

The studios and platforms hiring seriously for AI creative leadership are not looking for someone who adopted the tools when the whole industry did. They want to see the pre-commercial track record. Conference talks from before 2023. Open-source contributions that predate the mainstream generative wave. A portfolio where AI integration was structural, not a finishing layer.

For candidates navigating this, how you tell the story of your trajectory matters.

The most compelling answers I hear come from people who don't feel the need to prove it. Their involvement is visible and dated. The GitHub activity is there. The talks are on record. The community knows them.

That visibility is not accidental. It comes from years of building in public in a space that mattered before it became commercially attractive.

If your track record genuinely precedes the wave, that history belongs at the front of your story.

P.S. The studios worth working for can read a timeline.

*(~205 words)*

## Post 4 — What the Best AI Creative Tech Leaders Have in Common

**Pillar 2 | Talent Market Intelligence | Short Take**

---

I keep coming back to the same observation when I look at the leaders doing this work at the highest level.

Thomas Graham built strategic authority over AI production at scale at Brahma, without a technical background. Rachel Rose, Director of R&D at ILM, took a PhD in Computer Science to the work behind StageCraft and BlockParty and a Technical Achievement Award. Nikola Todorovic co-founded Wonder Dynamics because he understood the production problem from the inside, long before Autodesk acquired the company. Luba Elliott founded the Creative AI London meetup in 2016, six years before the mainstream wave.

Different paths. Same characteristic.

They all arrived through the work, not through the opportunity. The commercial moment came to them. They were not chasing it.

In searches for AI creative tech leadership, this is the signal I return to. Not the tools listed. Not the titles held. When they arrived, why they were there, and what they built before anyone was paying attention.

**The best candidates in this space share one thing:** they were curious about the problem before they knew it would become a market.

P.S. That kind of track record doesn't appear on the job spec. You have to know where to look.
