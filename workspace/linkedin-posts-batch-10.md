## Post 1 — FMX Signal: What the Event Tells Us

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Audience: Both / market signal | Schedule: Week of 5 May**

---

The gap between what studios present at events like FMX and what they're doing in their hiring conversations has never been more pronounced.

On stage: confident AI integration narratives, pipeline transformation, production at scale.

In the briefing call: "we think we need an AI creative lead, but we're not sure if we need someone technical or someone who can manage delivery. Can you find us both?"

FMX is one of the best windows into where the industry's head is at.

Not because of the sessions. Because of **the corridor conversations**.

Studios are past the question of whether to invest in AI creative leadership. That's settled.

The question now is structural. What does the role look like. Where does it sit in the org. What mandate does it get. Whether the infrastructure exists to give someone what they need to actually deliver.

The gap is not between progressive studios and cautious ones.

It's between **the story a studio tells publicly** and the actual operational readiness underneath.

That gap is what makes this a genuinely interesting moment to be recruiting in.

Curious what others are taking away from FMX this year.

## Post 2 — The 90-Day Problem

**Pillar: Client Intelligence | Hiring Process | Audience: Client-facing | Schedule: Week of 12 May**

---

The hire was right. What came after the offer wasn't.

When senior AI creative hires underdeliver in the first 90 days, studios assume they got the wrong person. In my experience, that's rarely true.

The failure is almost always structural.

**Unclear mandate.** They were hired to lead AI, but nobody defined what that means against the existing org or who they're accountable to.

**No internal champion.** Once they're in the building, the hiring decision-maker moves on to the next problem.

**Infrastructure not ready.** They're expected to build production capability using tools that haven't been procured or budgeted yet.

**Leadership not bought in.** The commitment to AI production existed at the person who made the hire. Everyone above and around them is still watching and waiting.

None of this shows up in the interview process.

It shows up at the 90-day placement follow-up call.

Senior AI creative leadership roles require onboarding investment that matches the seniority of the hire. Clarity before day one. Not orientation in week three.

The quality of the hire matters. So does what the studio builds around it.

P.S. The last placement I made who genuinely hit the ground running had a written 90-day mandate and a line manager who had cleared their diary for the first fortnight.

## Post 3 — The Two-Role Brief

**Pillar: Client Intelligence | Brief Quality | Audience: Client-facing | Schedule: Week of 12–16 May**

---

The most common structural error in AI production briefs is also the one most often presented as a feature, not a flaw.

Studios bundle two fundamentally different roles into a single job description. **Technical architect** on one side. **Creative delivery lead** on the other.

The person who designs AI infrastructure, and the person who uses it to make things.

Most briefs ask for both.

The reason is understandable. Studios can't find either, so they ask for both and hope the market delivers something in the middle.

What it signals to the candidate pool: the studio doesn't yet know what it actually needs.

What it means for the search: **the brief is written for a person who doesn't exist.**

Candidates who are genuinely strong at technical architecture are rarely strong at creative delivery leadership. The crossover exists. It's rare, expensive, and usually already employed somewhere that values it.

A stronger brief separates the immediate need from the ideal future state. What does the role need to deliver in year one? Who does this person need to be today, versus who you'd develop them into over time?

Those questions change the brief entirely. And they're worth answering before the search starts.

If you're working on an AI creative production brief right now, I'm happy to look at it.

## Post 4 — Why They Actually Move

**Pillar: Talent Market Intelligence | Candidate Perspective | Audience: Candidate-facing | Schedule: Week of 19 May**

---

Compensation and title get the conversation started. They rarely determine the outcome.

Senior AI creative professionals evaluating a move are running a different calculation beneath the surface. What gets said in public and what drives the actual decision are two different lists.

In the conversations I have with candidates thinking through an offer or a change, the real questions surface after the salary discussion.

Is this role genuinely senior, or is it advisory in practice?

Will I have what I need to build with from day one, or will year one be spent lobbying for budget and infrastructure?

Is this studio building AI capability, or are they hiring someone to say they are?

These questions rarely come up in final-round interviews.

They come up in debrief calls, in conversations with former colleagues, in quiet due diligence that happens outside the formal process.

Studios that want to hire serious AI creative talent need to answer those questions before they're asked. In the brief, in the interview structure, in the offer itself.

**What the role actually is needs to match what the offer says it is.**

That's the part of the hiring process where most studios lose the people they most need.

If you're evaluating a move right now and want a candid view on what to ask in final conversations, I'm available.
