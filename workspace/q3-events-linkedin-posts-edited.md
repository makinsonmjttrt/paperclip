Develop:Brighton next month. Heading down for a couple of days.

Most people assume Develop is purely a games conference. For FPZ it's something different.

The talent pipeline feeding virtual production, LED volume work, and spatial computing increasingly runs through game development. Unreal generalists, technical artists, rendering engineers who started in games but are being pulled towards virtual production and XR workflows. The convergence has been building for several years, and this is one of the rooms where you can see it in real time.

What we're going in to understand is the quality of that crossover. Are senior game studio leads actively building profiles in production environments? Are studios on both sides creating pathways, or is talent still moving informally, on an ad hoc basis?

There are also questions worth asking about how AI tool adoption differs between games and production. Games studios have been running generative tooling in pipelines for longer than many VFX houses. There's learning there worth taking seriously.

Around 2,000 people. Small enough for real conversations.

If you're at Develop:Brighton and thinking about where your career takes you next, we should talk.

### Post 2 — Post-event roundup
**Pillar: Market Intelligence / Field Report | Audience: Both | Schedule: 18–19 July (day after event ends)**

---

Back from Develop:Brighton.

The headline: the crossover between real-time games and virtual production is further along at some UK studios than anyone is publicly acknowledging. At others, it has barely started.

What stood out was the variance. Some technical artists at mid-size UK studios have already moved fluidly between games and VP work. The tools are the same, the workflows are close enough, and the senior compensation in production is often better. Others are watching from the outside and not sure whether the bridge exists.

The question being asked at Develop this year was not really "is AI coming for games?" That's settled. The question was who owns the AI layer within a development team, and whether that person needs to have come from a technical art background or whether engineering experience is sufficient.

No consensus yet. That's informative in itself.

Studios that work through those structural questions early will be better placed to retain the people who might otherwise drift toward VP or XR roles where the role definitions are more settled.

Worth thinking through before the next hire.

Flying to Los Angeles next week for SIGGRAPH.

The conference draws every major VFX studio and production technology company. ILM, Weta, Framestore, DNEG, Sony Imageworks, MPC. Researchers, supervisors, pipeline architects. The people who are actually building what production will look like in three years.

For FPZ, SIGGRAPH is a market intelligence trip as much as anything else. The Production Sessions track is where senior practitioners present what they have actually built, not what they are planning to build. That's a reliable indicator of where senior hiring needs are forming.

The question we're going in with: are the AI specialist roles being created inside studios stabilising into recognisable job shapes? Early in any technology cycle, roles get invented before they're understood. A few cycles in, patterns emerge. We're three or four years into serious AI integration in high-end production. There should be enough evidence now to see what's working, which roles are delivering, and which ones studios are redesigning after the first attempt.

That's the conversation worth having on the floor.

If you're at SIGGRAPH and want to compare notes, we're there all week.

### Post 4 — Post-event roundup
**Pillar: Market Intelligence / Field Report | Audience: Both | Schedule: 25–26 July**

---

Back from Los Angeles. Five days at SIGGRAPH.

One observation keeps coming back.

There is a significant gap between the AI research capability at major studios (the ML engineers, the people publishing papers and building the tools) and the senior leaders who can deploy that capability at production scale. Not technically. Organisationally.

The research is advancing fast. The deployment is slower. Not because studios lack the technology, but because the connective role (the person who bridges research, pipeline, and creative direction) is still genuinely rare.

That person needs the trust of the research team and the creative leadership simultaneously. Production confidence tends to follow. That combination does not come from a CV. It comes from specific work history in specific environments.

Studios know this. Several conversations at SIGGRAPH circled back to the same structural tension: the ML capability exists, the deployment is not keeping pace, and the reason is almost always the senior role architecture around the tooling rather than the tools themselves.

That is a hiring and organisational design problem. It is also exactly what FPZ is built to solve.

We want to understand which companies are at that point, what briefs they are writing, and whether the brief as written will actually find the right person. That second question matters more than it might seem. The wrong brief wastes months.

### Post 6 — Post-event roundup
**Pillar: Market Intelligence / Field Report | Audience: Both | Schedule: 15–17 September**

---

Four days at IBC. Back in London.

The market for senior AI production talent in European broadcast and post is more advanced than the public conversation suggests.

Several companies at IBC have already completed first-generation AI leadership hires. Some went well. A number did not, and the pattern in the ones that struggled was consistent. The hire was strong technically or strong in production leadership, but rarely both. A year in, the creative and technology teams were still not communicating through that person in the way the studio needed.

That mismatch is exactly what the assessment work FPZ does is designed to prevent. The question is not just whether a candidate understands AI tooling. It is whether they have the production credibility for a senior creative team to take direction from them. Those are different questions and both matter.

What was clear at IBC this year is that studios are past the exploratory phase. They know what kind of person they need. They are less confident about how to find them and whether to trust the process to a firm that does not understand both sides of the brief.

That is the clearest signal we have seen that demand for what FPZ does is converting into real pipeline.
