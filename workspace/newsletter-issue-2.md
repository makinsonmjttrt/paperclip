## Introduction

The briefs I'm seeing for senior AI creative roles share a structural problem.

They're written for a candidate who would be exceptional in 2023. Technical depth in AI tooling, plus some creative production exposure, plus leadership experience. Three categories, each well-defined, each searchable.

The problem is that this candidate profile splits the market in a way that doesn't reflect how the work actually gets done. Studios hiring AI production leaders aren't primarily looking for someone who knows the tools. They need someone who knows when to use them — and when not to. That's a different competency, it doesn't appear in most job specs, and it's far harder to assess through standard recruitment processes.

This issue looks at what's going wrong in how companies brief for these roles, what the market actually looks like, and three pieces of external reporting that inform where hiring is heading next.

## What companies are getting wrong in the search for AI creative leaders

The most common mistake is briefing for credentials that prove AI capability, when the actual requirement is judgment about AI in a production context.

A candidate can hold a strong AI/ML background, have worked at a well-regarded technology company, and still be the wrong hire for a VFX studio, immersive venue operator, or creative software company. The reason is usually that their AI experience has never been tested against real production constraints. Unreal Engine under a delivery deadline. Houdini pipelines where an AI tool failure costs the studio hours, not minutes. Creative direction decisions made with AI output that has to satisfy a client who understands craft.

The candidate who has navigated that environment looks different from the one who hasn't. They talk differently about adoption. They're sceptical of tools they haven't tested in production. They can tell you which part of their workflow AI has genuinely changed, and which part it's made more complicated.

Most recruitment processes can't distinguish between the two profiles. Interviews focus on the tools adopted, not the decisions made. Briefs specify technical qualifications that sit on one side of the intersection, then add a line about creative production exposure. The shortlists that come back reflect the brief.

There's also a structural problem with where these candidates sit in the market. The most experienced AI-in-production leaders are not actively looking. They're employed at studios, software companies, and platforms where their skills are being applied in real conditions right now. They receive recruiter outreach regularly. Most of it doesn't demonstrate enough sector understanding to be worth a response. The approach needs to reference their specific work, show genuine understanding of their context, and give them a reason to engage with this role specifically.

The Tier 1 candidate pool for the most senior AI creative leadership roles numbers roughly 50 to 75 people globally. Internal talent acquisition teams can reach one side. Specialist recruiters cover the other. The brief that crosses both markets requires a different process.

**What this means in practice:** Build the brief around the capability you need in the first six months, not the title the candidate has held previously. Write the evaluation criteria around judgment and production track record, not tool familiarity. And expect the search to take longer than an equivalent senior tech hire — not because the candidates don't exist, but because reaching them requires a different kind of outreach.

## From the network

A pattern I'm seeing across multiple studio and platform clients this quarter: the first AI leadership hire has been in post for 12 to 18 months, and a second mandate is now in planning. The brief for that second hire is consistently different from the first. Less about technical capability, more about organisational translation — someone who can make the AI team's work legible to creative directors, heads of production, and client-side stakeholders who are still learning the language.

This is the second generation of creative AI hiring. The first wave built the capability. The second wave integrates it. The talent profile, and the search process, are both meaningfully different.
