# When AI Comes to the Grade: How One VFX Studio Rebuilt Its Leadership Layer

*A FourPointZero case study*

---

The call came at a familiar moment. A mid-sized UK VFX studio had committed to an AI transformation programme. The board had approved the budget. The CTO had chosen the tooling. What they hadn't accounted for was the human architecture required to make it work.

They called us six months in. Not because the technology had failed them, but because the right people weren't in place to lead it.

---

## The problem with hiring for a role that didn't exist last year

AI-integrated production pipelines have created a specific talent problem for VFX and virtual production studios: the roles they need to fill are genuinely new. A Head of AI Production isn't a VFX supervisor who has read about machine learning. They're not a data scientist who has toured a post-production facility. The brief requires both sides: deep production credibility that earns the respect of artists and supervisors, and enough hands-on AI fluency to make real technical decisions.

This studio had spent three months with a generalist search firm before coming to us. The candidates presented were technically competent but lacked production environment experience. Artists were unconvinced. The search stalled.

The deeper issue was that the brief itself needed work. The original job description was written for a technology role at a production company, not for someone leading creative transformation inside a high-pressure delivery environment. The title, reporting line, and success metrics all needed adjusting before any candidate worth their time would take the call seriously.

---

## What we actually did

We started by rebuilding the brief. Not because the studio had got it wrong, but because articulating a role that sits between disciplines requires someone who understands both.

We worked with the CTO and Studio Director to redefine the mandate: not AI implementation manager, but senior technical leader responsible for pipeline modernisation and creative team adoption. The distinction matters. The first role attracts engineers who want to build systems. The second attracts experienced production leaders who want to transform how work gets made.

With a sharper brief, we ran a targeted search across three talent pools: senior VFX supervisors with documented AI project involvement, pipeline technical directors who had transitioned into leadership roles, and AI product leaders from creative SaaS companies with production studio backgrounds.

We approached 34 people over six weeks. Eleven were relevant enough to take to initial assessment. The studio met four. They hired one.

---

## The hire

The appointed candidate was a former VFX Supervisor turned Technical Production Lead with hands-on experience using machine learning tools in a major feature film production pipeline. Not a headline-maker. Not the most polished CV. The person with the most relevant actual experience, who understood both the creative team's concerns and the board's expectations, and who could credibly hold both conversations at once.

They started within two months of the brief being finalised.

---

## Why this search worked when the previous one hadn't

Three things made the difference.

First, the brief was honest about the role's difficulty. Candidates respected that. They came to initial conversations better prepared and more genuinely interested.

Second, the search reached people who were not on the market. Most of the relevant candidates we approached had not applied to anything for years. They took the call because they knew us and trusted we wouldn't waste their time.

Third, assessment went beyond the CV. We asked specifically about how candidates had navigated artist resistance to new tools, how they had communicated technical trade-offs to non-technical leadership, and what they had done when a pipeline decision proved wrong. Those conversations told us more than credentials alone.

---

## The wider pattern

This studio's experience is not unusual. AI transformation in creative production is creating a consistent talent gap at the leadership level: studios that have clarity on their technology roadmap but not on the human architecture that has to carry it.

The roles being created are not straightforward to fill. They require search firms that understand the production environment well enough to assess candidates credibly, and that have enough existing relationships to reach the relevant people before they become available through conventional channels.
