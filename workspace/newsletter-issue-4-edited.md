## Introduction

Issue 3 ended with a shift: senior AI creative candidates are no longer just asking about the technology. They are asking about organisational readiness. Has the board genuinely committed? Is there a creative culture that will collaborate, or one that will resist?

That shift is a response to something concrete. The studios that were running AI experiments in 2024 are now making permanent hires. The role has changed from a single-person advocate with a broad mandate to a functional leader building capacity the rest of the organisation depends on. The briefs, in many cases, have not caught up.

Three signals that a studio has crossed that line.

---

## Three signals a studio has moved from experimental to infrastructure hiring

### 1. Role seniority has increased

What was a Head of AI Innovation title 18 months ago has moved to VP, Director of AI Production, or CTO in some instances. The scope of the role and the level of the hire reflect the shift from "explore this for us" to "own this for the long term."

The candidate right for the experimental phase and the candidate right for the infrastructure phase are not always the same person. The early pioneers are often excellent at generating momentum. The infrastructure role needs someone who can convert that momentum into operational capability. Confusing the two profiles is one of the more common reasons a placement works for a year and then becomes a different problem.

### 2. The remit now includes building a team

Where the brief used to say "own the AI workflow," it now says "build and lead the AI production function." That distinction changes the evaluation criteria and changes what the organisation needs to have in place before the hire arrives.

A brief that includes team building responsibility but has not answered questions about budget, headcount, and internal support will create friction downstream. The candidates who have done this before know to ask those questions early. The brief that cannot answer them signals something about organisational readiness before the first interview happens.

### 3. Internal alignment is happening before the search starts

Studios making this hire for the second time bring briefs to market with creative leadership, production leadership, and the board already aligned on what success looks like. The brief reflects decisions already made, not a document that emerges from the process itself.

When that alignment is not in place, it shows up in the search. Shortlists stall. Briefs get revised mid-process. Candidates who were engaged in week two lose confidence by week six. The organisations moving fastest are treating the internal conversation as part of the hiring process, not a precursor to it.

## From the network

Senior AI creative candidates are arriving at early conversations with a clearer picture of what they will and will not walk into. They have seen the infrastructure hire that landed in an organisation that was not ready. They are asking harder questions earlier.

The organisations attracting the strongest candidates can answer those questions with specificity: who sponsors this internally, what the first 90 days actually looks like, and what has already been decided versus what the new hire will decide. Organisational readiness is now a filter the candidate applies to you, not just something you apply to them.

---

## Practical takeaway

Before briefing a search for an AI production leadership role, run three internal conversations: with the creative director who will work alongside the hire, with the production head who will depend on them, and with whoever controls the budget for team building in year one. If those conversations have not happened, the brief that goes to market will reflect the board's version of the role, not the studio's version. The candidates who know this work will notice the difference.
