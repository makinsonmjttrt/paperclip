## Post 1 — Cost of Slow Hiring Decision

**Pillar 2 | Talent Market Intelligence | Personal Observation**
**Hook type:** Reframe
**Format:** Observation post

---

The conversation about hiring cost usually stops at the fee.

The actual cost runs through the business while the role sits open.

I've watched studios run searches that stretched across six, eight, sometimes ten months. The fee at the end is visible. What's harder to see is the damage running in the background.

A senior AI production leadership role sitting vacant doesn't just create a gap in the org chart. It puts pressure on everyone around it. Existing leaders absorb work they shouldn't be carrying. Decisions that need senior authority slow down or get made without the full brief. Projects that require that leadership haven't waited for the person to arrive.

The businesses that account for this honestly usually reach the same conclusion: a three-month search done properly costs far less than a six-month search run without discipline.

Speed at the expense of rigour is not the answer. A process designed around what the search actually needs to close, rather than what feels manageable to run internally, gets there faster with less disruption along the way.

The companies I see navigate this well treat the open role as a live cost from week one, not a budget line at the end.

What would change about how your organisation approaches this if the vacancy cost was tracked from day one?

P.S. The most expensive searches I've been part of weren't expensive because of the fee. They were expensive because of what was happening inside the business while they were running.

## Post 2 — AI Leadership Skill Gap in Creative Companies

**Pillar 3 | Leadership/Org | Data-Backed Argument (Directional)**
**Hook type:** Diagnosis reframe
**Format:** Argument post

---

Most creative companies know they have a gap in AI leadership capacity.

The harder question is where that gap actually comes from.

The most common response is to elevate the most technically capable person on the existing team. Sometimes that works. More often, the individual who understands the technology best is not the same person who can translate it into a production workflow that actually ships.

The second response is to hire from tech companies or AI-native businesses. The technical depth arrives. The studio fluency often doesn't. These candidates understand infrastructure and scale. They frequently underestimate what production decision-making inside a creative environment requires.

The gap is growing. Not because the talent doesn't exist, but because the search is too often framed around one half of the requirement at the expense of the other.

Studios closing this gap quickly have been precise about what they're actually hiring for: demonstrated AI integration experience combined with a delivery track record in creative production contexts. Not one or the other.

The companies still searching are frequently running a brief that treats the technical and the creative as separate criteria, when they need to exist in the same candidate.

How are you framing the brief for senior AI leadership in your organisation?

P.S. The most common feedback I hear from senior AI candidates who've joined creative companies: they underestimated how different production decision-making is from anything they'd experienced before.

## Post 3 — Why Generalist Recruiters Fail at AI Roles

**Pillar 2 | Talent Market Intelligence | Short Take**
**Hook type:** Specificity over common explanation
**Format:** Short opinion post

---

Why generalist recruiters fail on senior AI creative production searches is more specific than the common explanation.

The assumption is a knowledge gap. They don't understand enough about AI to evaluate the candidates.

That's part of it.

The less visible problem is that they don't know enough about creative production either.

To evaluate a shortlist for a role like this, you need to understand what both sides look like in practice. What AI integration experience means in a production context versus a product or data context. What a Head of Production brief at a studio actually involves. Where the complexity lives in delivering creative work at scale.

Without that dual fluency, a recruiter can find candidates and filter CVs. They cannot tell you which three people from a 15-person longlist have actually done what the brief requires.

That's the gap.

The cost usually appears three stages into an interview process, when the studio and the candidate both realise the match was approximate from the start.

P.S. The first question I ask on any retained brief is what happened the last time it was run. The answer usually explains why it didn't close.

## Post 4 — CreativAI Practice Differentiator

**Pillar 3 | Leadership/Org | Practice Differentiator**
**Hook type:** Practical audit question
**Format:** Numbered list with bold headers

---

Studio directors and HR leads ask me the same question more often now: how do you tell the difference between a recruiter who genuinely knows AI creative production and one who's moved into the space because it's active?

The markers are readable once you know what to ask for.

**1. They can interrogate the brief, not just take it.** A specialist who knows this territory should push back on an incomplete brief. The most common reason AI creative production searches fail is that the role was defined wrong from the start.

**2. They can name the market before you describe the role.** In a talent pool this small, genuine network depth is visible. A recruiter who knows this space is already mapping candidates while you're still building the brief.

**3. They can evaluate both sides of the hire.** AI capability and production fluency are both required. Most generalists can assess one or the other. The specialist assessment requires both simultaneously.

None of this is difficult to test.

In the first conversation with any recruiter for a role in this space, ask them to describe the current candidate market as they understand it. How specific is the answer? How current? How honest about scarcity?

That response tells you more than any credentials claim.

P.S. The practices doing this well are the ones who've spent years inside creative production specifically, not arrived from adjacent search markets when AI became active.
