## Post 3 — The Structural Supply Shortage

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Client-facing | Audience: Studio leaders, Heads of Production, Founders | Schedule: Thu 7 May**
**Hook type: Contrarian/Myth-Bust | Structure: Problem → Why it's structural → Practical implication → Authority close**

---

The constraint in the AI creative leadership market isn't demand. The constraint is that genuine experience at this level took years to build.

The people with three to five years of real AI production delivery at commercial scale were the ones who started early. 2020, 2021. When the tooling was rough, the projects were experimental, and most studios hadn't decided whether AI was worth serious investment.

That cohort is small. Not because studios weren't interested in finding them earlier. Because experience like this compounds slowly. You can't accelerate it with demand.

What that means practically: the candidate you're describing in your brief almost certainly already has a role they find interesting. They're not checking job boards. They're working on projects that stretch them. The window to have a conversation opens rarely.

Getting in front of them requires a network built in those early years. Not a recruiter running keywords across a database. Not a job posting on a platform they haven't visited this month.

The studios moving quickly in AI production hiring have one thing in common. They have relationships with people who were already in this space before it became commercially obvious.

If you weren't in those conversations when that experience was being built, you're working from a list.

We weren't working from a list.

## Post 4 — Before You Accept an AI Creative Role

**Pillar: Talent Intelligence | Candidate Advice | Audience: Senior AI creative production professionals | Schedule: Fri 8 May**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Pattern-from-experience → Questions woven through paragraphs → Direct CTA**

---

Strong candidates take the wrong roles. Not because they don't have the skills. Because the structural conditions weren't right before they joined.

This comes up in post-placement conversations more than almost anything else. The role looked right on paper. The studio was credible. The brief was compelling. But something in the underlying setup hadn't been thought through, and year one didn't deliver the way anyone expected.

There are questions that surface the structural risk before you accept.

What does this function own, specifically? Not what will you be involved in. What is yours to deliver? Vague answers here aren't a sign of flexibility. They're a sign the mandate hasn't been designed.

Does the tooling and infrastructure budget exist now, or will year one be spent lobbying for it? The answer tells you whether this role is set up to deliver or set up to prove a case.

Who is your internal champion, and how much political capital do they actually have? The person who hired you matters less than the person who can protect the function when priorities shift.

What does a successful year one look like for both of you? If the answer is unclear, the measure of success will be defined retrospectively. It rarely favours the new hire.

And the question that tells you most: why does this role not exist yet? Or, if someone was in it before, what happened to them?

If you're evaluating something right now and want a candid read on what to ask before you decide, send me a message.
