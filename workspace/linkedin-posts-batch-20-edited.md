## Post 1 — The 3 Questions to Ask Any AI Candidate (That Most Studios Aren't Asking Yet)

**Pillar: Market Intelligence / Client Advice | Audience: Client-facing (Heads of Production, Studio Directors) | Schedule: Mon 9 June**
**Hook type: Numbered List with Bold Headers | Structure: Hook → Context → 3 Bold Items → CTA → P.S.**

---

Most studios interviewing AI creative candidates ask the same question.

"What tools have you used?"

That question tells you almost nothing.

It filters for familiarity, not for the thing that actually separates a good AI hire from a costly one: judgement about when the tools fail you.

The three questions I'd ask instead:

**Tell me about a project where you had to abandon an AI-generated output at a late stage. What did you do?**
This surfaces whether someone can cut their losses under pressure, or whether they over-invest in outputs that won't make the final cut.

**What's the most common mistake you see other people make when integrating AI into a creative workflow?**
Candidates who have genuinely operated at scale know the failure patterns. Candidates who haven't give you a theoretical answer you've already read in a think piece.

**If you joined this team and wanted AI integration to move meaningfully forward in year one, what would we need to provide?**
This tells you whether they understand the structural side of the role. Tools are the easy part. Organisational buy-in, workflow changes, and leadership alignment are where it actually stalls.

Most studios aren't asking question three.

That's where the wrong hire becomes obvious in retrospect.

Which of these would change how you run your next AI interview?

P.S. If you're briefing for a senior AI role, I'm happy to sense-check the process before you go to market.

## Post 2 — When a Studio Says "We Want an AI Generalist"

**Pillar: Market Intelligence / Talent Intelligence | Audience: Client-facing (Studio Directors, Founders) | Schedule: Tue 10 June**
**Hook type: Contrarian/Myth-Bust | Structure: Contrarian Rant**

---

"We want an AI generalist."

I hear this a lot.

And I understand why studios say it.

The tools are changing fast. Nobody wants to hire too narrowly and find themselves locked out of the next category of capability.

But in practice, the brief almost never describes a generalist.

It describes a specific problem.

When I ask studios what's not working right now, the answer is usually one of three things: the pipeline doesn't have a review stage that understands AI-generated content at the right quality bar; the team is using tools without a shared framework for when to use them; or there's a creative lead who needs to get comfortable with what the technology can and can't do.

Each of those is a different hire.

A generalist won't solve any of them as well as someone who has lived inside that specific problem before.

The studios that fill AI roles fastest describe the function they need the hire to own, not the range of tools they want someone to know.

The brief is almost always more specific than "AI generalist" once you dig in for fifteen minutes.

What's the actual gap your next AI hire needs to close?

P.S. I'm happy to work through a brief before you go to market. It usually saves time at the shortlist stage.

## Post 3 — The Salary Premium for AI Creative Roles: What the Market Actually Pays

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Audience: Client-facing (Founders, Heads of People, Studio Directors) | Schedule: Wed 11 June**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Observation-Pattern-Implication-CTA**

> **Publishing note:** All salary observations are directional only. No specific figures cited. This reflects genuine market observations from live briefs.

---

The salary premium for AI creative roles is real.

But studios are anchoring to the wrong market.

The comparison most people reach for is traditional creative production salaries. A Senior VFX Artist, a Production Supervisor, a Head of Post.

That's the wrong reference point.

The roles commanding the premium are not just traditional roles with AI skills added.

They are roles where the person is expected to build the function, not just operate within it. Where they need to understand workflow architecture, make tooling decisions, and advocate for investment across a leadership team that may have mixed views on AI in production.

That combination of creative depth and structural ownership is what the market is paying for.

I can't quote you a figure without knowing the role, the company structure, and what the person is actually being asked to own.

What I can tell you is that studios calibrating to "similar traditional role plus a modest premium" are regularly losing candidates to studios that have thought through the value of what the AI function actually delivers.

The anchor point matters as much as the number.

If you're benchmarking a salary for an AI creative role, the framing of the role will tell you more than the title.

What's your current reference point when building compensation for AI-focused hires?

P.S. I'm having this conversation regularly at the moment. Happy to help calibrate before you go to market.

## Post 4 — Why the Best AI Film and TV Hires Come from Unexpected Backgrounds

**Pillar: Talent Intelligence / Market Intelligence | Audience: Both (client and candidate) | Schedule: Thu 12 June**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Contrarian opening → Observation → Pattern → Implication → CTA**

---

The best AI hire I've seen a film and TV studio make in the last year didn't come from film and TV.

They came from live events.

Before that: a children's educational technology company.

Neither of those backgrounds appeared in the brief.

But when I spoke to them, the pattern made sense immediately.

Live events production trains you to make consequential creative decisions under time pressure with no ability to go back and fix it in post. That instinct (where does quality actually matter, where can you accept imperfect) is exactly what AI-integrated production needs at a senior level.

Educational technology trains you to think about AI output from the perspective of a non-specialist audience. What makes the output usable, versus impressive? Where does the technology need a human wrapper to actually land?

Studios writing AI briefs tend to specify depth in a particular production discipline. That makes sense.

But depth in a parallel discipline with genuine transfer value often produces a faster, more durable hire.

The studios that expand the aperture of their brief thoughtfully are finding candidates that studios with narrower specs are missing.

This is not an argument for lowering the bar.

It's an argument for defining the bar around the actual problem, not the most familiar route to it.

Where has an unexpected background surprised you with a strong hire?
