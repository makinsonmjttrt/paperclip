# When the Brief Was Wrong: A VFX Studio's AI Hiring Lesson

*A FourPointZero case study*

---

## The situation

A mid-sized UK VFX studio committed to an AI-integrated production programme. The board approved the budget. The CTO selected the tooling. The timeline was set.

Six months in, the technology was in place. Production hadn't changed. The reason was not technical.

The studio had no one capable of leading the adoption from inside the delivery environment. The pipeline work required someone who could sit between the AI systems and the artist teams, make credible technical decisions under production pressure, and bring senior creative staff along without losing them. That person didn't exist on the payroll, and their initial attempts to hire one had stalled.

They came to us at that point.

---

## The hire challenge: what they asked for versus what they needed

The original job description was written for a technology role at a production studio. The title emphasised AI implementation. The reporting line ran to the CTO. The success metrics were technical: tooling deployed, pipeline nodes integrated, test cycles completed.

The candidates it attracted were technically competent. Artists were unconvinced. Directors wouldn't engage with them. The search stalled after three months with a generalist firm.

The actual role required something different: a senior production leader who had operated inside a high-pressure VFX environment, carried the credibility of someone who had made real deliveries under those conditions, and could evaluate AI tooling with enough genuine fluency to make sound decisions about where it belonged in the pipeline and where it didn't. That is not the same as an AI implementation manager. The brief had described the wrong job.

---

## The placement: what we found, and why it worked

We started by rebuilding the brief. Not because the studio had got it wrong in intent, but because articulating a role that sits between disciplines requires someone who understands both.

Working with the CTO and Studio Director, we reframed the mandate: senior technical leader responsible for pipeline modernisation and creative team adoption. The distinction changes who applies. The first description attracts engineers who want to build systems. The second attracts experienced production leaders who want to change how work gets made.

With a sharper brief, we ran a targeted search across three intersecting pools: senior VFX supervisors with documented AI project involvement, pipeline technical directors who had moved into leadership, and AI product leaders from creative SaaS companies with production studio backgrounds.

We approached 34 people over six weeks. Eleven were relevant enough for initial assessment. The studio met four. They hired one.

The appointed candidate was a former VFX supervisor turned Technical Production Lead. Hands-on experience with machine learning tooling in a major feature film pipeline. Not a headline name. Not the most polished CV in the group. They were the person with the most relevant actual experience: someone who understood what artists needed to hear and what board sponsors needed to see, and who could hold both conversations without losing either audience.

They started within two months of the brief being finalised.

---

## The outcome: what changed

Following the hire, the studio's AI adoption moved from the infrastructure stage to the operational stage. Artist resistance dropped once someone with production credibility was leading the programme rather than someone arriving from outside it. The tooling that had been deployed but unused began to be incorporated into working pipelines. Production timelines on AI-assisted compositing work shortened. The team had a leader who could make technical calls on tooling trade-offs and communicate them in terms the creative team understood.

None of this would have happened with the original brief. The role as first written would have produced a different hire, and that hire would have encountered the same resistance.

---

## What this means for the market

This studio's experience is not unusual. Across VFX, virtual production, and creative technology, studios that have built or bought AI capability are running into the same problem at the leadership layer: the human architecture has not kept up with the technology investment.

The roles that need filling are genuinely new. They don't map cleanly onto existing job categories. They require search firms that understand the production environment well enough to assess whether a candidate can actually operate in it, and that have existing relationships to reach relevant people before they become available through conventional channels.
