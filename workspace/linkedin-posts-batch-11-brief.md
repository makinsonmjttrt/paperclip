## Post 2: AI-native studio structures

**Publish:** Wed 15 Apr

**Target audience:** Client-facing — Studio Directors, Founders, Heads of Production, COOs at creative production studios and agencies

**Core message / insight:** Creative studios aren't just adding AI tools to existing team structures — they're discovering those structures don't fit. Studios that hired AI talent into traditional production roles have found friction: the mandate wasn't there, the workflow ownership was unclear, and the capability was underused. The studios moving fastest have separated the AI production function rather than bolting it onto existing roles. New titles and new reporting lines are emerging.

**Suggested hook (first line):**
> The creative studio org chart is changing. Not because companies planned it that way — because AI didn't fit where they tried to put it.

*Alternative hook options:*
- "The problem with adding AI capability to an existing production team: the team already has a workflow."
- "Studios that hired for AI production in 2023 are restructuring in 2026. The role worked — the reporting line didn't."

**Key points / supporting evidence:**
- Studios initially hired "AI-capable" candidates into existing EP, Producer, or Creative Director roles — this worked for a while, then created structural tension
- The AI function needs ownership, mandate, and pipeline access — not just a capability someone brings to a traditional role
- Early-mover studios are separating: AI Creative Director, ML Pipeline Producer, AI Production Lead roles with their own team and P&L accountability
- This changes what hiring looks like — it's not "find someone who knows the tools," it's "build the function around the person"
- The conversation we're having with studios most often now is not "who should we hire" but "what should the role look like before we hire" — that's a structural shift
- Do not cite statistics. This is observational intelligence from conversations, framed as such.

**CTA:** Soft — position Martyn as someone worth talking to if this is the decision you're facing. Something like: "If you're thinking about how to structure this, I'm having this conversation with studios most weeks. Happy to share what's coming up." One sentence. Not salesy.

**Tone notes:** Insider observation, not prescription. This is someone who has visibility across multiple studios and is surfacing a pattern, not telling anyone what to do. The audience should feel like they're getting a window into conversations happening elsewhere in the industry. Keep the language concrete — "org chart," "reporting line," "P&L" — not abstract.

**Word count target:** 180–250 words.

## Post 3: What to put on your CV if you use AI tools

**Publish:** Flexible — any slot in the 14–17 Apr window that's available, or defer to the following week

**Target audience:** Candidate-facing — senior creative production professionals (EPs, Creative Directors, Heads of Production, Technical Producers) who use AI tools in their work

**Core message / insight:** Having AI tools on your CV is becoming table stakes. What differentiates strong candidates is not which tools they know — it's how they frame the work those tools enabled. Studios hiring AI production leadership are looking for workflow thinking and problem-solving, not a tool list.

**Suggested hook (first line):**
> If you use AI in your production work but can't describe it beyond "familiar with MidJourney," your CV is underselling you.

*Alternative hook options:*
- "The candidates landing senior AI production roles aren't necessarily the ones with the most tools on their CV."
- "AI tools on your CV are a starting point. Studios want to know what you built with them."

**Key points / supporting evidence:**
- Name the workflow, not just the tool. "Used ComfyUI to build a still-to-video pipeline for X project" is more compelling than "familiar with ComfyUI." The project context and the output are what signal capability.
- Show what problem it solved. The tool is the method; the problem is the value. Studios are hiring production leaders who identify problems and route AI to solve them — not operators.
- If you've embedded AI into a team's workflow, name that explicitly. Getting a team to adopt a new AI-assisted pipeline is a leadership achievement. It belongs in your experience section.
- There's a difference between "AI tools I use" and "AI workflows I've designed." The second category is what separates candidates right now. If you have both, make the distinction clear.
- Do not list tools in a skills block without context. A skills list is not evidence of ability.

**CTA:** Direct invite — "If you're a senior AI creative production professional thinking about your next move, DM me. Happy to give feedback on how you're presenting this."

**Tone notes:** Peer-to-peer advice, not lecturing. The best version of this reads like someone sharing something useful they've observed — not a careers advisor. First person is fine. Practical and specific is better than general. Avoid the word "leverage."

**Word count target:** 200–260 words.

## Post 4: Engagement / question post

**Publish:** Flexible — schedule for when engagement is needed. Good as a standalone or following a post that performed well.

**Target audience:** Both — broad creative tech community: studio leaders, creative directors, production talent, agency heads

**Core message / insight:** Surface what the real barrier to AI hiring in creative tech is — from the audience's perspective. This post is designed to drive comments and reveal market sentiment. It positions Martyn as someone embedded in the conversation, not broadcasting from above.

**Suggested hook (first line):**
> The biggest challenge hiring AI talent in creative tech isn't skills — it's...

**Format note:** This is an open-hook format. Martyn completes the sentence in the body of the post with his own perspective, then invites the audience to offer theirs. The Content Specialist should write two or three candidate completions for Martyn to choose from:

*Suggested completions (Content Specialist: include all three options, clearly labelled, so Martyn can choose):*

**Option A:** "...knowing what the role actually is before you start hiring for it."
> Most studios are describing what they need in AI production leadership six months after they needed it. The role gets written around the gap, not around the function. By then you're hiring reactively.

**Option B:** "...getting the organisation to move at the speed the market requires."
> Senior AI production talent doesn't wait. The candidate pool is small, the competition is real, and the window between shortlist and offer is shorter than most studios expect. Internal approval cycles were built for a different kind of hire.

**Option C:** "...separating genuine production experience from AI-native job-hopping."
> The market has candidates who can talk fluently about AI in creative production. Fewer of them have actually delivered a complex project using it. Knowing the difference requires context the job description doesn't give you.

**CTA (for all options):** Invite genuine input — "What's coming up for you? Curious what others are seeing." One sentence. No hashtags, no emojis, no "drop a comment below."

**Tone notes:** Genuine curiosity, not rhetorical. The question should feel like it's coming from someone who has a view but wants to hear whether others are seeing the same thing. Not a leadership quote. Not a thought experiment. A real question from someone in the middle of these conversations.

**Word count target (body only):** 80–130 words. Short post, strong hook, open close.
