## Post 1 — The Colourist Role Is Not Disappearing. It's Becoming Harder to Fill.

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Audience: Studio leaders, Heads of Post-Production | Schedule: Tue 12 May (or flex to week of 19 May)**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Observation-Pattern-Implication-CTA**

---

The briefing I keep getting about colourists is that AI will reduce the need for them.

That's not what I'm seeing in the briefs.

What I'm seeing is studios struggling to find colourists who understand how AI-assisted grading tools fit into a live production workflow.

Not replace. Fit in.

The tools that handle rough grade passes and LUT generation are genuinely capable now. They've taken work off the table. But they've also changed what a senior colourist is responsible for supervising.

The creative ceiling has lifted. The technical vocabulary required to work at that level has expanded.

The colourists most in demand right now understand where automated processes end and where creative judgement begins. And they can explain that boundary to a director under pressure.

That's a narrower profile than "experienced colourist."

The industry spent years building a junior pipeline that advanced people toward senior colour work. That pipeline is thinning because AI has absorbed the foundational tasks that used to develop colourists in the first place.

Which means the senior population is not being replaced. It's being undercut at the base.

Studios hiring for colour leadership in the next two years should be thinking about this now.

Where is your pipeline for that hire in 2027?

P.S. If you're a senior colourist working out how to position your AI fluency, that's a conversation I'm having regularly at the moment.

## Post 2 — Where AI Has Actually Cut Time in Post-Production (and Where It Hasn't)

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Audience: Both (studio leaders, VFX leads, senior candidates) | Schedule: Wed 13 May (or flex to week of 19 May)**
**Hook type: Contrarian/Myth-Bust | Structure: Contrarian Rant with Do/Don't framing**

---

The claim is that AI has compressed the production pipeline.

In some stages, that's accurate. In others, it has made the work harder.

I talk to VFX leads and heads of production regularly. Here is what they are actually saying.

Where AI has genuinely reduced time:

**Concept iteration and pre-vis.** Generative tools have cut rough exploration from days to hours. Studios that have built this into their workflow are generating more options earlier, with fewer people.

**Roto and cleanup.** The tooling has genuinely changed what used to be the most labour-intensive part of post. Not eliminated, but substantially faster in the right hands.

Where it hasn't:

**Supervision at every stage.** The time saved in execution has largely been absorbed by increased review and quality control. AI-generated elements need more checking, not less.

**Compositing at the final grade level.** The last 10% of a shot, where the human eye is the instrument, has not shortened. If anything, studios are spending more time here because the earlier stages move faster.

The net effect is not a shorter pipeline. It's a pipeline where the time is distributed differently.

The senior roles that carry judgement across all of it are in higher demand, not lower.

P.S. If your pipeline is shifting and the talent model hasn't followed, that's the conversation worth having first.

## Post 3 — The Hardest AI Creative Hire in VFX Right Now Is Not What Most Studios Name

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Audience: Studio leaders, VFX producers, Heads of Production | Schedule: Thu 14 May (conflict with batch-15 Post 1 — see scheduling note above)**
**Hook type: Bold Personal Declaration | Structure: Identify-the-problem → Why it's specific → What actually solves it → Authority close**

---

The hardest AI creative hire in VFX right now is not a generative artist or a pipeline engineer.

It's the VFX supervisor.

Here's why that's a specific problem.

The brief studios have in mind: a director-level supervisor who can run a complex live-action shoot, manage a large team under delivery pressure, and credibly evaluate AI-generated content in the edit suite.

Each of those is a high bar on its own.

Together, they describe someone who spent years building technical and creative depth in traditional VFX before the tools changed, then engaged seriously enough with AI to develop real judgement about its output.

That takes a long time to build. There is no shortcut.

The candidates who have it are not looking for roles. They're delivering on complex projects for studios that already know them.

Finding them requires knowing where the serious AI integration work has actually happened. Which companies gave supervisors time and budget to experiment. Where the projects ran that genuinely pushed the tooling to its limits.

This is the part of specialist recruitment that doesn't scale with a platform or a job posting. It's accumulated knowledge about who has been doing the interesting work.

If you're briefing for a VFX supervisor with genuine AI competence, the shortlist problem is real. Broader outreach won't solve it.

P.S. The more specific your brief is on what AI competence means in this role, the more useful I can be.

## Post 4 — A Producer's Guide to Asking Better AI Questions in Interviews

**Pillar: Market Intelligence | Client Advice | Audience: Studio leaders, Producers, Heads of Production | Schedule: Fri 15 May (or flex to week of 19 May)**
**Hook type: Contrarian/Myth-Bust | Structure: Numbered List with Bold Headers**

---

Most studios interviewing for AI creative roles are asking the wrong questions.

Not wrong intent. Wrong framing.

"What AI tools have you used?" tests for familiarity. It does not test for the thing that matters at a senior level: judgement about when and how to use them.

The questions that produce useful signal:

**Ask about constraints, not capabilities.** "Tell me about a project where an AI tool didn't do what you needed it to. What did you do?" This surfaces workflow ownership and problem-solving under pressure.

**Ask about decisions made under time pressure.** "When have you had to decide whether to use an AI-generated output or go manual? What drove that decision?" This tests practical judgement.

**Ask about failure modes.** "What's the most common mistake you see teams make when integrating AI into a production pipeline?" Candidates who have worked at scale know the failure patterns. Those who haven't give theoretical answers.

**Ask what they'd need from you.** "If you joined and wanted to move this pipeline forward in year one, what would the business need to provide?" This tests whether they understand the structural side of the role, not just the technical one.

The candidates worth hiring answer the constraint questions well.

Which of these would change how you run your next senior AI interview?

P.S. If you're building a brief for this kind of hire and want a sense-check on the interview process, I'm glad to look at it.
