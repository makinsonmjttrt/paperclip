## What specialisation actually means

There is a common misconception about what it means to specialise in a sector. Most large agencies that describe themselves as specialist have people who follow industry news, attend the occasional conference, and maintain a database tagged with sector keywords. They know the names. They can speak the language reasonably well in a first meeting.

That is not specialisation. That is awareness.

Specialisation in recruitment means knowing the people -- not knowing of them. It means having spent enough time in a sector that you understand the informal reputation economy: who delivers under pressure, who has a name in the right rooms, who has moved and why. It means the talent you need to reach picks up your call because they know who you are and trust that you only call with something worth hearing.

In AI creative production, this distinction is sharpened by the nature of the candidate pool. The people who can actually run an AI-integrated creative production -- who have hands-on experience with the workflows and the track record to manage a team through them -- are not numerous. They are employed. They are not actively looking. They do not respond to job board postings or automated InMail sequences. They move when a conversation reaches them through a channel they trust.

Knowing the sector is not sufficient to reach them. Knowing them is.

The relationships built over that period are not database entries. This difference in starting position is what drives time-to-hire. The roles that take generalist agencies three to four months to fill are often filled faster through a specialist search not because more hours are put in, but because fewer calls are wasted on candidates who were never the right profile in the first place. Less noise in the process means a shorter path from brief to shortlist.

An AI recruiter pivoting to cover creative production can acquire sector knowledge reasonably quickly. The relationships that make that knowledge actionable took six years to build. There is no shortcut to that.

## The assessment problem

There is a second reason generalist search consistently underperforms on AI creative roles, and it is less often discussed.

These roles require assessment on two distinct axes. The first is production competence: can this person actually run creative work at scale, manage a team, deliver under deadline pressure, and operate credibly with a creative director or an executive producer? The second is AI workflow fluency: do they have a genuine working understanding of how AI tools enter a production pipeline, what they change, what they don't, and where judgment is required rather than automation?

Most recruiters can assess one or the other. AI and technology recruiters can probe the second axis reasonably well. Entertainment and media recruiters can assess the first. Very few can assess both -- and the ones who can't will consistently shortlist candidates who are strong on one dimension and weak on the other. That is how a studio ends up with eight candidates who understand AI tools and none who understand production.

The studios that have worked with FourPointZero on AI creative roles describe the shortlist quality in similar terms. The candidates understand the production environment. They also have a formed point of view on AI in that environment -- not enthusiasm for the tools, but actual judgment about where they belong in a workflow and where they create problems. That is a harder thing to find than it looks on paper, and it takes time in the sector to recognise it on a CV or surface it in a conversation.
