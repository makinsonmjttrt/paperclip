## Introduction

Issue 5 looked at the supply side: the pool of candidates with genuine AI production delivery experience is smaller than demand, and the best ones are not available.

This issue narrows the lens. One part of the creative workflow is moving faster than most studios expected — and the hiring briefs for senior post-production roles have not caught up.

---

## What is actually changing

Post-production has always evolved slowly between hardware cycles. The current shift is different in character, not just speed.

AI tooling is now embedded in the working pipeline at leading studios — not in a dedicated AI team, not in a sandbox, but in the daily workflow of colourists, compositors, and finishing artists. Noise reduction, rotoscoping, object removal, shot matching, QC passes: tasks that previously required hours of highly skilled manual work are being augmented or replaced.

The supervisors managing these workflows are now making daily decisions about where AI output is trusted, where it is checked, and where it is wrong in ways that a non-technical eye would miss. That is a different job from the one these roles were designed for.

---

## Where the hiring brief has not kept up

Most briefs for senior post-production roles still describe the profile built for the previous generation of work: deep craft knowledge, track record managing large teams through complex deliveries, established industry relationships.

Those things still matter. But they are no longer sufficient on their own.

The brief that describes a "VFX Supervisor who understands AI" is underspecifying the problem. The relevant question is not whether someone can name the current tools. It is whether they have the technical literacy to understand what those tools are doing inside the pipeline, where they fail, and how to manage a team through a workflow that changes meaningfully year on year.

Studios that are running open searches for these roles are finding that candidates fall into one of two groups: people with the craft depth and leadership experience but limited working familiarity with AI-augmented pipelines; and people with strong AI tooling fluency but no track record managing large-scale post-production delivery. The dual profile is rare.

---

## What studios ahead of this are doing

The pattern across recent conversations with post-production leadership is consistent. Studios that are not in reactive hiring mode have taken two steps that others have not.

They audited the existing team before defining the external search. Before writing a brief, they assessed which senior people already had working familiarity with AI tooling — either through formal training or through personal initiative. In several cases, the person they needed was already in the building in a different role.

Then they changed the brief question. The filter is no longer "has this person worked with AI tools." It is "has this person successfully managed a major workflow transition before." The most relevant recent analogue is the integration of real-time rendering and virtual production into traditional VFX pipelines. The people who led that well, a few years ago, have the disposition for this shift — even if the specific tooling is different.

The search starts from there, not from a job description written for the previous cycle.

---

## Practical takeaway

If you are anticipating a senior post-production hire in the next 12 months, look at the brief now rather than when the role is formally open.

The question to ask: does this brief describe the job as it was three years ago, or as it is today? If the answer is three years ago, that is where to start the revision. Specifically, how the role manages an AI-augmented workflow — the decisions it makes daily, the skills required to make them well, and the leadership profile needed to bring a creative team through ongoing change.

The search built on that brief will produce a shorter, stronger shortlist.
