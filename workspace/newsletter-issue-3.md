## Three mistakes companies make when hiring AI creative leaders

### 1. Designing interviews that test knowledge instead of judgment

Most interview loops for AI creative leadership roles are built around what the candidate knows. What tools have they worked with? What productions have they been involved in? What's their view on generative AI in a studio environment?

These are reasonable questions. They're also questions any well-prepared candidate knows how to answer correctly.

What the process rarely asks: what did you get wrong? What AI implementation did you back that you'd approach differently now? What's a decision that looked right in isolation and turned out badly in production?

A candidate with real production experience has direct answers to those questions. They have specific failures. They remember the pipeline that didn't hold, the tool that worked in testing and broke under a live deadline, the creative director who needed convincing and why. The candidate who has been adjacent to AI work rather than accountable for it tends to give much more general answers.

The interview loop that can't distinguish between these two profiles is not testing what it thinks it's testing. Reorient at least one session around decisions made under real production pressure, not tools adopted or projects shipped.

### 2. Commissioning the search without the creative leadership's input

The mandate for an AI creative leadership hire typically originates at CEO, CTO, or board level. That's appropriate. The decision to invest in this kind of role is a strategic one.

The brief, however, often travels through HR or a department head without meaningful input from the creative directors, studio leads, or heads of production the new hire will actually work alongside. The brief reflects what the board wants from AI. It may not reflect what the studio actually needs from the person tasked with delivering it.

The result is a shortlist that impresses in a board room and struggles to earn trust in the studio. For senior roles in creative companies, that trust is not a secondary consideration. It's what makes the hire work.

This doesn't mean creative leads should own the brief. It means they should shape the criteria for what 'credible' looks like in their environment before the search starts. The difference between a brief built with that input and one built without it shows up on the shortlist.

### 3. Running the timeline on your process, not on the candidate's decision

The best candidates for senior AI creative roles are in work. They are not refreshing their inbox waiting for the right approach. They are evaluating your opportunity against the trajectory they're already on.

Their decision window is longer than most internal hiring timelines assume. If a candidate first engages with your role in September, the conditions that would make them seriously consider moving may not be fully formed until December. That is not procrastination. That is how considered career decisions get made at this level.

Most approval-to-offer processes were designed for candidates who are actively looking. They compress the timeline in ways that work when the candidate has already committed to moving. For a passive candidate who needs to reach that point, the same timeline creates a gap you cannot see from the inside, because the candidate is still engaged and still polite.

The fix is not to speed up your process. It is to brief the candidate earlier, understand what is actually driving their consideration before you reach the offer stage, and be prepared to work on their timetable as well as yours. The approach that says "we need an answer by Friday" to a candidate who was first approached four weeks ago will lose that candidate to a process that moves at their pace.

## From the network

Something is shifting in how senior AI creative leaders describe what they want from a next role. A year ago the priority was almost always the technology: what tooling is being built, what the production environment looks like, what scale the AI programme is at.

The conversation now is more often about organisational readiness. Has the board genuinely committed, or is AI still positioned as an experiment? Who else in the leadership team understands what this work requires? Is there a creative culture that will collaborate, or one that will resist?

The technical bar is assumed. What they're assessing is whether the organisation is ready to use them well. That shift in what candidates are evaluating has direct implications for how these roles need to be positioned and who in the client team should be doing the selling.
