## Article title

Why the best AI creative hires were made two years ago

---

## Article body

I placed someone at a mid-sized experiential studio in 2023. Head of Production. The brief said nothing about AI. It wasn't part of the conversation.

That person is now leading every AI-integrated creative workflow in the building.

I've seen this pattern a dozen times in the last eighteen months. Senior leaders I placed before AI was in anyone's job description are the people their organisations turned to when it arrived. Not because they were hired for it. Because of who they were before it showed up.

That distinction matters more than most hiring teams realise.

---

**What actually happened**

When AI tools started appearing inside creative pipelines, studios needed someone to figure out what to do with them. Not just technically (that part's relatively straightforward once the right person is involved), but editorially, operationally, financially.

The people who stepped into that role weren't the ones who'd been using Midjourney the longest. They were the ones who already understood why certain production decisions get made. Who could bring a creative director and a technical team to the same table without losing either of them. Who could see what a new capability actually solved for, and what it didn't.

Judgment. That's what they had.

They'd also built trust. They'd delivered difficult projects, on time, under pressure, in formats that didn't have a rulebook yet. When the studio director handed them something experimental, nobody questioned it.

Creative credibility, technical fluency, demonstrated delivery, and institutional trust. That combination is what's running AI creative operations right now. None of those things were in the job description.

---

**What studios are getting wrong**

I've started seeing a new pattern in the briefs coming across my desk.

"Proficiency with Sora." "Experience of Firefly in production workflows." "AI pipeline knowledge."

I understand why. The tools feel important because they're visible and changing fast. But hiring for tool familiarity is a proxy for the thing you actually want, and it's not a reliable one.

The tools will look different in eighteen months. The workflows built around them will shift. Someone who learned to use a specific image generation model in early 2024 is not the same as someone who can read a creative brief, identify where AI saves meaningful time, push back when it doesn't, and ship something that doesn't embarrass the studio.

What's happening is this: studios are trying to de-risk AI by hiring people who already know the current tools. What they're actually doing is narrowing the field to candidates who are fluent in today's capabilities but may not be able to handle whatever comes next.

The person who built physical experience installations in 2019 and figured out how to integrate real-time systems into live events in 2022 is better placed to run AI creative production than someone who learned Runway ML six months ago.

One is tool-fluent. The other is judgment-led.

---

**What the right brief looks like**

I'm not saying ignore tools entirely. Technical literacy matters. But the brief should lead with something different.

The things that actually predict success in AI creative roles right now:

A track record of pulling in unfamiliar technology without losing delivery quality. The ability to translate between creative and technical teams, specifically to be understood by both sides when things go wrong. A reputation for making decisions stick in ambiguous situations. Evidence of building something from scratch, without a manual, inside a live production context.

The question I find most useful: what did you build when the tool didn't exist yet?

If the answer is interesting, keep going. If the answer is "I started using AI tools as soon as they launched," that's table stakes, not a differentiator.

The best candidates for AI creative leadership aren't always the most vocal about AI. They're often the people who've been doing hard production work in emerging formats for years, and who've integrated AI into that work as one capability among many, not the headline.

---

**Why this matters now**

There's a version of this hiring mistake that's recoverable. You bring in someone tool-fluent, they do useful work, the tools evolve, and you adjust.

There's a version that isn't. You build your AI creative capability around tool knowledge rather than judgment, and eighteen months from now you're rebuilding the team because the talent you hired can't adapt when the tools shift again.

The people who will run AI creative operations in 2027 are in the market now. Most of them don't look particularly "AI." Their CVs say VFX, or experiential, or virtual production, or real-time. They've spent years solving hard production problems at the intersection of creative and technical. They built trust inside studios. They delivered things that didn't have a precedent.

They won't come from an AI job board. They won't respond to an ad with "Sora proficiency" in the requirements.
