## Post 1 — Market Signal: What the 2026 brief actually demands

**Pillar 2 | Market Intelligence | Market Signal**
**Hook type:** News/observation lead
**Format:** Observation post with directional market shift

---

The brief for AI creative production leadership has quietly changed.

Not in title.

Not in seniority level.

In what it actually requires.

A year ago, studios were hiring for AI literacy. The question was: does this person understand what AI is and where it's heading? Curiosity, perspective, an early track record. That was enough.

The briefs I'm working on now are asking something different.

They want evidence of full-cycle delivery.

A production slate that ran through AI-integrated pipelines from pre-production to delivery. Not a proof of concept. Not a pilot. Something that shipped.

The shift matters for candidates.

Positioning yourself around AI knowledge, tooling familiarity, or thought leadership is no longer sufficient on its own. The studios in active search mode have moved past exploratory conversations. They know what they need. They want to see where you've already done it.

If you're in this space and considering a move in the next 12 months, the question worth asking now is: what in your current role are you building that you can point to?

What's changed in how you think about your own AI production credentials over the last year?

P.S. The hardest feedback I give candidates right now is that their AI credentials are strong but their production delivery evidence is thin. Both are required.

## Post 2 — Candidate Tactical: What AI creative leaders get wrong when they start looking

**Pillar 3 | Leadership/Org | Candidate-perspective**
**Hook type:** Contrarian/Fear
**Format:** Problem-Agitate-Solution

---

Most AI creative leaders approach a job search the wrong way.

They wait.

Until the frustration is high enough. Until a role appears that looks interesting. Until the market seems right.

Then they update their profile, start responding to approaches, reconnect with some contacts.

The problem is the process they've walked into.

Passive availability in a niche talent market doesn't work the way it does in generalist spaces. The candidate pool for senior AI production leadership is genuinely small. The best roles at this level aren't advertised. They're filled through relationships built before there's a brief. By the time you're visible and open, the search may already be over.

The candidates I see move into the most interesting roles in this space didn't find them.

They were findable before they needed to be.

That's not luck. It's a decision made 18 months earlier about which conversations to have, which work to be visible about, and who in their network knows what they're building next.

What would you do differently in your current role if you were making that decision today?

P.S. Most searches in this space close before they're ever advertised publicly. If you haven't had a quality approach in the last six months, that's worth paying attention to.

## Post 3 — Candidate Perspective: The positioning mistake that kills candidacy before the first call

**Pillar 3 | Leadership/Org | Candidate-perspective**
**Hook type:** Bold personal declaration
**Format:** Story-Lesson-CTA

---

I've seen senior AI creative leaders torpedo their own candidacy before a first conversation has happened.

Not through poor interviews.

Not through mismatched ambitions.

Through how they described themselves.

The default positioning for someone at this level going into a career move is competence-first: the tools they know, the companies they've worked at, the AI frameworks they've applied. A credentials inventory.

Studios hiring at senior level in this space don't need convincing that the candidate knows AI.

They're trying to understand something harder: can this person lead through the parts of AI integration that have no script? The decisions with no precedent. The stakeholders with no framework for evaluating the technology. The moments where creative instinct and technical judgment have to be weighed simultaneously.

That question can't be answered by a list of tools.

The candidates who generate the strongest interest describe not what they know, but what they've navigated. The decisions made under uncertainty. The times they said no to a tool because of what it would cost downstream.

If your current professional summary reads like a CV, it's positioned for the wrong audience.

P.S. The most compelling candidate brief I've written this year was one paragraph. It was entirely about judgment under pressure.

*(~215 words)*

## Post 4 — Skills Gap Narrative: The gap runs in both directions

**Pillar 1 | Role Evolution | Market Signal / Candidate-facing**
**Hook type:** Contrarian/Myth-Bust
**Format:** Contrarian Rant

---

The skills gap in AI creative production is described as a pipeline problem.

Not enough people with AI skills entering the creative sector.

That framing is half right.

There are candidates with strong AI capability. Machine learning practitioners, AI product builders, technical specialists who have followed the technology into production contexts.

What doesn't exist in the same numbers is the other half.

Production fluency. The understanding of how creative work actually gets made at studio scale. The difference between an idea and a deliverable. What it takes to hold creative direction and technical rigour in the same room without losing either.

The rarest candidate in this market isn't the most technically advanced.

It's the person who has moved across both worlds with enough depth to be credible in either.

Studios that understand this stop posting for "an AI expert who understands production" and start asking a different question: who have I already heard about who has actually done both?

The skills gap runs in both directions.

The solution starts with framing the problem honestly.

What's your experience of this in your own team or studio?

P.S. I've worked with exceptional technical candidates who struggled in creative environments, and exceptional creative candidates who underestimated the technical depth required. Both are common. Neither gets discussed publicly.

*(~210 words)*
