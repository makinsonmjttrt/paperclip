# Winning your first retained mandate with zero track record

**A solo founder with 300+ contingency placements can close a first retained mandate within 2–4 months by deploying an advisory-led BD model, converting one existing relationship with a bridge engagement structure, and using published market intelligence as the primary door-opener.** The critical shift is behavioural, not structural: retained search is sold at problem level, not job-spec level, and the consultant who diagnoses the leadership gap becomes the natural partner to fill it. What follows is every extracted framework, script, and decision point required to execute that transition — drawn exclusively from practitioner sources who have done it.

## Section 1: Solving the cold-start problem when nobody has paid you a retainer

### Five practitioner-tested strategies for the first mandate

**Strategy 1: The pilot mandate at full methodology, reduced financial commitment.** James O'Brien (i-intro, retained search platform used by hundreds of UK firms) prescribes a three-step approach: design a documented retained service with visible enhancements over contingency (video assessments, behavioural profiling, structured shortlist presentation, post-hire support); offer this service to an existing client at no additional fee above your standard rate, requiring only exclusivity; after delivery, gather feedback and price the service at full retained rates for the next engagement. O'Brien's logic: "You'll be amazed how many employers, just like recruiters, don't want to go back to contingency once they've experienced the benefit of retained." The pilot establishes process credibility without asking the client to bet money on an unproven model.

**Strategy 2: Sell the problem, not the model.** Giig (recruitment technology platform, February 2026): "Retained recruitment is sold at problem level, not job-spec level." The technique: after a discovery conversation, send a short written summary of the client's leadership challenge back to them. Seeing their problem clearly articulated by someone who understands it increases perceived value immediately. Close with: "Given the importance of this role, it deserves a process that gives it the highest chance of success." Most clients are not against retained — they are against risk. Address the risk before discussing pricing.

**Strategy 3: Market candidates to generate mandates.** Paul Press (now Partner at Eastward Partners, profiled on Retrained Search podcast) broke through by marketing executive-level candidate profiles to private equity firms. Once PE firms engaged, he pivoted to retained-only terms and competed directly against Korn Ferry and Heidrick & Struggles — and won. For a solo founder in AI leadership for creative production, this means identifying **2–3 exceptional passive candidates** in the Director+ AI talent pool and using their anonymised profiles as conversation-starters with target companies.

**Strategy 4: The question-heavy diagnostic approach.** Mike Gionta (RecruiterU, 25+ years retained search coaching) insists: "Getting a retainer is not a pitch. The approach has to be very question-heavy." When asked about fees, his scripted response: "Right now, I have no idea what our fee would be in this situation because I have no idea (1) if we are the right firm to fill it, (2) if you have proper expectations of the marketplace, and (3) based on both of those, the level of complexity of the search. So, why don't we have a quick conversation?" His killer diagnostic question: "Imagine you have hired this person. You are walking down the hall with them a year later. They have met and exceeded all your expectations. What did they do?" This takes the client into the visual part of their brain and separates you from every recruiter asking for duties and responsibilities.

**Strategy 5: Use the niche scarcity argument.** For a talent pool of **200–500 individuals globally**, the retained model sells itself. The bridging narrative: "In seven years specialising in AI and creative production talent, I've completed 300+ placements — I know this market at a molecular level. For your Head of AI role, this talent pool is perhaps 200 people worldwide. That requires original research, confidential passive-candidate outreach, and structured assessment. That's what my retained practice delivers."

### The pilot mandate debate: both sides with a recommendation

**For reduced-fee first engagement:** O'Brien (i-intro) explicitly recommends offering the first retained engagement at standard contingency rates, requiring only exclusivity. Primaire Consulting used this approach and won **five retainers "almost straight away."** The logic is that process credibility matters more than fee credibility in the first engagement — once a client experiences retained-quality delivery, they will not revert.

**Against reduced-fee first engagement:** Jordan Taylor (Retrained Search, quadrupled revenue after transitioning) takes the opposite position: "I remember the first ever time I pitched a retainer, I'm never going to work a contingent role ever again. And I never did." His view is that any concession signals you do not fully believe in the model, and half-committed transitions produce half results. Greg Savage reinforces this: the contingent model is dying, and recruiters who cling to it will be "flattened and commoditised" by AI.

**Recommendation for a solo founder with no retained track record:** Use the container/engaged model as a bridge. Charge a **£5,000–£10,000 engagement fee on commencement** (not "upfront" — Retrained Search explicitly advises avoiding that word), with the remainder on placement. Require exclusivity. This structure gives the client financial commitment without full retained risk, gives you cash flow validation, and positions you clearly above contingency. TML Partners confirms: "Most container relationships will evolve into retainers as trust builds." After 2–3 successful container engagements, shift to full retained thirds.

### The psychological barriers and how practitioners overcame them

Enric Miquel (Pharma New Talent, grew from €500K to €10M after transitioning) describes the core barrier: "I felt the famous imposter syndrome — who are you to become a recruiter overnight — and that led to beginning with low fees. Overcoming this syndrome has been the most challenging part." His solution: **obsessive metric tracking** to build objective evidence of value delivered — fill rate, candidate retention, time-to-fill, market coverage.

Greg Savage provides the courage framework: "Selling exclusivity to a client might provoke stress and nerves. But once you believe in the benefits for the client, you have role-played the pitch, and you have predicted the objections that might come, you just need to tell yourself, **'Ten seconds of courage.'** And then you start."

James Cairns (The CSP Group, started a boutique with $30,000 and no agency experience, now operates 40% retained) identifies the decisive moment: "One summer night, sitting on his back porch, James made a decision that changed everything. He eliminated Plan B." His first year produced only three placements. But the elimination of alternatives forced total commitment to the higher-value model.

### Case studies: what they actually did

**Primaire Consulting** (James Russell and Paige Warburton): Had 10+ year contingency client relationships. Built an enhanced service specification and pitching strategy before approaching any client. Won five retained contracts quickly. One new client who had worked with 100+ recruiters said their retained method "looked really professional compared to what they were used to." Offered **12–24 month replacement guarantees** as the key differentiator — exposing the weakness in competitors' 90-day guarantees. Target: 25% retained in year 1, 50/50 by year 2. Actual pace was faster.

**WNTD** (Blaine Daws, co-founded two weeks before lockdown): Built multi-million retained partnerships in AI infrastructure. Core approach: "Act like an advisor, not an order taker, and anchor everything to outcomes your clients can measure." Used SLAs tied to delivery milestones rather than meeting counts. Discovery questions: "Why the role exists, what's failed so far, what competitors are paying, what 'good' looks like in a defined timeframe." Built credibility through "the courage to qualify out" — declining wrong-fit mandates.

**Carol Wenom** (40+ years recruitment): Used a **"position prospectus"** document — a written diagnostic and search strategy presented to the client before engagement — that elevated every conversation from transactional to consultative. Transitioning to retained **increased her billings by 70% in one year**.

## Section 2: The advisory-led business development framework

### Structuring an advisory conversation that is not a sales call

The advisory conversation follows four phases, synthesised from Elements Advisory Group (Darren Van Zyl), Consulting Success, and Pacific International frameworks:

**Phase 1 — Intention setting (5 minutes).** Open with: "What would make this conversation valuable for you?" This shifts the dynamic from pitch to partnership. Do not present capabilities. Do not discuss your firm. Listen.

**Phase 2 — Diagnostic discovery through market intelligence (20–25 minutes).** Lead with a prepared market insight relevant to the client's sector — not a capabilities overview. Share 2–3 specific data points about their talent landscape: compensation shifts, competitor hiring patterns, role architecture changes, candidate sentiment. Ask open-ended diagnostic questions. Key principle from Van Zyl: "In compliance, you're paid to know. In advisory, you're paid to ask better questions."

**Phase 3 — Insight exchange (15–20 minutes).** Present observations framed as patterns across the market, not sales ammunition. The goal is co-creation: help the client see their talent challenge through a new lens. Pacific International's David Howells describes this as natural: "The consultative approach built on mutual trust felt very natural to us, so even without realising it, we were already offering some of our clients the level of service traditional retained search firms did."

**Phase 4 — Natural bridge to engagement (5–10 minutes).** Frame future opportunities around the client's challenges: "Based on what we've discussed, I'm noticing a gap in [area] that could affect [outcome]. Would it be useful to explore that further?" Never propose a search mandate in the first meeting. Propose a next step that deepens the advisory relationship — a talent landscape briefing, a compensation benchmark, a candidate market map.

### What market intelligence a boutique can credibly offer

Five intelligence categories that internal teams and job boards cannot replicate:

**Compensation benchmarking across competitors.** You see what multiple employers are paying for the same role. No single company has this view. Even informal data from candidate conversations is valuable when aggregated.

**Talent landscape mapping.** A visual representation of where senior AI leaders sit across competitor organisations, their reporting lines, tenure, and likely movement triggers. Intellerati defines this as identifying "virtually every single executive candidate at target companies."

**Role architecture intelligence.** How comparable companies are structuring AI leadership — reporting lines, scope, title level, team size, budget authority. In an emerging domain like AI in creative production, there is no standard playbook. You are the playbook.

**Candidate sentiment and movement patterns.** What motivates senior AI leaders to move, what retention risks exist, what compensation premiums generate movement. This is proprietary intelligence from your candidate relationships.

**Competitive positioning analysis.** James Abruzzo (JGA Partners, via Hunt Scanlon): "Search firms identify challenges of their clients in recruiting top talent more clearly than their client — they have an outsider-looking-in view, the same view a potential candidate might see."

### What CXOs actually care about when buying retained search

From synthesis of buyer-side research across Stanton Chase, Fahrenheit Advisors, Keller Executive Search, and Capstone Search:

**Confidentiality** ranks first for sitting-executive replacement. Retained firms talk only to carefully selected people and reveal the client's identity at the latest possible stage. **Access to passive candidates** ranks second — career experts estimate 70–80% of executive roles are never made public. **Risk mitigation** ranks third — the cost of a bad senior hire runs to **2x annual salary**. **Time efficiency** for the hiring executive ranks fourth — a retained firm manages the entire process, reducing executive time commitment. **Market calibration** ranks fifth — knowing what "good" looks like externally when you have no internal benchmark.

What makes a client trust a boutique over a major firm: **no off-limits restrictions** (boutiques can approach candidates that larger firms cannot because of existing client relationships); **senior partner involvement throughout** (not hand-off to junior associates); and **deep sector specialisation** that generalist firms cannot match.

## Section 3: Converting contingency clients to retained buyers

### Greg Savage's conversion framework

Savage prescribes a **stepped progression: contingency → exclusive → retained**. He teaches selling exclusivity first, then adding a retainer component. His core argument: "The contingent, multi-listed perm market — I think that is over. It will be flattened and commoditised by AI." The recruiter must now take on "the client's hiring risk — not just a 'job-fill' — and structure the fee accordingly. And that means retained and exclusive briefs."

Savage identifies **four pillars of recruiter value** that justify retained fees. First, **defining the brief**: "Executive and search recruiters usually charge a third of their fee upfront. That's what it is for — the diagnostic." Second, **finding people**: "Great recruiters have 'unique candidates' — we find candidates the client cannot find themselves." Third, **bringing candidates to the hiring table**: "The client has LinkedIn. Everyone is there. Just knowing who a candidate is is not the same as bringing them to the hiring table." Fourth, **managing the process**: negotiation, candidate management, counteroffer handling.

His blog post "Why do clients make recruiters compete?" identifies eight specific client misconceptions and counters for each. The most relevant for senior roles: clients perceive recruiters as "CV-flickers" (counter: demonstrate the diagnostic, headhunting, and process management work — show the 90% of the iceberg they cannot see); clients think they get better candidate spread from multi-listing (counter: they get more inappropriate candidates and recruiters competing on speed, taking shortcuts); procurement leverages competition to drop fees (counter: "You need to go the other way" — increase fees through value positioning).

On the psychological barrier, Savage is characteristically direct: "The only sleepless nights I have had in recruitment is when I sold a retainer process to a client and a week later I had no candidates. Then I was interviewing candidates on Sunday morning over coffee. Do you think I would do that for clients who gave us orders in competition with four other recruiters?"

### Mike Gionta's "reverse retainer" script

For clients who say "we've never done retained" or had a bad experience:

*"Steve, if I did everything I said I did — I did not fill the opening because you cancelled it or found someone internally. Still, I sent you a portfolio of candidates. If you had that knowledge in advance — if there was a way for you to pull up a crystal ball — if God could descend from a cloud and tell you, would you be uncomfortable paying a retainer?"*

Client responds: "No, not at all, because you guys did do the work."

Then: *"Here is what I will do. I am going to give you retained-level service. [Map out what that means.] If you cancel the openings and/or fill it internally and I have presented you a portfolio of X candidates that you have interviewed in X amount of time, you will send me £7,000/£5,000/£10,000 at the end of the search as a consulting fee for providing you the knowledge that you are hiring the best available person in the market."*

Gionta's graduated transition plan: drop non-exclusive contingency immediately; accept only exclusive contingency as transitional; use the reverse retainer as a bridge for new clients; convert to full retainer on second engagement — "Now that they are comfortable with me, we can switch them on the second search with the money upfront."

### The "drawing a line" script for legacy clients

Gionta provides specific language for converting clients who have benefited from retained-level service on contingency terms:

*"I took a bloodbath financially on those five searches when you cancelled them. You got retained-level service."*

Client: "We're not going to do that again."

*"Are you 100% convinced I will fill the openings? And you just said you are 100% convinced you will not change the specs? So if I bill you £5,000 per position upfront, with the balance on the completed search, there is no problem — because you are 100% convinced I will fill them, and you are 100% convinced you will not cancel them. You are advancing me money that you would send me two months from now."*

### Jordan Taylor's conversion methodology (Retrained Search)

Taylor provides the framing that makes retained fees irrelevant: "When we engage with clients, there's typically a message behind the recruitment. They're looking for a new Sales Director — it's not because they're looking for a new Sales Director, it's because they want to grow revenue by £10 million next year. So when you realise the **£10 million on the line**, fees don't come into it anywhere near as much."

His fee structure: clients pay in thirds — a third on commencement of project, a third on submission of shortlist (typically five candidates), a third on placement. Revenue impact: Taylor moved from billing £100–150K per year contingently to billing more than that per quarter on retained.

### When to attempt conversion and which clients are convertible

**Optimal timing** (consensus across Savage, Gionta, Taylor, O'Brien):

- After a successful placement — you have proof of delivery
- When the role is genuinely senior or strategic — the business impact justifies the model
- After the client has been burned by contingent failure — they have experienced the downside
- When talent is scarce — the harder the search, the easier the retained sell
- On the second or third engagement — after trust is established

**Convertible clients:** Those who see hiring as an investment, not a cost. Those hiring at Director+ level. Those who have experienced a bad hire. Line managers and hiring authorities — not HR or procurement (Finkel's consistent principle). Those who already give you exclusive contingency work.

**Not convertible:** Procurement-driven organisations that treat recruitment as a commodity. Clients who respond to the retained conversation with "I just want you to keep an eye out" — Jordan Taylor: "Now you have your answer. It's not a serious search." High-volume hiring clients who need bodies, not leaders.

### Risk mitigation for the conversion conversation

**Risk: Losing the client entirely.** Mitigation: use the pilot approach (O'Brien/i-intro) or reverse retainer (Gionta) as a bridge. Never ultimatum an existing client.

**Risk: Reverting to contingent under pressure.** Mitigation: Gionta's graduated approach — drop non-exclusive contingency immediately but grandfather existing exclusive-contingency clients with a good-faith clause.

**Risk: Underdelivering on first retained mandate.** Mitigation: Savage's accountability principle — the retainer creates commitment that drives quality. His confession about interviewing candidates on Sunday morning after selling a retainer is the point: you will work harder when you have taken money.

### Steve Finkel's methodology

Finkel's book *Unlimited Clients!* (2020) contains a dedicated section titled "Transitioning from Contingency to Retainer" with proprietary scripts. The specific methodology is behind a paywall ($45–85 at stevefinkel.com), but reviewers confirm it includes step-by-step conversation tracks for the transition. His foundational principle is directly applicable: **motivate hiring authorities, not HR** — the person who understands the business value of a senior hire and can authorise a retainer is the line manager, not procurement.

## Section 4: How published intelligence generates retained mandates

### The empirical case for thought leadership

The **2024 Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report** (3,500+ management-level professionals, seven countries) provides the numbers: **73% of decision-makers** trust thought leadership content over marketing materials when assessing capabilities. **90% of decision-makers** are moderately or very likely to be more receptive to sales outreach from a company that consistently produces high-quality thought leadership. **60%** say a piece of thought leadership revealed they were missing a significant business opportunity. **95% of business clients** are not actively seeking services at any given time — thought leadership reaches the hidden majority.

### Content types ranked by mandate-generation effectiveness

**Tier 1: Proprietary research reports with original data.** Annual benchmark reports with compensation data, market maps, and role architecture analysis. These create gated downloads that identify prospects and position the firm as the definitive market authority. The data itself becomes the reason clients call — they need the expert who gathered it to help interpret and act on it. Artico Search's CISO benchmark and Heidrick & Struggles' six compensation survey series are the models.

**Tier 2: Regular market intelligence newsletters.** Weekly or fortnightly digests targeting a specific buyer persona. The newsletter-to-mandate pipeline: regular cadence keeps the firm front-of-mind; when a hiring need emerges, the subscriber already trusts the source. Critical requirement: every issue must deliver standalone value (market intel, movement tracking, salary data points) — not promote the firm.

**Tier 3: LinkedIn thought leadership and podcast content.** The Rosenstein Group (boutique martech executive search) built the Martalks Podcast interviewing innovators among customers. LinkedIn following grew significantly over two years; the podcast accelerated new business conversations by providing a reason to connect with potential clients and candidates simultaneously.

**Tier 4: Published thought pieces on industry trends.** Short-form analysis of market movements, role evolution, compensation shifts. C-suite and VP+ titles who post on LinkedIn get approximately **600% more reach** than other users, and only **1% of LinkedIn's 1B+ members** post weekly — competition for attention is minimal.

### LinkedIn content strategy for a retained search consultant

**Post 2–3 times per week minimum.** Content pillars should rotate between market data and intelligence, role and function evolution analysis, leadership and hiring insights, and industry-specific trend commentary.

**What to post:** Compensation data points and trends. Commentary on leadership transitions in the niche. Anonymised insights from search activity. Mini market maps showing how AI leadership is being structured across VFX, virtual production, experiential, and spatial computing companies. Behind-the-search insights demonstrating advisory depth without breaking confidentiality.

**What to avoid:** Job postings (positions you as a recruiter, not an advisor). Generic motivational content (destroys credibility with C-suite buyers). Content that reads as marketing rather than expert insight. Over-sharing personal anecdotes or performative vulnerability — C-suite buyers find this off-putting.

**Profile optimisation:** Headline should read as market advisor, not recruiter. "Advisor on AI Leadership for Creative Production | Senior Search" rather than "Executive Recruiter | VFX & AI."

**Comment strategy:** Engage thoughtfully on posts by target buyers (CTOs, CHROs, CEOs at studios and agencies). Your name, headline, and insight become visible to their entire network. LinkedIn newsletters build a subscriber base within the platform itself.

## Section 5: The weekly BD operating rhythm for months 1–3

### Realistic activity metrics for a solo retained search founder

A solo practitioner running three BD days per week (Monday, Tuesday, Thursday) should target **8–12 outreach conversations per BD day**, generating **3–5 deep advisory conversations per week**. Gary Stauble (The Recruiting Lab) recommends enough daily marketing calls to generate two "A-level" searches per week plus five candidate-mapping calls per week. Mike Gionta (RecruiterU) teaches that the MPC (Most Placeable Candidate) approach — presenting a strong candidate profile to generate client conversations — outperforms generic service pitches for opening doors.

In months 1–3, the recommended split is **60–70% BD and client-side activity, 30–40% candidate network building**. This shifts to approximately 50/50 once the first mandate is secured. Mark Whitby's critical math: transitioning from contingency to retained increases fill rate from 20–25% to 80–90% — a **400% efficiency increase** with the same amount of work.

### The conversion timeline from first conversation to signed mandate

**Typical timeline: 4–12 weeks** from first substantive advisory conversation to signed mandate. A "burning platform" hire (departing executive, new role for imminent initiative) compresses to 2–3 weeks. Strategic or succession-planning conversations extend to 3–6 months. For a solo founder with an existing network of 300+ placements, the first mandate should close within **2–4 months** of launch.

**Pipeline stages and durations:** Target identification and research (ongoing from week 0); initial contact and warm outreach (weeks 1–2); advisory conversation of 30–45 minutes (weeks 2–4); proposal and methodology presentation (weeks 4–8); negotiation and contracting (weeks 6–10); signed mandate and first retainer payment (weeks 8–12). Post-signing, the AESC benchmark for executive search completion is **90 days**, with a standard range of 90–120 days.

### Leading indicators that BD activity is working before revenue appears

**Advisory conversations per week trending upward** — target 3–5 per week by end of month 2. **Second-meeting rate above 30%** — if more than 30% of first conversations lead to a follow-up, the advisory positioning is resonating. **Inbound enquiries** — even 1–2 per month signals market positioning is working. **Referral introductions** — being introduced by contacts to others indicates trust building. **"When we have a need, we'll call you" responses** — these are pipeline entries, not rejections; track and nurture them. **Candidate-side pull** — senior candidates proactively reaching out to be on your radar means you are being seen as a serious search practitioner.

**Milestone targets:** By end of month 1, 15–20 advisory conversations completed, target company list of 50–75 organisations built, 10+ senior candidate relationships initiated. By end of month 2, 2–3 companies expressing future hiring intent, first proposal submitted. By end of month 3, 1–2 proposals in play, first retainer signed or strong verbal commitment, 50+ senior candidate relationships in network.

### ADHD-optimised weekly schedule with single-task blocks

The schedule below uses the existing four-block day structure with BD days (Monday, Tuesday, Thursday) and delivery days (Wednesday, Friday). Each block is 90 minutes with **15-minute buffers** between blocks for transition. Only 50% of each block is formally scheduled — the remainder is buffer for tasks that run long or unexpected needs. The "ADHD tax" rule applies: double your time estimate for every task.

**Monday — Outreach and conversations**

- Block 1 (09:00–10:30): Cold and warm outreach calls, 6–8 attempts. Call list pre-prepared Sunday evening.
- Block 2 (10:45–12:15): Scheduled advisory conversations, 2–3 calls.
- Block 3 (13:15–14:45): LinkedIn engagement and email follow-ups. Batch all written communication here.
- Block 4 (15:00–16:00): CRM updates and plan Tuesday call list.

**Tuesday — Follow-up and deepening**

- Block 1 (09:00–10:30): Follow-up calls and proposal discussions with warm prospects.
- Block 2 (10:45–12:15): Scheduled second meetings and proposal presentations.
- Block 3 (13:15–14:45): Content creation — one LinkedIn post, one insight piece, or newsletter draft.
- Block 4 (15:00–16:00): Research new target companies and contacts.

**Wednesday — Candidate network (delivery day)**

- Focus entirely on candidate conversations, market mapping, industry research, and search delivery when mandates are live. No BD. No context switching.

**Thursday — Network and nurture**

- Block 1 (09:00–10:30): Outreach calls to new targets, 6–8 attempts.
- Block 2 (10:45–12:15): Referral conversations and introductions.
- Block 3 (13:15–14:45): Administrative tasks, invoicing, pipeline review.
- Block 4 (15:00–16:00): Weekly metrics review. Plan next week's call lists.

**Friday — Strategic work (delivery day)**

- Candidate work, search execution, strategic planning, learning, content research. No BD.

### ADHD-specific adjustments built into the system

**Three-tier targeting** prevents the all-or-nothing trap. Every BD day has three levels: bare minimum (3 calls and 2 follow-up emails — maintains momentum on low-energy days), standard (6–8 calls and 2–3 advisory conversations), and peak performance (10+ calls and content creation). Caren Magill (It's ADHD Friendly) recommends a **routine workflow** — doing things in a specific order rather than a rigid time-based schedule. Complete step A, then step B, then step C, regardless of what time it is.

**Decision fatigue management:** Pre-plan the call list the evening before. When you sit down at your desk, the decisions are already made — you just execute. Use a physical sticky note next to the phone to track calls versus conversations — low-tech, immediate visual feedback. **Body doubling** (having someone on a video call while you do BD calls, even silently) dramatically improves task initiation for ADHD.

### Which BD activities generate the highest conversion in months 1–3

Based on practitioner evidence, ranked by conversion effectiveness:

- **Warm outreach to existing network** (past clients, past candidates now in leadership) — highest conversion, lowest friction. You have 300+ placements of relationship capital.
- **MPC marketing** (presenting anonymised exceptional candidate profiles to target companies) — Paul Press built his entire retained practice this way.
- **Advisory and insight-led outreach** (sharing specific market intelligence, not pitching services) — positions you as advisor from first contact.
- **Content-led inbound** — one Resilient Recruiter podcast guest replaced cold outreach with a £4,000 content campaign that generated £55,000 in revenue.
- **Referral requests** — explicitly asking existing contacts: "Who else should I be talking to about AI leadership in creative production?"

### Months 1–3 activity prioritisation

**Month 1 (weeks 1–4): Foundation and activation.** Weeks 1–2: finalise positioning, build target company list of 50–75 organisations, create CRM, optimise LinkedIn profile, draft the "State of AI Leadership in Creative Production" survey instrument. Weeks 3–4: contact 30–50 people from existing network. Frame as: "I've launched my own firm specialising in AI leadership for creative production — I'd love to get your perspective on the market." Target 10–15 advisory conversations. Begin LinkedIn content at 2–3 posts per week.

**Month 2 (weeks 5–8): Pipeline building.** Full three-day BD rhythm operational. Weekly targets: 15–20 new outreach attempts, 5–8 live advisory conversations, 2–3 follow-up meetings, one piece of original content, 5–10 candidate conversations on delivery days. Milestone: 30+ advisory conversations completed, 2–3 prospects in active discussion, first proposal submitted.

**Month 3 (weeks 9–12): Conversion push.** Increase proposal activity to 2–3 proposals in play. Offer complimentary talent landscape briefings to warm prospects. Begin converting candidate intelligence into client advisory value. Add 1–2 industry events or virtual roundtables per month. Target: first signed retained mandate by end of month 3.

### The Randee Staats point-based BD system for consistency

Randee Staats (S4 Search Partners, profiled on Resilient Recruiter podcast) rebuilt from $250,000 in debt to a **seven-figure desk within one year** using a daily point system. Assign point values to different BD activities — calls, meetings, emails, LinkedIn engagements — and target a minimum daily point total to maintain pipeline health. The system prevents zero-BD days, which are the real danger for ADHD founders. Even a bare-minimum day scores points and maintains momentum.

## Conclusion: three decisions that determine whether the first mandate closes

The research converges on three strategic decisions that separate founders who close their first retained mandate from those who remain stuck in contingency.

**Decision one: commitment level.** Every practitioner who successfully transitioned — Taylor, Cairns, Miquel, Daws — describes a moment of eliminating the fallback option. Half-committed transitions produce half results. The container/engaged model is a legitimate bridge, but the founder must be psychologically committed to retained as the destination, not the experiment.

**Decision two: lead with intelligence or lead with services.** The advisory-led model — where published data, compensation benchmarks, and market maps open doors — consistently outperforms the service-pitch model. Artico Search proved a firm founded in 2021 could achieve market authority within three years through co-produced research. The "State of AI Leadership in Creative Production" annual benchmark is the single highest-leverage BD investment available in this niche, because no equivalent data exists.

**Decision three: who to approach first.** The research strongly favours approaching **mid-market companies making first-time AI leadership hires** (the 50–500 employee segment identified in the existing operating plan). These companies lack established search relationships, have no internal recruitment infrastructure for senior roles, and cannot calibrate what "good" looks like externally. They need the advisory conversation more than anyone — and they are the most likely to say yes to a boutique founder who can demonstrate molecular-level knowledge of a 200–500 person global talent pool that no other search firm currently owns.
