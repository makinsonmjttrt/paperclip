# 1. CONTRADICTION MAP

## Contradiction 1: Discount the first mandate or hold the line?

**Position A — Discount to break in.** O'Brien (i-intro) prescribes offering the first retained engagement at standard contingency rates, requiring only exclusivity. Primaire Consulting used this approach and won five retainers "almost straight away." Whitby offers flexible structures including a "nominal deposit of $2,500" for reluctant first-time buyers. Logic: process credibility matters more than fee credibility on the first deal. (DR1, DR4)

**Position B — Never discount, ever.** Taylor (Retrained Search) never worked a contingent role again from his first retained pitch. Savage says a discounted fee means a discounted you. DR3 states explicitly: "Do not discount fees. Instead, reduce client risk through engagement structure." GSJ Consulting warns: "Discounting fills a gap created by a weak value proposition." (DR1, DR3, DR4)

**Judgement for your situation:** Position B, but with the container model as the structural bridge. You have no retained track record, which makes discounting tempting, but your niche scarcity (200-500 global candidates, no competitor search firm) makes it unnecessary. The container model (£5,000-£8,000 upfront, full percentage rate, balance on placement) gives the client lower financial risk without training the market to expect discounts from you. Frame it as "our engaged search model for first projects together" and hold your percentage. The distinction matters: you're adjusting payment timing, not price.


## Contradiction 2: Container engagement fee — £3,000 or £8,000?

**Position A — £3,000-£5,000.** The Operating Manual (Block K, Block L) sets the container floor at £3,000-£5,000 with four-week exclusivity.

**Position B — £5,000-£10,000.** DR1 recommends £5,000-£10,000. DR3 recommends increasing to £5,000-£8,000, noting the current plan "is priced at the low end of market." Y Scouts charges $5,000-$8,000. O'Brien's Danish client explicitly said his 20% fee was too low — clients read low fees as low commitment.

**Judgement for your situation:** £5,000-£8,000 is correct. The £3,000 floor from the Operating Manual was set before the gap closure research confirmed that retained search norms are 30-33% of total compensation. At £3,000, you're signalling contingency-level commitment. At £5,000-£8,000, you're in the range that credible container firms charge. For Director-level searches (£80K-£120K base, generating £20K-£36K total fees), a £5,000 engagement fee represents 14-25% of total — below the industry one-third standard but enough to signal genuine retained commitment. Update the Operating Manual.


## Contradiction 3: Fee anchoring — 25%, 28.6%, or 30%?

**Position A — Quote 25-30% range.** Operating Manual and Gap Closure Research anchor at 25-30% of base salary. This is already conservative relative to the industry norm of 30-33% of total compensation.

**Position B — Quote exactly 28.6%.** Gionta's signature technique: the odd number signals precision and data-backed methodology. Clients negotiate less aggressively against calculated numbers than round ones. (DR3)

**Position C — Anchor at 30% and negotiate to 28%.** DR4 positions 25-30% as "lower-to-mid range, appropriate for a solo practitioner building a track record." TriSearch reports specialist firms command 33% premiums. (DR3)

**Judgement for your situation:** Start at 30% and hold. Here's why: your minimum fee floor is £24,000. At a Director-level base of £80K, 30% generates £24,000 — exactly your floor. At 25%, you get £20,000 — below floor. Anchoring at 25% forces you into awkward conversations where your percentage doesn't cover your minimum. Anchor at 30%, present it as your standard rate, and use the £24,000 minimum fee as the floor for lower-salary roles. Reserve the 28.6% technique for when you have the time-and-motion data to back it — right now it's borrowed precision you can't defend. If pushed, come down to 28% in exchange for a concession (exclusivity, multiple mandates, longer guarantee waiver). Never go below 25%.


## Contradiction 4: Phone first or digital first?

**Position A — Phone is the gold standard.** DR2 cites 57% of C-level prefer phone as first contact. Savage's entire methodology centres on picking up the phone. Social Talent reports ~50% response rate for scheduled phone calls versus single-digit InMail rates. (DR2)

**Position B — Email and LinkedIn first, phone as escalation.** DR2 also notes cold calling is "increasingly impractical at senior level" — direct office numbers are hard to find. The Operating Manual (Block H) sequences LinkedIn and email first, with phone on Day 12. C-level InMail response rate is 6.98% (lowest of any seniority), but email achieves 6.4% C-level average, rising to 10.44% with timeline-based hooks. (DR2, Operating Manual)

**Judgement for your situation:** Lead with email, use LinkedIn to warm, phone to convert. Here's the practical reality: your first 20 research conversations will be with people you're approaching cold or semi-warm. You don't have their direct phone numbers. You do have their email addresses (via Hunter.io, Apollo, or LinkedIn). Send the email first (under 150 words, WIIFM-led). Send the LinkedIn connection request on Day 2. If connected, engage with their content. Phone on Day 3-5 once they've seen your name. This matches the multi-channel sequence in DR2 (email Day 1, LinkedIn Day 2, phone Day 3) and achieves the 34.5% response rate SourceWhale found for multi-channel plus hyper-personalisation. The phone remains the conversion channel — once you've booked the call, the phone is where the relationship happens.


## Contradiction 5: How many BD conversations per week?

**Position A — 8-12 outreach conversations per BD day.** DR1 cites this as the solo practitioner target across three BD days, generating 3-5 deep advisory conversations per week. Gionta targets enough daily marketing calls to generate two A-level searches per week.

**Position B — Three calls a day to people not expecting to hear from you.** Savage's discipline. Three calls per day touches the entire 200-500 person network roughly once per quarter. (DR2)

**Position C — 20 connections and 5 messages per week.** Operating Manual Block S allocates 6-8 hours total BD per week.

**Judgement for your situation:** Savage's three calls a day is the correct anchor for months 1-3. The 8-12 outreach conversations per BD day is a mature-practice target — it assumes you have a full pipeline, a call list prepared, and momentum. You don't have any of that yet. Start with Savage's discipline: three calls per day, every working day. That's 15 per week. Of those, you'll get through to perhaps 5-7 (one-third to half). That generates 3-5 genuine conversations per week, which matches DR1's advisory conversation target. The Operating Manual's 20 connections/5 messages figure is the LinkedIn component only and sits alongside the calls. Don't try to hit 8-12 per BD day from week one — you'll burn out before the pipeline builds.


## Contradiction 6: Placement guarantee wording

**Position A — Promise to fill the role.** Whitby: "That's not going to happen! Our success rate is 98%. I don't accept search assignments unless I'm confident of success." (DR4)

**Position B — Promise process and retention, not placement.** Retrained Search: "Success isn't just putting a bum on a seat." O'Brien redirects toward retention guarantee (96% at 12 months, 93% at 24 months). (DR4)

**Judgement for your situation:** Process and retention guarantee only. You cannot claim a 98% retained success rate with zero retained mandates. You can credibly promise: a rigorous methodology, comprehensive market coverage of a finite talent pool, and a 12-month replacement guarantee conditional on client onboarding compliance. When your contingency track record comes up, reframe it through Whitby's lens: "Your case studies don't have to be retained searches." Use your 300+ placements as outcome proof without claiming a retained-specific completion rate you don't yet have.


## Contradiction 7: Proposal or agreement?

**Position A — Send an agreement for signing.** Whitby: "Don't send speculative proposals, send agreements for signing. A proposal implies they have a choice; an agreement implies they have already decided." (DR4)

**Position B — Send a detailed proposal.** JRG Partners details a nine-section proposal structure. DR4 includes a full proposal template. DR3 recommends different proposal lengths for CXO buyers (4-6 pages) versus HR buyers (8-10 pages). (DR3, DR4)

**Judgement for your situation:** For your market (mid-market CXOs making first-time AI leadership hires), send a short proposal that reads like an agreement. These buyers have never used retained search. They need enough context to understand what they're buying (4-6 pages with methodology, fee, and guarantee) but the document should end with a signature block and next steps, not "we look forward to hearing from you." Whitby's principle is correct in spirit — project confidence. But a CXO who has never engaged a retained firm needs more education than a pure agreement provides. The hybrid: a "Search Engagement Brief" that's structured as a proposal but closes as a contract.


## Contradiction 8: Whether to continue contingency work in parallel

**Position A — Eliminate the fallback entirely.** Taylor, Cairns, and Miquel all describe eliminating contingency as the decisive moment. "Half-committed transitions produce half results." (DR1)

**Position B — Maintain contingency selectively for cash flow.** Operating Manual Block N permits contingency "for highly specific pipeline-building purposes where the required candidate pool directly overlaps with existing retained mandates." Gap 8 flags the revenue bridge as unresolved.

**Judgement for your situation:** Continue contingency work for the first 90 days, but with two hard rules. First, only accept contingency briefs where the candidate pool overlaps with your retained target market (this builds your AI leadership network while generating cash flow). Second, set a hard deadline: no new contingency briefs accepted after the end of month 3, regardless of whether a retained mandate has closed. The revenue bridge is real — you cannot build a practice while panicking about rent. But the deadline creates the forcing function that Taylor, Cairns, and Miquel describe. The danger isn't doing contingency work in parallel; it's doing it indefinitely because it's comfortable.

# 2. TOP 20 TECHNIQUES, RANKED BY STAGE-APPROPRIATE IMPACT

**Ranking logic:** You haven't pitched retained yet. Techniques that build the competence and confidence to make that first pitch rank highest. Techniques that generate qualified pipeline rank second. Closing techniques rank third — they're important but useless until the first two stages are passed.

### STAGE A: Building competence and confidence to pitch credibly

**1. The 20 research conversations framework (DR2, Section 5)**
Run 20 structured conversations with senior AI leaders in creative production before pitching retained to any client. Sequence: conversations 1-5 with accessible Director-level contacts (calibration), 6-10 with VP-level at larger companies (intelligence deepening), 11-15 with C-suite and prominent leaders (authority), 16-20 with referral contacts and second-round calls (reinforcement). Each conversation uses the PIR framework: Perspective, Intelligence, Referrals. This single activity simultaneously builds your candidate network, generates proprietary market intelligence, tests your positioning language, produces your market briefing deliverable, and gives you the confidence of having spoken to 20 senior people in the niche before you ever pitch a client. Nothing else comes close to this in terms of compound return.

**2. Gionta's diagnostic question sequence (DR1, DR3, DR4)**
The retained pitch is won in the diagnostic, not the close. Memorise and practise these questions until they're automatic: "What are the consequences of this position remaining open?" / "If there is no one in this role by [date], what is the downside to the company?" / "Imagine you have hired this person. You are walking down the hall with them a year later. They have met and exceeded all your expectations. What did they do?" The last question takes the client into the visual part of their brain and separates you from every recruiter asking for duties and responsibilities.

**3. Eliminating tentative language (DR3)**
Savage identifies tentative language as the single fastest way to destroy fee credibility. Words to permanently eliminate: "normally," "approximately," "usually," "I feel that," "sometimes," "our average price," "ballpark figure," "maybe," "probably." Replace with specific, definitive statements. "The project fee for this search is £45,000, structured in three milestone payments, and backed by a 12-month replacement guarantee." Practise this in front of a mirror or on camera until you can say your fee without flinching, qualifying, or adding softeners.

**4. Write SOPs for each search phase before the first mandate (DR4)**
SOPs are not bureaucracy — they are an external executive function for ADHD. Write one SOP per search phase: intake workshop, research and identification, outreach and qualification, assessment and shortlisting, client interviews and selection, offer and close. Each SOP tells you what to do next when your brain can't remember. This is the single highest-value preparatory investment for a solo ADHD operator because it means you never have to make process decisions under cognitive load. Build these during the 12-week practice calendar.

**5. The advisory conversation four-phase structure (DR1, Section 2)**
Phase 1 — Intention setting (5 min): "What would make this conversation valuable for you?" Phase 2 — Diagnostic discovery through market intelligence (20-25 min): Lead with prepared market insight, share 2-3 data points. Phase 3 — Insight exchange (15-20 min): Present observations as patterns, co-create understanding. Phase 4 — Natural bridge (5-10 min): "Based on what we've discussed, I'm noticing a gap in [area] that could affect [outcome]. Would it be useful to explore that further?" Never propose a search mandate in the first meeting. This structure works for every conversation — research, advisory, and pitch.

**6. Whitby's "recommend, don't offer options" (DR4)**
When you reach the point of suggesting retained: "Considering the importance of this appointment to your business, and the consequences of getting it wrong, the logical solution is retained search." Not "would you like retained or contingent?" Not "we offer both models." A recommendation, framed as a diagnosis. Practise this phrasing until it feels natural, not prescriptive.

**7. The market briefing as signature deliverable (DR2)**
Across your 20 research conversations, you simultaneously build a market briefing document covering: the state of AI leadership in creative production, emerging role archetypes, compensation benchmarks (anonymised, aggregated), talent supply observations, key skills gaps, and recommendations. This single document, built from real conversations, becomes the most credible piece of collateral your practice can produce. It replaces the need for a retained track record because it demonstrates the thing clients actually buy: deep market knowledge.

### STAGE B: Generating qualified pipeline

**8. Three-tier contact strategy: source calls → door openers → decision makers (DR2)**
Contact industry sources first (conference speakers, VES board members, VC partners) — not as candidates but as domain experts. Ask: "Who is doing the most interesting work in AI-driven creative production?" Each source call generates 2-3 new names. Within 10-15 source calls, you have a mapped landscape with warm paths to most targets. This is how retained search has always worked — the research phase generates the network.

**9. MPC marketing: anonymised candidate profiles to generate mandates (DR1)**
Paul Press built his entire retained practice by marketing executive-level candidate profiles to PE firms. Identify 2-3 exceptional passive candidates in the Director+ AI talent pool. Create anonymised one-page profiles. Use these as conversation starters with target companies: "I'm representing a VP-level AI leader currently at a major VFX studio who is considering their next move. Would this profile be relevant to your plans?" This flips the power dynamic — you're bringing talent to them.

**10. "BD by not doing BD" — research invitations and content as door-openers (DR1, Operating Manual)**
Two mechanisms: (a) "We're putting together an industry report on AI leadership in creative production. Before we do the research, I'd like to understand what data points you'd find most valuable." (b) The "Two Distinguished Minutes" video series: invite CXOs to record a two-minute answer to "Why do you do what you do?" Both create genuine engagement without a sales ask.

**11. Savage's three calls a day (DR2)**
Three phone calls a day to people not expecting to hear from you. Targets: past clients who've moved on, candidates you've placed who are now in leadership, someone from a conference, someone in your candidate pool you promised to follow up with. Critical instruction: "Don't ask for anything. Just engage, listen, empathise and share." This is a daily discipline, not a BD tactic. It compounds.

**12. The multi-channel outreach sequence (DR2)**
Day 1: personalised email (under 150 words). Day 2: LinkedIn connection request. Day 3: phone call attempt + voicemail. Day 5: engage with their LinkedIn content. Day 7: follow-up email (new insight). Day 10: LinkedIn message. Day 14: second phone call. Day 21: final email, respectful close. 42% of all campaign replies come from follow-ups, yet 48% of senders never send a second message.

**13. Trigger event monitoring and outreach (Operating Manual, DR1)**
Track 20-30 target companies for three events: leadership departures, product expansion announcements, and capital injections. When triggered, use the KRP framework: "I understand you're currently looking to [expand/implement/restructure]. Here's an example of work we've done in that area. I think it's valuable for us to connect." Trigger events create genuine urgency that makes the retained conversation natural.

**14. The "State of AI Leadership in Creative Production" annual benchmark (DR1, DR2, Gap Closure Research)**
The single highest-leverage BD investment available in this niche. Survey 50-100 AI leaders on compensation, reporting lines, team structures, and organisational maturity. Follow the Artico Search/IANS Research template. Every survey participant becomes both a candidate relationship and a client touchpoint. The report generates media coverage, positions you as the definitive authority, and creates the advisory conversations that convert to mandates. This is a medium-term investment (months 3-6 to produce) but its compound returns are unmatched.

### STAGE C: Converting to a signed retained engagement

**15. The container model as bridge engagement (DR1, DR3)**
£5,000-£8,000 engagement fee on commencement, full percentage rate, balance on placement, four-week exclusivity. Frame it explicitly as a bridge: "For our first project together, I'd recommend starting with our engaged search model. Once you've seen the quality of execution, we'll move to our standard retained structure." This gives the client low risk while preserving your fee integrity.

**16. Taylor's "always sell retained down" as qualification (DR4)**
Propose retained first for every conversation. The response tells you everything: "Sure" too quickly = may not be serious enough. "No, I'll get back to you" = high buying behaviour (needs internal approval). "No, not a serious search" = you've saved weeks. This isn't a closing technique — it's a diagnostic that prevents you wasting time on searches that were never real.

**17. Cost of vacancy / bad hire calculator (DR3, DR4, Operating Manual)**
Quantify the cost before stating your fee. For a £200K total-comp AI leader, a conservative 10% productivity impact on a £10M production pipeline = ~£83K per month of vacancy. Cost of a bad hire: 3-5x annual salary (Harvard Business School) = £600K-£1M. Your £45K-£60K fee is then 5-10% of the cost of getting it wrong. Present this calculation explicitly. Build a simple spreadsheet that generates these numbers per role.

**18. Gionta's "reverse retainer" for hesitant first-time buyers (DR1, DR4)**
"If I did everything I said I did — I did not fill the opening because you cancelled it or found someone internally — still I sent you a portfolio of candidates. If you had that knowledge in advance, would you be uncomfortable paying a retainer?" Client says no. "Here's what I'll do. Retained-level service. If you cancel and I've presented you X candidates, you send me £5,000-£7,000 at the end as a consulting fee for proving you're hiring the best available person in the market." This is the ultimate bridge for clients who've never used retained and are afraid of upfront commitment.

**19. Silence after stating the fee (DR3)**
MIT Sloan research: 3-second pauses shift negotiators into reflective state. Chris Voss: "State your rate, then stop. Let the client respond first. Whoever speaks first usually concedes." State: "The project fee is £45,000, structured in three milestone payments." Then stop talking. If silence extends beyond 10 seconds: "What are your initial thoughts?" Do not cave on price.

**20. Weekly status update discipline and Research Coverage Reports (DR4)**
During active searches, weekly written updates with RAG indicator, activity summary, pipeline by stage, market intelligence, client actions required. During sourcing, Clockwork's Research Coverage Reports showing which target companies you've mapped and which candidates identified. This converts invisible effort into visible progress and is what separates retained from contingency in the client's experience.

# 3. TWELVE-WEEK PRACTICE CALENDAR

**Constraints applied:** Maximum 4 hours per week. Sessions under 60 minutes. ADHD-friendly (concrete, single-task, clear "done" state). Runs alongside active BD — this is practice and preparation, not a substitute for being in market.

## WEEK 1: Prepare for the first research conversation

**Session 1 (45 min): Write your research conversation opening**
Draft the Phase 1 opening for your 20 research conversations. Use the template from DR2: "I'm a specialist headhunter focused exclusively on the intersection of AI and creative production. I'm mapping the leadership landscape in this space and your name keeps coming up as someone doing interesting work at [Company]. I'm not calling with a specific role — I'm conducting research into how AI leadership is evolving across the sector, and I'd value fifteen minutes of your perspective." Customise for your voice. Record yourself delivering it. Listen back. Revise until it sounds natural, not scripted. **Done = you can deliver the opening from memory without reading.**

**Session 2 (45 min): Build your first five target contacts**
Identify conversations 1-5 from DR2's sequencing: Director-level individuals at mid-tier studios or post-production houses where barriers are lowest. For each: full LinkedIn profile review, company context, one "insight gift" you can share. Document in your CRM/tracker. **Done = five names with prep notes and contact details ready for outreach.**

**Session 3 (45 min): Draft your three question tracks**
Write out your Perspective, Intelligence, and Referral questions from DR2's PIR framework. Perspective: "How is AI actually changing the creative leadership function at [Company]?" / "What does your AI team structure look like today versus two years ago?" Intelligence: "Where are the real skill gaps in AI leadership within VFX right now?" / "Which companies are furthest ahead?" Referrals: "Who else in the space is doing work you admire?" Practise asking them aloud. **Done = three question tracks written and practised.**

**Session 4 (45 min): Research conversation dry run**
Call a trusted contact from your existing network — someone who won't judge a rough first attempt. Run the full four-phase framework: opening, exploration using PIR questions, soft assessment, close with the market briefing offer. Debrief immediately: what felt natural, what was clunky, what questions generated the best responses. **Done = one live practice conversation completed with notes.**


## WEEK 2: Refine research conversation skills, start diagnostic question practice

**Session 1 (45 min): Run research conversations 2-3**
Apply lessons from Week 1's dry run. Focus on the 80/20 rule (they talk 80%). Track: conversation duration, referrals generated, intelligence items captured. **Done = two conversations completed with CRM entries and follow-up emails sent within 2 hours.**

**Session 2 (45 min): Practise Gionta's diagnostic questions**
Write out and rehearse: "What are the consequences of this position remaining open?" / "If there is no one in this role by [date], what is the downside?" / "Imagine you've hired this person. A year later, they've exceeded all expectations. What did they do?" Say these aloud 10 times each. Record yourself. Listen for tentative language. **Done = can ask all three questions naturally without notes.**

**Session 3 (45 min): Draft the retained pitch "recommend" statement**
Write your version of Whitby's recommendation: "For a role this critical — a first-time AI leadership hire, a niche skill set, a global talent pool of a few hundred people, and £Xm in projects dependent on getting this right — I'd recommend a dedicated search campaign." Practise delivering it as a confident recommendation, not a tentative suggestion. **Done = pitch recommendation statement memorised and deliverable.**

**Session 4 (45 min): Build contacts 6-10 for research conversations**
Identify your next five targets (VP-level, larger companies). Full prep as Week 1, Session 2. **Done = ten total contacts prepped and ready.**


## WEEK 3: Begin building SOPs and practise the advisory conversation structure

Include: contract signing checklist, intake workshop agenda (use DR4's 90-minute structure), Position Prospectus population process, Executive Scorecard creation. Use a checklist format you can physically tick off. **Done = Phase 1 SOP complete and saved in your working folder.**

**Session 2 (45 min): Run research conversations 4-6**
You're now in the "Intelligence deepening" tranche. Push for C-suite introductions. Reference patterns from earlier conversations: "I've been speaking with several AI leaders and a consistent theme is [X] — does that resonate?" **Done = three more conversations completed.**

**Session 3 (45 min): Practise the full advisory conversation four-phase structure**

Would it be useful to explore that further?" **Done = one full advisory conversation practised with debrief notes.**

**Session 4 (45 min): Write SOP for Phase 3 — Outreach and Qualification**

## WEEK 5: Practise fee language and begin market briefing

Practise delivering this as a natural part of conversation, not a presentation slide. **Done = cost narrative written and rehearsed.**

**Session 3 (45 min): Begin drafting the market briefing**
From your 7-9 completed research conversations, extract: recurring themes, compensation data points, role architecture patterns, talent supply observations, skills gaps. Draft the executive summary and emerging role archetypes sections. **Done = first two sections of market briefing drafted.**

**Session 4 (45 min): Run research conversations 10-12**

## WEEK 8: Full pitch simulation — round one

# 4. GAP ANALYSIS

## (a) Gaps that block pitching

**Gap A1: You don't know the actual fee sensitivity of mid-market creative production companies.**
The research establishes retained search norms (30-33% of total comp, 25-30% of base) and the theoretical minimum fee floor (£24,000). What it cannot tell you: will a 100-person VFX studio or a 200-person creative technology agency actually sign a £30,000-£50,000 retained engagement? The Gap Closure Research flags this directly: "What is the actual fee sensitivity at mid-market companies? Will a £15k-£30k retained fee for a Director-level search be acceptable to a 100-person VFX studio, or will they default to contingent recruitment or internal hiring?" Your 20 research conversations must include at least 3-5 conversations with people who have hiring authority at target companies (not just AI leaders who are potential candidates). Ask: "When you last made a senior hire, how did you go about it? Did you use a search firm? What did that look like commercially?"

**Gap A2: You don't know how these companies currently buy senior talent.**
The Gap Closure Research confirms near-zero retained search penetration for AI leadership in creative production. Most roles were filled through M&A, internal promotion, personal networks, or direct poaching. But the 20 research conversations need to establish: is this because companies actively choose not to use search firms (ideological resistance), or because no specialist firm has offered? The distinction determines whether your pitch needs to sell the retained model itself or just sell FPZ as the right firm for the model. Ask: "Have you ever worked with an executive search firm? What was the experience like? What would make you consider one for your next senior hire?"

**Gap A3: You don't have a live case study from a retained engagement.**
Every practitioner (Whitby, Taylor, Gionta) emphasises case studies. Whitby specifically says your case studies don't have to be from retained searches — past contingency placements reframed through a retained lens work. But you need to build at least one detailed case study structured as: (a) client's original business problem, (b) your search approach, (c) the commercial impact the placed candidate delivered. Pick your strongest contingency placement at Director+ level in creative tech and write this before your first pitch meeting. This is partially addressed in the Operating Manual (Day 4, Asset 2) but hasn't been done.

**Gap A4: You don't have a solicitor-reviewed agreement.**
The Operating Manual flags this as Gap 6. You cannot close a retained engagement without legally sound terms. The 12-month replacement guarantee conditional on client onboarding compliance is a non-standard clause that needs professional drafting. The self-sourced hire clause (50-75% of fee payable if the client fills the role through their own efforts during the engagement) is another clause that needs legal review. This blocks signing, not pitching — but you need it ready before the first serious prospect conversation reaches the proposal stage.


## (b) Gaps that block delivery

**Gap B1: You have no tested intake workshop process.**
DR4 provides the 90-minute structure. The Operating Manual identifies this as Gap 12. But running a 60-90 minute stakeholder workshop with 2-4 senior people, extracting KPOs, mapping competencies, and calibrating the search — this is a facilitation skill you haven't practised in a retained context. Your research conversations will give you some of this muscle memory, but the workshop format (multiple stakeholders with potentially conflicting views, time-pressured, high-stakes) is different from a one-on-one conversation. Practise this before the first mandate, not during it.

**Gap B2: You have no reference checking framework for executive-level candidates.**
The Operating Manual flags this as Gap 13. DR4 mentions 360-degree referencing as a retained differentiator but no template or methodology exists in any of the four research outputs. Executive reference checking is fundamentally different from contingency references — you're calling peers, board members, and direct reports of people who are often still employed and don't want their job search known. You need: a structured reference questionnaire, a protocol for approaching referees discreetly, and language for requesting references from candidates who haven't yet resigned. Build this before the first shortlist delivery.

**Gap B3: You don't know the actual compensation ranges from primary data.**
The Gap Closure Research and Market Sizing Validation provide estimates (Director: $113K-$183K; VP: $150K-$250K; C-suite: $200K-$400K+). But these are drawn from job postings and indirect sources. Your 20 research conversations must systematically gather real compensation data: "Without needing specific numbers, could you give me a sense of how compensation is structured for someone at your level — is it primarily base, or is there significant equity/bonus?" Aggregate this data into your market briefing. Without primary compensation data, you cannot credibly advise clients on how to structure competitive offers — and that advisory capability is central to why they pay retained fees.

**Gap B4: You haven't defined the sector-specific competency framework.**
Operating Manual Gap 9. The Executive Scorecard template needs sector-specific competencies for AI leadership in creative production. What does "translating creative vision to technical execution" actually mean at a VFX studio versus a creative technology agency versus an experiential company? Your 20 research conversations should explicitly explore this: "What competencies separate the best AI leaders in this space from the mediocre ones?" Build the competency framework from real market language, not internal assumptions.

**Gap B5: You have no candidate-side positioning.**
Operating Manual Gap 7. The entire pitch apparatus is client-facing. The moment a retained mandate is signed, you need to approach passive senior candidates as a retained search partner — not the contingency recruiter they may associate with your existing brand. You need: a candidate value proposition explaining why a retained process benefits them (confidentiality, thorough briefing, structured assessment, single point of contact, career advisory relationship), and candidate-facing outreach language equivalent to the CXO messaging. Your 20 research conversations are partly solving this — you're already positioning as a market intelligence exchange, not a recruitment pitch. Formalise that into a repeatable candidate engagement framework.


## (c) Gaps that reduce credibility but don't block

**Gap C1: No published compensation benchmark or market intelligence report.**
The "State of AI Leadership in Creative Production" annual benchmark is the single highest-leverage BD investment (DR1, DR2, Gap Closure Research, Artico Search template). It doesn't exist yet. Without it, you can still pitch and deliver — your market briefing from the 20 conversations is a credible interim asset. But the published report is what separates you from every other boutique that claims niche expertise. This is a months 3-6 investment, not a pre-launch requirement.

**Gap C2: No AESC alignment or professional body membership.**
DR4 notes AESC membership isn't required but "aligning with their standards provides credibility, particularly with clients who have used larger retained firms." For mid-market CXOs making first-time AI leadership hires, this matters less — they've never heard of the AESC. But it's worth understanding AESC's six pillars and referencing them in your methodology description.

**Gap C3: No LinkedIn thought leadership track record.**
DR1 and DR2 both emphasise LinkedIn content as a credibility signal. C-suite who post get ~600% more reach. 1% of LinkedIn members post weekly. You don't have an established content cadence. This is a medium-term credibility builder — start posting 2-3 times per week during the 12-week calendar, but don't wait until you have a content track record before pitching.

**Gap C4: No psychometric assessment capability.**
The service matrix lists psychometric candidate testing as a retained-tier deliverable. No specific tool, provider, or certification is named in any source. This is a "nice to have" for the first mandate — you can omit it from the first engagement's scope and add it later. But long-term, having a credible assessment tool (e.g., Hogan, SHL, or the i-intro behavioural assessment) strengthens the retained proposition.

**Gap C5: No GDPR-compliant privacy infrastructure.**
DR2 covers GDPR compliance for candidate outreach (Legitimate Interest basis, LIA assessment, Article 14 notification within 30 days). The Operating Manual flags this as Gap 16. You're already processing candidate data through contingency work, so this isn't a new obligation — but formalising it (privacy notice on website, data retention policy, CRM configured for GDPR compliance) removes a latent risk and signals professionalism to UK enterprise clients.

**Gap C6: No financial management system for milestone invoicing.**
Operating Manual Gap 15. Retained search requires three milestone invoices per engagement. You need: an invoicing tool configured for milestone billing, a cash flow tracker, and potentially a separate business bank account for retained fee deposits. Not complex, but needs setting up before the first retainer payment arrives.

## WHAT THE 20 RESEARCH CONVERSATIONS MUST ANSWER

The published research can tell you about retained search methodology, fee norms, objection handling, and market sizing. It cannot tell you six things that only primary conversations with real people in your target market will reveal:

**1. How do target companies actually make senior hiring decisions today?**
Is it the CEO who decides unilaterally? A CTO with budget authority? An HR function that gatekeeps? A board that must approve above a salary threshold? This determines who you pitch to and how.

**2. What is the real compensation structure for AI leadership in creative production?**
Not job posting ranges (which are often aspirational). Actual base, bonus, equity, and benefits packages for sitting Director/VP/C-suite AI leaders. This data populates your market briefing and makes you indispensable to clients who have no external benchmark.

**3. What would make a mid-market CXO pay £30K-£50K for a retained search?**
Is it speed? Is it access to a talent pool they can't reach? Is it the guarantee? Is it the advisory relationship? The answer determines which WIIFM item you lead with and how you structure the pitch.

**4. What are the actual career motivators for senior AI leaders in creative production?**
Is it compensation? Creative freedom? Team building opportunity? Technical challenge? Company mission? The answer determines how you position opportunities when you have a live mandate, and it's the intelligence clients can't get from LinkedIn.

**5. Where are the genuine skills gaps that create urgency for external hiring?**
If every company can promote their CTO to cover AI, there's no retained search market. If there are specific competencies (neural rendering, generative pipeline architecture, creative-technical bridge leadership) that cannot be developed internally, that scarcity is your market.

**6. What is the actual recruiter penetration rate — and why?**
The Gap Closure Research estimates 5-15% retained search penetration. But why? Is it because companies actively avoid search firms? Because no specialist firm has offered? Because the roles are filled before a search is needed? Because the fees are perceived as too high for mid-market budgets? The answer shapes your entire go-to-market.

These six questions should be woven into every research conversation alongside the candidate-focused PIR framework. You're building a dual-use intelligence base: candidate network for future mandates, and market intelligence for your pitch.
