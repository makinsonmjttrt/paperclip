# Retained search fee negotiation: a practitioner playbook for solo boutique founders

**The single most important principle in retained search fee negotiation is that the fee conversation is won or lost before you ever state a number.** Every practitioner researched — Greg Savage, Mike Gionta, Jordan Taylor, James O'Brien — converges on the same conclusion: the diagnostic phase, not the pitch phase, determines whether a client pays full retained fees. Your fee is justified by the depth of the problem you've uncovered, not by the quality of your closing technique. For a solo founder entering retained search with deep niche expertise and zero retained mandates, this is liberating: your 300+ placements and knowledge of a 200–500-person global talent pool are more powerful fee-justification tools than any script.

What follows extracts the specific techniques, frameworks, and language that practitioners use to position, defend, and close retained fees — organised around the five sections requested, with contradictions flagged and recommendations tailored to a solo boutique founder operating at 25–30% of first-year base salary with a £24,000 floor.

## Section 1: How to position and anchor the fee so clients never question the number

### When and how to introduce the fee

Every practitioner agrees: **never lead with the fee**. The recommended sequence, drawn from Retrained Search (Jordan Taylor and Louise Archer) and reinforced by Greg Savage's Savage Recruitment Academy, follows a diagnostic model:

1. **Context/Diagnosis** — Ask about the business, the role, what they've tried, what's failed. Jordan Taylor frames this as a doctor's appointment: "Your doctor will always seek to fully understand the situation before suggesting treatment." Mike Gionta (RecruiterU) teaches a similar diagnostic: "When was the last time you had an opening? What was that process like? Can you afford to wait six months this time? What is the cost of the position remaining open?"

2. **Value/Segue** — Empathise with the difficulty. Reassure with credibility. Ask permission to share your approach: "I'd love to share my thoughts on how I'd like to approach it — is that OK?" (Retrained Search framework). This is where you establish that only ~200–500 people globally hold the intersection of ML/AI expertise and creative production leadership, and that contingency firms cannot access them.

3. **Recommend the model** — Explain why retained is the appropriate methodology for *this specific search*, not as a policy but as a diagnosis. Taylor: "For everyday, easy-to-fill hires we offer a contingent service. When things start to get more critical — niche skillset, senior, confidential, strategic — we apply a search methodology."

4. **Pre-close** — "I'd really like to show you how effective this way of working is. Can I show you on this project?" Handle any objections about approach *before* stating a number.

5. **State the fee** — Only now. Use "on commencement of project," never "upfront."

### The anchoring number: 28.6%, not 25% or 30%

Mike Gionta's signature technique is the most specific anchoring method publicly documented. He recommends quoting **28.6%** rather than a round number. His rationale: "There is tremendous magic in the number 28.6% because the hiring manager will often ask, 'Where did you come up with that number?' I could honestly say we have done time and motion studies. For us to execute our process, reach out to a list of 60 to 80 candidates, up to seven times each, factoring in the likelihood of success and failure, the fee is 28.6%." The odd number signals precision and data-backed methodology. Clients negotiate less aggressively against calculated numbers than round ones. Gionta reports the bandwidth of counter-offers narrowed significantly compared to quoting 25% or 30%.

**Recommendation for FPZ CreativAI:** Anchor at 28–30% and present it as a calculated figure tied to the cost of executing a 90-day dedicated search campaign in a talent pool of ~300 people. If your target base salaries sit at £100,000–£200,000, a 28% fee generates £28,000–£56,000 — comfortably above your £24,000 floor and within market norms.

### Eliminating tentative language

Greg Savage identifies **tentative language as the single fastest way to destroy fee credibility**. Words that signal your price is negotiable and must be eliminated permanently:

"Normally," "approximately," "usually," "I feel that," "sometimes," "our average price," "ballpark figure," "maybe," "probably," and "I will check with my manager." Savage: "When you say 'normally,' the client knows at once that your price is negotiable. The battle is lost." A commenter on his blog, endorsed by Savage, adds a critical refinement: never say "these are our *standard* terms" — say **"these are our terms."** The word "standard" implies other terms exist.

The correct approach is to state a specific, definitive figure. Savage: "When a recruiter says, 'For this job, we charge $20,000, and we deliver an outcome in one week or less,' your client is instantly impressed." For retained search, this translates to: **"The project fee for this search is £45,000, structured in three milestone payments, and backed by a 12-month replacement guarantee."** No hedging, no qualification.

### Percentage versus fixed fee: when each is stronger

**Percentage-based fees** work best when compensation is clearly defined, the role sits within established market ranges, and clients expect it. This is the industry default; the AESC standard and virtually all retained firms use it.

**Fixed fees** work best when compensation is uncertain or variable, when you want to eliminate the conflict-of-interest perception (the firm has no incentive to push candidate compensation higher), or when salary might calculate below your minimum fee floor. Egon Zehnder, the world's most prestigious search firm, uses fixed fees exclusively. Their rationale: "Our only incentive is to deliver what's best for you."

**For FPZ CreativAI's specific situation**, a hybrid approach is strongest. Quote a percentage (28–30%) but with a minimum fee floor of £24,000. This gives you the upside when salaries are high and protects your economics when they're lower. For roles where the compensation range is genuinely uncertain — common for first-time AI leadership hires at mid-market companies — offer to fix the fee at the estimated percentage-based level, noting that if final compensation significantly exceeds the discussed range, you'll reconcile at close.

### How specialist boutiques justify premium pricing

Three data-driven arguments support premium positioning:

**Fewer client conflicts.** Large firms maintain extensive off-limits lists. Wilton & Bain's analysis confirms: "SHREK firms often face client conflicts due to their extensive client portfolios — restricted from reaching out to the best candidates because those candidates work for other clients." A boutique with a narrow niche has a shorter off-limits list and wider candidate access.

**Partner-led engagement throughout.** HRM Outlook's research found boutique specialist firms "provide more tailored, personal service with fewer client conflicts, delivering better quality candidates faster, with higher retention rates." At FPZ CreativAI, the founder is the consultant — there's no handoff to juniors.

**Depth of niche knowledge.** Christian & Timbers notes that firms specialising in AI leadership "integrate machine learning systems into their candidate analytics" and command premiums. For FPZ CreativAI, the value proposition is knowing the 200–500 people globally who sit at the intersection of AI/ML and creative production — a market no generalist firm can map.

### The "investment not cost" reframe

Practitioners deploy this reframe through a specific sequence, not as a standalone phrase. Ken Yarmosh (pricing consultant) summarises the method: "Walk through the problem in detail. Quantify the cost of inaction. Illustrate what success looks like. Connect outcomes to their revenue or efficiency goals. Only then introduce pricing — by that point, the number feels justified." Mike Gionta teaches recruiters to replace the word "fee" with "investment" in every client conversation. Savage's version is outcome-focused: "If clients could hire the great people they need independently, they would. But they can't. They need us. So, we deliver crucial outcomes. There is a fee for that."

The practical deployment for FPZ CreativAI: after diagnosing the client's need, quantify the cost of vacancy (for a $350K total-comp AI leader, a conservative 10% productivity impact on a $50M pipeline equals ~$100K per month of vacancy) and the cost of a bad hire (3–5× annual salary per Harvard Business School research, or $1M–$1.75M). Your £45,000–£60,000 fee is then 3–6% of the cost of getting it wrong. Present this calculation explicitly.

## Section 2: Practitioner-tested responses to the six core objections

### (a) "You're too expensive / our budget is lower"

**The wrong response:** Immediately offering a discount ("I could come down to X%"), apologising for the fee, or saying "that's our standard rate" without explanation. Savage: "If you say 'my fee is 20%' and the client asks for a discount and you say 'OK, 18%,' you have just signalled to the client that you never believed in your value proposition."

**The underlying concern:** "I don't yet see enough value to justify this spend" or "I need help making the business case internally."

**The right response (Greg Savage's four-step framework):**

Step 1 — Don't talk about the fee; talk about what the fee is for. Walk through your entire search process: market mapping, targeted headhunting of 60–80 candidates, assessment methodology, compensation benchmarking, offer management. Savage calls this the iceberg analogy: "10% of an iceberg is visible above the waterline. 90% is out of sight. So it is with what clients see of our work."

Step 2 — Put the ball in the client's court. "Then, and only then, ask the client why they feel a reduced fee is appropriate. The client is asking for a discount. They should be squirming, not you." Savage's exact script: **"Why do you think this placement warrants a discounted fee?"** A practitioner who used this line reported: "Needless to say, the full fee was paid with no further issue."

Step 3 — If the client mentions competitors' lower fees, deploy the killer question: **"Can you tell me about a situation where you were charged less than my fee, where you got the level of service and calibre of talent you want — on a regular basis?"**

Step 4 — If you must reduce, always extract a concession: exclusivity, a retainer, multiple assignments, client-paid advertising, or waiving the guarantee period. Savage: "Make sure the negotiation involves both sides giving."

Gionta adds a diagnostic reframe: **"If I agreed to fill this position for free and did not give you any suitable candidates, tell me how much money I have saved you."**

### (b) "We'll just go contingent instead"

**The wrong response:** "OK, I can work on contingency too" or badmouthing contingency firms.

**The underlying concern:** "I don't want to pay upfront for something uncertain" or "We've used contingency before and it worked."

**The right response:** Acknowledge contingency has its place, then explain the structural problem for *this specific search*. Three practitioner frameworks:

**Gionta's accountant analogy:** "Do you bring in three auditors and say whoever comes up with the best audit is the one we'll pay? No. Do you bring in multiple attorneys and say whoever wins the case is the one we'll pay? No. Yet you're doing it with recruiters."

**Completion rate data:** Retained searches complete at **95%+** versus roughly **20–35%** for contingency (PrincePerelson, Sales Talent Inc). For a role this critical, the client needs to decide which odds they're comfortable with.

**Brand risk argument (Redline Executive):** "Multiple agencies contacting the same candidates can create brand confusion — sometimes resulting in conflicting messages. This damages your employer brand." In a tight-knit community like VFX and virtual production, where everyone knows everyone, this is especially damaging.

**Jordan Taylor's vetting technique:** "Always sell retained down" — not to win retainers, but to test seriousness. If the client says no and explains they don't think it's a serious enough search for a retainer, you've learned the search isn't real. Taylor: "It's not contingent that's the problem. It's the fact that the client isn't really serious about the search."

### (c) "Other firms charge less / we've had quotes at 20%"

**The wrong response:** Matching the competitor's fee, panicking, or disparaging competitors by name.

**The underlying concern:** "I want to make sure I'm not overpaying" or "I need to justify this expenditure." Often a negotiation tactic rather than a genuine comparison.

**The right response:** Challenge the comparison, then redirect to outcomes.

**Savage's killer question** (repeated because it is the single most effective line in retained search fee defence): "Can you tell me about a situation where you were charged less than my fee, where you got the level of service and calibre of talent you want — on a regular basis?"

**The logic test:** "If those firms could actually deliver at 20%, why are you still talking to me?"

**The priority economics argument:** "If a firm has another client paying 30% and they're working your search at 20%, where does their focus lie when they have a candidate suitable for both?"

**The scope differentiation:** "At 20%, those firms are running a contingency-style process under a different name. My fee funds a dedicated 90-day research campaign, original market mapping, executive-level assessment, and a 12-month replacement guarantee."

A practitioner on Savage's blog contributed this technique: **"I countered by querying which part of the recruitment process the client would like me to leave out in return for the reduced charge. It was quickly established every part was essential."**

### (d) "Can you do it for a flat fee?"

**The wrong response:** Reflexively saying no, or agreeing to a flat fee that undervalues the search.

**The underlying concern:** Budget certainty, procurement system requirements, or a backdoor discount attempt.

**The right response:** Understand the motivation first. "I'm open to discussing that. Help me understand what's driving the preference — is it budget certainty, or a concern about the percentage model?" If it's genuine budget certainty, structure a flat fee at approximately what the percentage-based fee would generate (or slightly higher to account for compensation uplift risk). Maintain the staged payment structure. If it's a discount tactic, include a reconciliation clause: "If final compensation exceeds the range we've discussed, the fee reconciles at 28% at close per the engagement letter."

**Contradiction worth noting:** Egon Zehnder uses fixed fees and positions this as client-aligned. Some boutique founders (The Good Search) do the same. Most retained practitioners, including Savage and Gionta, work on percentages. **Recommendation for solo boutique founder:** Use percentage as default with a minimum fee floor. Offer flat-fee structuring when the client requests it, pricing it at the percentage equivalent or above. Never let a flat fee become a backdoor discount.

### (e) "We want to pay the full fee on completion, not in stages"

**The wrong response:** Agreeing entirely (converts retained to contingency) or saying "we can't do that" with no explanation.

**The underlying concern:** "I don't want to pay for work and get nothing in return" or "What if the search fails and I've already paid two-thirds?"

**The right response:** Explain that staged payments protect the *client*, not just the firm. "Your upfront investment ensures this search is my absolute priority. If you're paying nothing until completion, I'm economically incentivised to work the same way contingency firms do — volume over depth. That's exactly what you told me hasn't worked." Tie each payment to a tangible deliverable: "Each payment coincides with a specific deliverable you receive — the search strategy and market map, the calibrated shortlist, and the placed candidate."

If the client won't accept full thirds, offer a modified structure: 20% on engagement, 30% at shortlist, 50% on placement. This loads more toward completion whilst preserving the commitment signal. Always include the 12-month replacement guarantee as the risk-reversal mechanism.

### (f) "We need to get procurement / finance approval at that level"

**The wrong response:** "OK, let me know when you hear back" (loses all momentum) or lowering the fee pre-emptively.

**The underlying concern:** Genuinely procedural, a soft stall, or the hiring manager needs help building the internal business case.

**The right response:** Validate and equip. "That's completely standard at this level of investment. Who specifically needs to approve?" Then arm your champion with a one-page business case: cost-of-vacancy calculation for this specific role, cost-of-bad-hire data (3–5× annual salary), retained versus contingency completion rates, your niche differentiation, staged payment structure tied to deliverables, and the replacement guarantee. Offer to present directly: "Would it help if I joined a 15-minute call with your procurement lead?" Reframe in procurement language: "This isn't a recruitment fee — this is a professional services engagement with defined deliverables, milestones, KPIs, and a performance guarantee."

Create urgency without pressure: "While we navigate the approval process, I'd suggest we align on the search specification so we don't lose time."

## Section 3: Milestone payments, guarantees, and the commercial architecture of retained search

### The thirds model and its alternatives

The **one-third/one-third/one-third** model is the dominant industry standard. The AESC and virtually all major retained firms use it. Payments typically fall on engagement, shortlist delivery, and offer acceptance (or candidate start date). Some firms use date-based billing (start, 30 days, 60 days) rather than milestones, which means two-thirds of the fee is billed before most searches conclude.

**Earned versus at-risk fee analysis across models:**

| Model | Guaranteed at start | Earned at shortlist | At-risk until placement | Firm advantage |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|----------------------|----------------|
| Thirds (milestone) | 33% | 33% | 33% | Balanced |
| Thirds (date-based) | 33% | 33% (at 30 days) | 33% (at 60 days) | Strong firm advantage |
| 50/25/25 | 50% | 25% | 25% | Strong firm advantage |
| 40/30/30 | 40% | 30% | 30% | Moderate firm advantage |
| 20/30/50 | 20% | 30% | 50% | Client advantage |
| Container (deposit + success) | 10–25% | — | 75–90% | Strong client advantage |

**Recommendation:** The milestone-based thirds model is the right default for FPZ CreativAI. It is universally understood, easy to explain, and ties payments to tangible deliverables. For clients who resist, the 20/30/50 modified structure preserves commitment whilst loading toward outcomes.

### Cancellation terms and kill fees

**Industry standard:** The first instalment is almost universally non-refundable. It compensates for strategy development, market mapping, and research already completed. If the shortlist has been delivered, the second instalment is also non-refundable. River Partnership (UK) charges a cancellation fee of 25% of the Remuneration if the search is cancelled before completion. Some firms simply retain all payments made to date as the kill fee.

**Key contract elements:** Clear definition of cancellation versus postponement; non-refundable status of instalments already paid; any credit toward future searches; client obligation if they fill the role through other means; mutual termination rights with notice period (typically 30 days).

**Recommendation:** Make the first third non-refundable and specify this explicitly. If the search is cancelled after shortlist delivery, the second third is also retained. Include a clause covering self-sourced hires: if the client fills the role through their own efforts during the engagement period, **50–75% of the total fee** remains payable, reflecting the work already invested.

### Replacement guarantees: market norms and competitive positioning

The Top Echelon survey provides the clearest industry data: **44.9%** of firms offer 90-day guarantees, 20.3% offer 30-day, and 20.0% offer 60-day. Only ~2.5% offer 12-month guarantees. However, this data includes contingency firms. For retained executive search specifically at senior levels, **6–12 months is the norm**. JRG Partners calls 12 months "the industry gold standard" for C-suite. TalentRise states retained firms "usually offer a 12-month replacement guarantee."

James O'Brien (i-intro) provides the statistical backing: **96% of candidates placed through his retained methodology are still in post after 12 months; 93% after two years.** This means statistically only 1 in 25 searches will require a replacement — making a 12-month guarantee commercially viable and a powerful differentiator.

**FPZ CreativAI's 12-month guarantee conditional on client onboarding compliance** is positioned at the premium end of the market. The conditionality is critical. Standard conditions that should void the guarantee: role materially changes, position eliminated through restructuring, client fails to provide agreed onboarding support, client doesn't uphold agreed employment terms, invoices unpaid within 10 days. O'Brien's data suggests this guarantee costs virtually nothing whilst dramatically reducing client risk perception.

**UK versus US differences:** UK firms more commonly use clawback/sliding-scale refunds (100% in month one, declining monthly). US firms more commonly offer replacement searches rather than cash refunds. A Top Echelon survey found **61.4%** of firms offer replacement with no refund, 17.6% offer pro-rated refunds, and only 8.4% offer full money-back guarantees. The replacement model is recommended for FPZ CreativAI — it preserves cash flow and keeps the client relationship active.

### The container model as a bridge

The container (or engaged) model charges a smaller upfront engagement fee — typically **10–33% of the total fee** — with the balance contingent on successful placement. It functions as a hybrid between retained and contingency. Typical upfront amounts range from £1,500 (Mattinson Partnership, UK) to $5,000–$8,000 (Y Scouts, The Good Search, US).

**When to offer container:** Building trust with a new client who has never used retained; VP or Director-level roles (not C-suite); salary range £80K–£150K where total fee is £20,000–£45,000; when you need early case studies to build retained credibility.

**When to hold firm on retained:** C-suite or board appointments; confidential replacement searches; highly specialised roles in your core niche; total fee exceeds £30,000; established client relationships.

**FPZ CreativAI's container structure (£3,000–£5,000 upfront plus four-week exclusivity)** is priced at the low end of market. Consider increasing to £5,000–£8,000 to signal commitment without reaching full retained levels. Frame it as a bridge: "I'd recommend we start with our engaged search model — a commitment fee of £5,000 on commencement which guarantees dedicated resources and exclusive focus, with the balance payable on successful placement. This demonstrates mutual commitment while I prove the value of our approach on this first search together."

### UK versus US payment and fee differences

**Fee basis:** US firms typically calculate fees on **total first-year cash compensation** (base + bonus + sign-on). UK firms more often use **base salary only**. Two firms both quoting "30%" can generate significantly different absolute fees depending on basis. Clarify this explicitly in engagement letters.

**Fee ranges:** US retained search runs 25–35% of total compensation, with 30–33% the most cited benchmark. UK retained search runs 25–33%, more commonly on base salary. C-suite in the US can reach 40–50% at top-tier firms. FPZ CreativAI's 25–30% of base salary is conservative relative to the industry standard of 30–33% of total compensation.

## Section 4: Negotiation tactics, red lines, and when to walk away

### Handling percentage negotiation downward

When a client agrees to retained but wants to negotiate the percentage down, the principle from every practitioner is the same: **never reduce without extracting a concession**. Savage: "If you must reduce, always get something in return — exclusivity, a retainer, multiple orders, waiving the guarantee." Gionta offers a specific structure: "Instead of 25% for one search, your fee is 22% for three exclusive searches."

An alternative to discounting the percentage: **reduce scope rather than rate**. Ken Yarmosh: "Never lower your rate without changing what's included. Discounting with the same deliverables trains clients to negotiate every time." Remove the 12-month guarantee, reduce to 6-month. Remove the compensation benchmarking report. Remove the market map deliverable. Then ask: "Which elements would you like to retain?"

**The maths of discounting (GSJ Consulting):** "A 10% discount seems harmless. But when the profit margin at most firms is circa 30%, a 10% discount is the equivalent of slashing your profits by one-third. You'll need to increase sales volume by 50% just to break even."

### Holding the £24,000 minimum fee floor

AESC-affiliated firms operate with non-negotiable minimums below which they will not accept assignments. Large retained firms set minimums at $80,000–$100,000 (Hunter Recruiting data). A £24,000 floor is modest by retained search standards.

**The script:** "Our minimum project fee for retained search is £24,000. This reflects the investment of senior partner time, dedicated research, comprehensive market mapping, and our 12-month guarantee — regardless of salary level. The work involved in a thorough search doesn't scale linearly with compensation. A Head of AI at £80K base requires the same rigorous methodology as one at £120K."

**Alternative approach:** Quote a fixed fee for lower-salary searches rather than a percentage. If the base salary is £75,000, a 30% fee generates only £22,500 — below your floor. Instead, quote £24,000 as the project fee. This avoids the awkward conversation about percentages exceeding 30% and positions the fee as a fixed professional services engagement.

### When to walk away

Practitioners converge on specific red lines:

- The client asks to work contingency first "to see if you're any good" — signals they don't understand or value the retained model
- Repeatedly asking for discounts before understanding the service
- No genuine urgency around the hire — if they're not feeling pain, retained commitment is unlikely
- Multiple agencies already working on it in a race format
- Budget not approved — they're shopping, not buying
- Won't commit to exclusivity — fundamental misalignment with retained methodology
- Procurement-led process with no hiring manager buy-in

Savage's advice on walking away: "Be prepared to walk away if the fee suggested does not match your expertise and ability to deliver. Always leave the door open. You will be amazed at how often they come back."

### Procurement versus hiring manager negotiations

**With procurement teams**, the dynamic shifts fundamentally. Beroe (procurement intelligence) notes that buyers conduct "services cost analysis to derive the optimal service fee threshold" and attempt to decompose the fee into hourly billing components. The counter-strategy: maintain one point of contact, refuse to commoditise the service, and reframe in procurement language — deliverables, milestones, SLAs, and guarantees rather than hourly rates.

**With hiring managers and CXOs**, the conversation is naturally more collaborative and outcome-focused. Senior buyers care about quality, speed, confidentiality, and cultural fit. They understand the cost of failure. The fee discussion integrates naturally into the strategic conversation.

**Critical insight from Gionta:** "The last thing you want is for them to take your idea and present it to their CFO because it will die 80% of the time. They are not recruiters. They will not take their boss through the same process you did." Always ask: **"Are you authorised to approve that, or do you and I need to have this conversation with someone else?"**

### The psychology of fee conversations at CXO level

**Confidence signals:** Hold firm on fee without apologising. Use silence comfortably. Demonstrate you don't need this deal. Lead with diagnostic questions, not pitch. Speak concisely. Present standardised structures (signals established practice).

**Desperation signals:** Offering discounts before being asked. Over-explaining or justifying the fee immediately. Customising everything (makes everything feel negotiable). Apologising for the rate.

**Why CXOs respect higher fees:** James O'Brien learned this lesson directly from a Danish client who told him his 20% fee was too low. The client said: **"We want to know that you're motivated to do the work. Everybody else is charging 33% plus, and they're doing equal thirds."** O'Brien now references the Stella Artois principle: "Reassuringly expensive." Low fees signal low commitment and low quality to sophisticated buyers.

Savage reinforces the equal-partnership principle: "Your relationship with your client is one of equality. It is not a 'master/servant' relationship. Through your credibility, your communication skills and your self-belief you impose on your client a process which you know will benefit them. If you are subservient, apologetic or lacking in confidence, the client automatically takes control and you will be lost."

### Using silence after stating the fee

MIT Sloan research (Jared Curhan et al., "Silence is Golden," *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2022) demonstrated that **3-second pauses** in negotiations shift negotiators into a more reflective, deliberative state. Naturally occurring silences consistently preceded breakthroughs.

Chris Voss (former FBI lead hostage negotiator) teaches **dynamic silence**: "After any great question or great label, you've got to go silent. Let them answer. Be cool. 90% of the time they will." Ken Yarmosh puts it bluntly: "State your rate, then stop. Let the client respond first. The discomfort you feel is the same discomfort they're processing. **Whoever speaks first usually concedes.** I've watched leads agree to rates I thought were too high — simply because I stayed quiet long enough."

Savage doesn't teach this technique explicitly but his guidance aligns: "Don't feel pressured or emotional. It's a purely commercial decision." His "10 seconds of courage" concept — "Just enough to start. Once you begin, you're in the conversation. And once you're in, you're almost always fine" — applies directly to sitting with silence after stating your fee.

**If silence extends beyond 10 seconds**, re-engage gently with: "What are your initial thoughts?" Do not cave on price.

## Section 5: The fee conversation playbook from opening to close

### The complete sequence

**Phase 1 — Diagnostic (15–30 minutes).** Ask about the role, the business context, what they've tried, what's failed, what's at stake. Quantify the cost of vacancy and the cost of a bad hire. Gionta's diagnostic questions: "What is the cost of the position remaining open? If there is no one in this role by [date], what is the downside to the company? What work is not getting done?" Have them monetise the impact. This creates the value foundation that makes your fee seem rational.

**Phase 2 — Credibility and differentiation (5–10 minutes).** Establish who you are, who you serve, and what makes your approach different. For FPZ CreativAI: "I work exclusively in senior AI leadership for creative production — VFX, virtual production, experiential, spatial computing. I know the 200–300 people globally who do this work at a senior level. Most of them take my calls." Use a STAR-method case study if available.

**Phase 3 — Recommendation (3–5 minutes).** Recommend retained as the appropriate model for this search, grounded in the diagnosis. Jordan Taylor's framework: position it as a diagnosis, not a sales pitch. "Based on what you've described — a first-time AI leadership hire, a niche skill set, a global talent pool of a few hundred people, and $Xm in projects dependent on getting this right — I'd recommend a dedicated search campaign."

**Phase 4 — Fee statement (30 seconds).** State the number definitively. No tentative language. "The project fee is £45,000, structured in three milestone payments — one-third on engagement, one-third on shortlist delivery, one-third on accepted offer. The engagement includes a 12-month replacement guarantee." Then stop talking. Wait.

**Phase 5 — Objection handling (as needed).** Use the frameworks in Section 2. Remember Gionta's principle: "You do not overcome fee objections through rebuttals. You uncover them by setting up the process to win in the first place."

**Phase 6 — Close.** Assumptive: "When can we schedule the kickoff meeting?" or "Shall I send over the engagement letter?" If the client needs time: "Absolutely. Can I ask — is there a specific element you'd like to reflect on? Is it the approach, the investment, or the timing?" Set a specific follow-up date. Create urgency without pressure.

### Decision tree for months 1–3 of a new retained practice

**Should you discount to win first mandates?** The practitioners split here. Consulting Success recommends making any first-time discount explicit: "List your official price and exactly how much you are taking off. Otherwise, clients will use that price as the going rate." GSJ Consulting warns: "Discounting fills a gap created by a weak value proposition." Greg Savage is unequivocal: a discounted fee means a discounted you.

**Recommendation for FPZ CreativAI:** Do not discount fees. Instead, **reduce client risk through engagement structure.** For your first 2–3 mandates, offer the container/engaged model (£5,000–£8,000 upfront plus balance on success) at your full 28–30% rate. This gives clients low-risk entry whilst preserving your fee integrity. Frame it explicitly as a bridge: "For our first project together, I'd recommend starting with our engaged search model. Once you've seen the quality of execution, we'll move to our standard retained structure." This builds case studies without training the market to expect discounts.

**How to build credibility without retained track record:** Lead with expertise, not firm history. Your knowledge of the AI leadership market, your network of 300+ placed candidates, and your ability to map a talent pool of 200–500 people are more compelling than "years of retained search experience." Publish thought leadership on AI talent trends. Offer a free "AI Leadership Talent Market Briefing" to demonstrate depth before asking for commitment. Use candidate testimonials and placement outcomes from contingency work as proxy credibility.

### Red flags that indicate a client will never pay retained

The clearest signals, synthesised across practitioners: asking to "try contingency first"; no urgency or pain around the vacancy; budget not yet approved; multiple agencies already racing; refusal to commit to exclusivity; procurement-driven process with no hiring manager sponsorship; and asking for discounts before understanding the service. When you see two or more of these signals simultaneously, invest no further time. Politely offer to stay in touch and move on. Jordan Taylor's "always sell retained down" technique is the most efficient diagnostic tool here — if the client reveals they "just want you to keep an eye out," the search isn't real, regardless of how they frame it.

---

## Conclusion: three principles that matter more than any script

First, **the fee is earned in the diagnostic, not in the close**. Every practitioner — Savage, Gionta, Taylor, O'Brien — builds fee acceptance through the quality of the discovery conversation. If you've properly uncovered the client's pain, quantified the cost of vacancy and the cost of failure, and demonstrated that you know a finite global talent pool better than anyone, the fee becomes a logical conclusion rather than a negotiation.

Second, **confidence is the fee's best friend**. O'Brien's Danish client rejected his 20% fee as too low. Savage's entire methodology rests on believing you are the expert, not the vendor. For a solo founder with zero retained mandates, this confidence must come from niche expertise — the fact that no other search firm owns AI leadership in creative production — rather than from retained track record. The track record will follow.

Third, **structure reduces risk better than discounts**. The container model, milestone-based payments, and a 12-month replacement guarantee all lower the client's perceived risk without eroding your fee. In months 1–3, use these structural tools liberally. Hold the fee. Hold the floor. Let the work speak for itself. Savage: "Be prepared to walk away. Always leave the door open. You will be amazed at how often they come back."
