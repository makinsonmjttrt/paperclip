## Role landscape

The commercially useful reality is that “AI leadership” in creative production is being hired under **three distinct title families**, and your exec practice will win or lose based on whether it can search across all three rather than only chasing obvious “Head of AI” labels: **AI-in-production leadership (pipelines/workflows), AI-in-product leadership (creative software/platforms), and AI-in-operating-model leadership (AI labs, governance, transformation).** This is visible in the current mix of job postings and senior appointments across studios, agencies, and media owners. citeturn9view0turn1search10turn3search2turn3search6turn15view1

Creative software houses are hiring AI leaders who can translate between generative model capability and real production constraints, with job specs anchoring on VFX workflows rather than generic ML leadership. citeturn11search20turn3search2  

**Assumption to flag**: it is tempting to assume “Chief AI Officer” is becoming standard in creative production. The evidence here suggests the opposite: in many creative organisations, AI leadership is being embedded into existing technology/production leadership, while net-new “AI Officer” titles skew toward agencies and some enterprise environments. citeturn15view1turn15view0turn9view0

## Hiring behaviour

The commercially useful insight is that **creative production fills senior roles through three parallel channels at once: trusted network hiring (often opaque), direct/internal TA (especially at enterprises), and specialist external recruiters who can assess niche portfolios and pipelines.** Your entry strategy should assume you will rarely displace all three, so you need a wedge that complements how clients already buy.

### How they hire at senior level

Network and in-house bias is still structurally strong in VFX and production. VFX Hire’s founding rationale explicitly describes senior opportunities being kept in-house and “leaning towards who they know,” which is a classic indicator of network-driven hiring and limited external search penetration unless a trusted specialist is involved. citeturn5search11  

Direct-to-studio hiring infrastructure is also visible via industry bodies and member directories that route candidates to studio career pages. For example, UK Screen Alliance is characterised (even in third-party commentary) as a gateway to member studio recruitment pages, which reinforces the reality that many studios expect direct applications and/or direct sourcing. citeturn5search14turn5search50  

At large media/entertainment groups, internal posting and internal TA are clearly active at VP level alongside explicit pay band publication. FOX’s VP AI roles are posted on FOX Careers with detailed mandates and disclosed pay ranges, signalling internal TA-managed recruitment rather than purely retained search. citeturn9view0turn10view0  

### When they do use external search, what model do they buy

In your target market, three commercial models show up repeatedly in how search firms describe buying behaviour:
- **Contingent** (success-only, often non-exclusive)  
- **Retained** (staged payments, typically 25–33% of base in the UK, often higher when measured on first-year compensation) citeturn4search16turn4search10  
- **Container / hybrid / engaged** (shared-risk, small upfront + success fee), explicitly described as a blend between contingency and retained. citeturn7search0turn7search1turn7search5  

The presence of “container search” language is not theoretical. Multiple firms describe it as a hybrid model with an upfront component to secure focus and a success component on hire. citeturn7search0turn7search5turn7search8  

### Who they are using now (observable signals)

Concrete, citable evidence of “who is used” is limited because many agencies and studios do not publish supplier lists. However, there are clear signals that specialist recruiters are embedded with named studios:
- Talent Outpost publishes a client testimonial from **Framestore’s Head of Talent** about partnering on hiring and shortlisting, implying repeatable usage for hard-to-fill roles. citeturn16view0turn17view0  
- Some specialist recruiters explicitly position themselves as offering retained/executive search for VFX/animation/games/immersive studios (e.g., DAX includes “Retained / EXECUTIVE Search” as a product line). citeturn5search0turn5search2  

In agencies, current evidence strongly suggests many “AI C-suite” roles are handled as internal appointments rather than external searches (SVP Ops elevated to CAIO; CTO/Head of Production elevated to CAIO). That does not eliminate external search, but it changes the wedge: the external hire is more likely when internal credibility is missing or when a client needs someone who has already executed AI adoption elsewhere. citeturn15view0turn15view1  

**Assumption to flag**: it is easy to assume “executive search firm” is the default route at this level. In creative production, the evidence suggests **direct + network hiring remains dominant**, with specialist recruiters winning when the role is niche or when portfolio/pipeline assessment is hard for internal TA. citeturn5search11turn5search14turn2search4  

## Competitor landscape

The commercially useful insight is that the competitive set splits into **(a) creative production specialist recruiters who understand reels/pipelines but do not own the AI leadership narrative, and (b) AI executive search specialists who understand AI leadership but are structurally weak on creative production context and craft constraints.** That gap is real and ownable if FPZ defines the niche precisely.

Creative-production-native recruiters (VFX/animation/games/immersive) with explicit senior remit:
- **DAX (Digital Arts Xchange)** positions itself as specialist recruitment for “VFX, Animation, Games and Immersive Studios” and offers **both “Exclusive Contingent Agency Hiring Agreement” and “Retained / EXECUTIVE Search”**, plus RPO and in-house support. citeturn5search0turn5search2  
- **Talent Outpost** positions as “VFX and Animation Recruitment and Executive Search” and explicitly lists senior roles studios demand (including CTO, head of pipeline, head of studio, executive producers, R&D engineers) while also publishing “competitive price” language from clients. citeturn16view0turn17view0

Creative agency / “createch” recruiters (often strong on creative tech, sometimes weaker on deep ML/pipeline leadership):
- **Major Players (Createch)** explicitly positions its Createch practice as “bridging the gap between tech and creativity” for roles spanning interactive, VR/AR, motion, and tech-first content. citeturn6search2

### Genuine gaps and where competitors look weak or overstretched

The most defensible gaps, based on competitor messaging and what current job specs demand, are:

**Lack of “production-grade AI” specialism at leadership level**

## Candidate landscape

The commercially useful conclusion is that **candidate depth exists, but it is thin if you only hunt for “AI” in titles.** The real candidate pool sits in adjacent leadership roles across pipeline, R&D, creative technology, and production operations, and your search methodology must be built to identify “AI translators” who have already shipped workflow change, not just built models.

### Where senior AI-creative talent sits today (observable employer types and titles)

VFX and post-production studios:
- Senior AI capability is often inside **ML leadership (Head of Machine Learning)** and **visual development / creative tech leadership** that explicitly integrates GenAI tools (Framestore’s Visual Development TD spec is an example of this skill stack). citeturn1search10turn2search4turn2search2  

Creative agencies and experiential networks:
- AI leadership is frequently held by **CTO/Head of Production/Operations leaders** who are given “Chief AI Officer” responsibility, suggesting the candidate archetype is a production operator with strong technical fluency and change leadership. citeturn15view1turn15view0  
- On the talent side, senior individual contributors and leads are being hired under titles like **Senior AI Creative Technologist** with stated pay ranges, which can be feeder pools for future “Head of AI” roles. citeturn6search6  

Media owners / production studios:
- AI leadership is emerging as dedicated VP roles that bridge production, R&D, and enablement (FoxNXT model), which implies the candidate pool overlaps heavily with executive production, post/VFX leadership, and creative technology leadership, plus vendor/partnership capability. citeturn9view0turn10view0  
- BBC Studios’ AI Creative Lab approach indicates a “lab-led” model where creative technologists and AI/ML tool builders support production genres. citeturn3search6turn3search17  

Creative software and AI platforms:
- AI-native and creative-software companies hire leadership around **applied research, AI solutions, and VFX engineering**, often with substantial compensation and equity upside. Example: Stability AI’s **Head of AI Solutions** role publishes a $214k–$238k range plus stock options. citeturn3search16  
- VFX-specific AI engineering leadership at Adobe illustrates the profile: deep M&E workflows plus GenAI. citeturn11search20turn3search2  

Academic and research centres feeding the industry:
- UK creative-industry AI research depth is material in places like the University of Surrey’s People-Centred AI institute, which references long-running computer vision/audio-visual AI strengths and “creative industry AI technologies”, signalling a non-trivial pipeline of senior research engineering talent that can be converted into industry leadership with the right packaging. citeturn6search56  

### Is there sufficient candidate depth to sustain a search practice?

There is enough depth to run a search practice **if** you define the niche correctly and search laterally. Evidence that “AI-head” titles alone are not abundant: UK vacancy benchmarking shows only **39 permanent jobs** requiring “Head of Artificial Intelligence” in the UK over the 6 months leading to 12 March 2026. That is across the entire economy, so the creative-production subset will be smaller still. citeturn4search0  

The viable approach is therefore to treat “AI-adjacent exec search” as a **capability-based category**, not a title-based one, and to build slates from people in:
- pipeline leadership (Head of Pipeline / Pipeline Supervisor),  
- R&D leadership (R&D engineers, ML leads),  
- production operations leadership (VP production support, creative operations),  
- creative technology leadership (VP creative technology, creative technologists),  
then qualify for “AI shipped into workflow” rather than “AI in the title”. citeturn16view0turn9view0turn10view0turn2search4  

### Are these candidates moveable, and how do they respond?

Hard public data on response rates is not available, but market signals point to **scarcity dynamics** and therefore higher candidate leverage:
- Some searches explicitly advertise remote scope plus very high pay and equity to attract scarce leaders (e.g., Head of Machine Learning, “fully remote in Europe”, “up to 200,000 + substantial equity”, explicitly for VFX/creative tech). citeturn11search23turn11search21  
- Employers are formalising AI ethics/governance responsibilities inside production roles, suggesting candidates will be cautious about reputational risk and will respond better to approaches that show fluency in IP, consent, security, and the realities of production. citeturn1search10turn9view0turn2search4  

**Assumption to flag**: “the talent pool is too thin to make this work”. The pool is thin for *pure* AI leadership titles, but broader and healthier for AI-adjacent change leaders across pipeline/R&D/production ops. If FPZ Exec builds the practice only around “Head of AI” searches, it will feel thin. If it builds around “AI-in-production leadership”, it becomes viable. citeturn4search0turn9view0turn1search10turn2search4  

## Client profile

The commercially useful answer is that the best early adopters for paid search are the organisations where AI is already budgeted as a core delivery or product lever, and where failure is expensive. In practice, that concentrates in **creative software/SaaS and well-capitalised media/entertainment innovation groups first, then the largest agencies and select VFX studios.**

### Ranked by likelihood to need these hires and pay exec search fees

AI-native creative companies and GenAI production studios (highest likelihood)  
They compete on AI capability directly, pay aggressively (often with equity), and need leaders who can ship systems, not just prototypes. This category includes GenAI storytelling studios and platforms like those posting senior VFX/AI leadership roles with published six-figure pay bands. citeturn2search14turn3search16turn11search23  

Creative SaaS platforms and VFX software houses (very high likelihood)  
Adobe’s disclosed director-level compensation for AI/VFX engineering is a strong indicator of willingness to pay for scarce leaders, and these firms can afford retained-level fees when roles are transformative. citeturn11search20turn3search2  

Media owners, broadcasters, and major production groups building internal AI capability (high likelihood)  
FOX is explicitly hiring VP-level AI production leadership with $179k–$300k ranges and a central innovation studio model, which is exactly the kind of mandate where executive search value is legible. BBC Studios is also formalising AI production capability via its AI Creative Lab. citeturn10view0turn9view0turn3search6turn3search17  

Games studios and interactive entertainment (selective but can be extremely high value)  
The Netflix director GenAI games posting is an extreme case but a useful proof that certain gaming organisations will pay “exec-plus” money for GenAI leadership at the intersection of creativity and technology. citeturn18search0turn18search2  

Creative agencies, experiential agencies, and large networks (medium-to-high likelihood; more complex buying behaviour)
