# The complete practitioner playbook for selling and running retained executive search

**Retained search is sold on process, won on positioning, and delivered through disciplined methodology — not by repackaging contingency recruitment with an upfront invoice.** The practitioner consensus from Greg Savage, Mike Gionta, Jordan Taylor, Mark Whitby, James O'Brien, Somer Hackley, and AESC guidance converges on a single insight: clients do not buy retained search because it costs more. They buy it because the search methodology eliminates the risk of a catastrophic senior hire. For a solo founder with 300+ contingency placements and zero retained mandates, the transition requires rewiring how you sell, what you deliver, and how you manage every stage of the engagement. This report extracts every usable framework, script, and operational methodology from the best publicly available practitioner content and maps it to a solo boutique launching in a niche with no competitor search firm and a candidate universe of 200–500 globally.

## Section 1 — The retained pitch framework

### The meeting structure that wins mandates

The retained pitch is not a sales presentation. It is a diagnostic consultation where you lead the client to conclude that retained search is the only rational approach for their hire. Three practitioner frameworks define the architecture.

**Mike Gionta (RecruiterU)** teaches a four-phase sequence that moves from pain to process to price to commitment. Phase one establishes need and urgency — not by asking "how urgent is this on a scale of one to five?" but by asking *"What are the consequences of this position remaining open?"* You dig until the client articulates the economic cost of the vacancy in their own words. Phase two presents your process in granular, almost theatrical detail. Gionta's exact script: *"We will put together a list of 60 to 80 people. We will reach out to those people seven times through a combination of voicemail, email, text, and InMails, over three weeks. Of the 80, we will have talked to 60. Of the 60, statistically, we will speak to a bunch who are interested and not qualified. We are going to talk to a whole bunch that are qualified and not interested because of the timing, because of money due to them, because they love their jobs — but out of that list are going to fall two or three people that have two things going for them. One, they are in the top 15% or 20% of what they do, and two, we have identified two or three valid reasons for them to make a career change right now."* Phase three names the investment: *"Because we are going to do all that, that represents an investment in the search of 28.6% with a $7,000 upfront deposit."* Phase four is the trial close: *"Now, Mr. or Ms. Hiring Manager, are you authorised to approve that, or do you and I need to talk to someone else?"*

**Mark Whitby (Recruitment Coach)** compresses this into a five-step meeting flow: qualify the opportunity as a "Grade A" search, confirm you are dealing directly with the decision-maker, establish the client is highly motivated to fill the role, win their trust that you can deliver, and then **recommend retained as the solution — not as an option**. Whitby's critical principle: *"Don't give options, tell them what they should do!"* His closing language includes seven variants, each framed as a recommendation rather than a question: *"Considering the importance of this appointment to your business, and the consequences of getting it wrong, the logical solution is retained search"* and *"For a project of this importance, where you just can't afford to make a mistake, I recommend our retained service."*

**Jordan Taylor (Retrained Search)** adds a pre-qualification step that protects your time. He advises always selling retained down first — not to win retainers, but to vet how urgent the hiring need actually is. *"ALWAYS sell retained down. ALWAYS. The point of this isn't to win retainers. The point is to actually VET HOW URGENT THE HIRING NEED IS."* Three responses reveal the truth: if the client says "sure" too quickly, they may not be serious enough; if they say "no" or "I'll get back to you," that signals high buying behaviour because they need internal approval; if they say "no, not a serious search," you have saved yourself weeks of wasted effort.

### Positioning retained without badmouthing contingency

Since the founder still operates a contingency firm, the pitch must elevate retained without denigrating the model that pays existing bills. Practitioners solve this through three reframes.

**The "problem-first" approach** (Jordan Taylor) anchors the conversation in the client's business outcome before discussing any delivery model. Taylor's exact framing: *"When we engage with clients, there's typically a message behind the recruitment. They're looking for a new Managing Director or a new Sales Director. It's not because they're looking for a new Sales Director, it's because they want to grow revenue by $10 million next year. So when you realise the $10 million on the line, fees don't come in to anywhere near as much."* He adds the "cost vs investment" frame: *"It comes down to how they see that hire and whether they see it as a cost or an investment. If it's an investment, then the reality of it is they need to invest in a proper partnership."*

**The "same cost, different timing" frame** (Mark Whitby) neutralises the fee comparison entirely: *"The good news is that the fee for our retained service is no more than you would pay for our contingency service, and yet you get a much higher level of service! The only difference is the fee is paid in instalments, starting with a deposit of one-third the fee upon commencement of the assignment, which is then deducted from the final invoice."*

**The "conversion rate" frame** (James O'Brien, i-intro) uses mathematics: *"By opting for retained recruitment, you can increase your conversion rate from 20% — which is the norm for contingency recruitment — to 60% or more."* O'Brien explicitly avoids anti-contingency language: *"I am not here saying that retained recruitment is the only way to deliver service."* He then uses a **bad hire calculator** to show the client the true cost of staff turnover, making the retained fee appear modest by comparison. His retention data provides the proof: **96% of candidates placed through their methodology are still in the job after one year; 93% after two years**.

### Objection handling — five scripts from practitioners

**(a) "Why should I pay upfront when I can go contingent and pay on results?"**

Gionta's response is the most aggressive and effective script available publicly. He opens with a reframe: *"If I agreed to fill this position for free and did not give you any suitable candidates, tell me how much money I have saved you."* Pause. Then: *"You told me this would cost you $2 million a week in revenue, and we are talking about an $8,000 difference in fee. I am having trouble making sense of this."* He then probes the client's existing contingency arrangements: *"With the other firms that you worked with, what was their process for fulfilment?"* The client rarely knows. *"So, if I am hearing you correctly, a position that has a consequence of XYZ… you are telling me that you had no other vetting than they agreed to your fee, and they sounded okay on the phone? And then you are challenging me as to why I will not accept the lousy terms that have produced no results for you so far?"*

For a first-time client who remains hesitant, Gionta offers his **"Reverse Retainer"**: *"If I did everything I said I did, I did not fill the opening because you cancelled it or found someone internally, still I sent you a portfolio of candidates. If you had that knowledge in advance — would you be uncomfortable paying a retainer?"* Client says no. *"Here is what I will do. I am going to give you retained-level service. If you cancel the opening and/or fill it internally and I have presented you a portfolio of X candidates that you have interviewed in X amount of time, you will send me $7,000 at the end of the search as a consulting fee for providing you the knowledge that you are hiring the best available person in the market."*

Taylor's analogy complements this: *"It's like baking a wedding cake. All of that work goes in at the start. Once the cake's in the oven, the job's kind of done."*

**(b) "We've never used retained search before."**

Whitby recommends leading with case studies of similar roles you have filled — and critically, *"your case studies don't have to be retained searches."* Past contingency placements reframed through a retained lens demonstrate the outcome the client wants. His closing line: *"When we have filled similar roles in the past, retained search has invariably yielded the best results."* Gionta adds the mindset insight through his client Gerard in Dublin, who said "nobody pays retainers in Ireland" and sold his first retainer three weeks after coaching. The shift was not in skill but in conviction about his own value.

**(c) "Your fee is higher than our contingency suppliers."**

Whitby's script: *"The good news is that the fee for our retained service is no more than you would pay for our contingency service, and yet you get a much higher level of service! The only difference is the fee is paid in instalments."* His analogy: *"First Class versus Economy. Lexus versus Toyota. It saves time, effort, hassle and takes the stress out of recruiting."* He also offers flexible fee structures for reluctant buyers — quarter/quarter/half, third/two-thirds, or a **nominal deposit of $2,500** (or equivalent) with the balance on completion — to lower the barrier to a first retained engagement.

**(d) "Can you guarantee a placement?"**

Retrained Search addresses this directly: the promise is not filling a seat — *"Success isn't just putting a bum on a seat."* The retained fee pays for the process and expertise, not a placement guarantee. Whitby takes a more confident line: *"That's not going to happen! Our success rate is 98%. I don't accept search assignments unless I'm confident of success. My reputation is more important to me than the fee."* O'Brien reframes from placement guarantee to **retention guarantee** — his 96% one-year and 93% two-year retention rates shift the conversation from "will you fill it?" to "will the person you place actually stay and perform?"

**Contradiction and recommendation:** Whitby essentially promises to fill the role; Retrained Search and O'Brien redirect toward process and retention guarantees. For a solo founder with no retained track record, the **process/retention guarantee is safer and more credible**. You cannot claim a 98% retained success rate with zero retained mandates, but you can promise a rigorous methodology with a 12-month replacement guarantee.

**(e) "We want to use multiple agencies."**

Whitby quotes Bill Radin: *"You wouldn't hire three accountants to do your tax return and have them fight over the fee, would you?"* His own script: *"We have access to all the same candidates as our competitors. Would you rather have five agencies giving you 20% effort or one consultant who's 100% committed?"* Taylor uses the builder analogy: *"A builder wouldn't come to your house to do an extension and the customer will say, 'I've got 10 builders. I want all of them to come and have a go at the extension. And at the end I'll decide if I want to pay you or not.' It just doesn't work that way."* Gionta goes further: when told a client has 12 firms on the role, he asks *"Then why are we talking? A dozen firms have agreed to this fee; why are we even talking?"* — forcing the client to acknowledge those firms are not delivering.

### CXO buyers vs HR/TA buyers

No named practitioner published a specific CXO-vs-HR retained pitch guide, but the principle set is clear from their combined work. **With a CXO, you sell the outcome. With HR/TA, you sell the process.**

For a CXO buyer, Taylor's framing applies: lead with the business result the hire will deliver, not the methodology. Ask Gionta's question — *"What are the consequences of this position remaining open?"* — and quantify the cost of vacancy in revenue terms. CXOs care about speed to outcome, confidentiality, quality of the shortlist, and how their brand is represented in the market. They have **three to five minutes of patience** (Savage) and will not read a 10-page proposal.

For an HR/TA buyer, O'Brien's bad hire calculator gives them the internal ammunition to justify the retained fee to their CFO. Whitby's process-heavy framing applies: *"Client saves a huge amount of time and effort — one point of contact, only interviewing the most suitable applicants, having an expert consultant to project manage the entire process."* HR buyers need process rigour, EDI compliance in the candidate longlist, defensibility of the hiring decision, and a document they can take to budget holders.

**For the founder's market** — mid-market companies of 50–500 employees making first-time AI leadership hires — the buyer will almost always be the CEO, CTO, or COO. HR functions in these firms are typically too under-resourced for executive search. Lead with strategic insight about AI leadership, not methodology. Keep proposals short and confident.

### Materials and leave-behinds

Whitby's critical advice: *"Don't send speculative proposals, send agreements for signing."* A proposal implies they have a choice; an agreement implies they have already decided. O'Brien's **bad hire calculator** serves as a leave-behind that quantifies the problem. Somer Hackley (Distinguished Search) uses her "Two Distinguished Minutes" video series — short interviews with senior leaders — as relationship-building content that positions her as a trusted adviser before any pitch meeting occurs. Her philosophy: *"Doing BD by not doing BD — everything I do is BD."*

The recommended materials portfolio combines: an engagement letter (not a proposal), a process document showing your search methodology, case studies of similar placements from contingency work (Whitby: *"Your case studies don't have to be retained searches"*), a bad hire calculator or cost-of-vacancy tool, a market map sample, and the three-column service comparison matrix the founder has already designed.

## Section 2 — Structuring the retained proposal

### What goes in, what does not, and why

A winning retained proposal contains nine sections in sequence: executive summary restating the client's specific challenge (not boilerplate), understanding the mandate including strategic context and ideal candidate profile, search methodology phase-by-phase with projected timeline, your team and relevant expertise, value proposition with tangible outcomes, investment (fees and payment schedule), guarantees and ethical commitments, terms and conditions, and next steps with a signature block.

**What does not go in:** generic company boilerplate not tailored to the client (JRG Partners identifies *"a one-size-fits-all feel"* as a deal-breaker), vague methodology with no detail, ambiguous administrative fees, or candidate CVs and names (these come during the search, not the proposal).

JRG Partners provides a **proposal quality scorecard** — five tests every proposal must pass: does it clearly define the strategic need behind the role, a detailed multi-channel search methodology, a clear fee structure with no hidden costs, a strong candidate replacement guarantee, and a robust off-limits policy?

### Presenting the fee structure

The **one-third/one-third/one-third model** is the industry standard across all major sources — JRG Partners, PricingLink, RecruiterFlow, Hunter Recruiting, NPAworldwide, and AESC. First third on engagement, second on shortlist presentation (typically at 30–45 days), third on candidate acceptance (60–90 days).

Fee percentages range from **25–35% of first-year total cash compensation** across the market. North American retained firms commonly charge 30–35%; Advocate Staffing notes that exclusive retained search (lowest risk to the firm) can justify 25–27%. The founder's planned 25–30% sits at the lower-to-mid range, which is **appropriate for a solo practitioner building a track record in a niche**.

The container/engaged alternative operates as a hybrid: a small non-refundable upfront fee secures commitment, with the remainder on placement. Y Scouts charges $5,000–$8,000 upfront then 20–25% on placement; Spencer James Group charges $3,000 to one-third of the expected fee; Digital Talent Recruiters uses a $3,000 example. The founder's planned **£3,000–£5,000 upfront plus four-week exclusivity** aligns with market norms. The four-week exclusivity window is shorter than the typical 90–120 days for full retained, but functions as a trust-building entry point.

PricingLink recommends framing the fee section header as **"Your Investment"** rather than "Fees" and pairing it with three context points: the cost of a bad hire, the value of access to passive candidates, and the time savings of a structured process.

**Refund terms in retained search are unambiguous: no refunds.** JRG Partners states: *"The guarantee is a commitment to conduct a one-time replacement search at no additional professional fee. The original fee paid for the successful execution of the search process — market mapping, sourcing, screening, and stakeholder alignment — not just the resume or the hire."* If the client cancels, standard contract language from Triton/Justia: *"Fees are considered earned to the date of cancellation."*

### Framing exclusivity as a benefit

Christian Spletzer (Clockwork Recruiting) provides the strongest reframe: *"Exclusivity clauses are exclusive only in the fact that the retained firm earns your full attention, participation, and collaboration in achieving the eminent goal of the search: finding the best candidate for your role."* He emphasises two benefits: exclusivity reduces distraction from competing agencies, and it encourages client involvement. His critical reassurance: *"At any point in a retained search, you can choose to hire someone internally or an external referral."* This eliminates the "locked in" fear.

The Kondo framework adds practical conversation language: *"I understand you might be hesitant to put all your eggs in one basket. Let's build a process with clear checkpoints to ensure we're always aligned."* Then quantify the cost of non-exclusivity: *"How much time does your team currently spend managing multiple agencies, dealing with duplicate submissions, and interviewing unqualified candidates?"*

O'Brien draws the clearest philosophical line: *"Are you looking for the best person available or the best person? If it's who's available in the pool right now, that's contingency. If you want to actually find the person — based on what they need to achieve for your organisation — that's a retained campaign."*

### Replacement guarantees — what is standard, what is competitive

Top Echelon survey data shows **90 days is the most common guarantee period across all recruitment (44.9% of recruiters)**, but this reflects the contingency-heavy sample. For retained executive search specifically, JRG Partners and multiple retained-focused sources state **6–12 months is standard**. At C-suite level, 12 months is the competitive benchmark; only approximately 2.5% of all recruiters offer it, making it a powerful differentiator.

The guarantee type matters: **61.4% of recruiters offer replacement only (no refund)**, which is the correct model for retained search. Legacy MedSearch publishes a tiered structure: 100% replacement before day 30, 50% between days 31–90, 25% between days 91–360. JRG Partners identifies standard conditions that void the guarantee: the role's scope changes significantly, the client fails to provide supportive onboarding, or the client does not uphold agreed employment terms.

**Recommendation for the founder:** The planned 12-month replacement guarantee conditional on client onboarding compliance is excellent positioning. It sits at the top end of market practice, functions as a differentiator against competitors, and the conditionality protects you from guarantee claims caused by client-side failures. Frame it in proposals as: *"Should the placed candidate voluntarily resign or be terminated for cause within 12 months of their start date, we will conduct a one-time replacement search for the same position at no additional professional fee, provided the role scope, reporting structure, and employment terms remain materially unchanged."*

### How proposals differ by buyer type

For CXO buyers — the primary audience in mid-market first-time AI leadership hires — proposals should be **4–6 pages maximum**, lead with strategic impact and the cost of not making the hire, and focus on your understanding of the market and your network. PricingLink confirms: *"C-level buyers want to engage with sales professionals who they view as trusted advisors and subject-matter experts."*

For HR/procurement buyers, extend to **8–10 pages** with detailed methodology, D&I commitments, GDPR compliance, assessment framework, and reporting cadence. These buyers are comparing you side-by-side against other firms and need internal justification for the spend.

## Section 3 — Running the retained process operationally

### From signed mandate to completed placement — the full process with benchmarks

The retained search process follows a consistent architecture across all major practitioner sources. Krista Bradford (The Good Search) maps **14 steps** from engagement through closing, the most detailed publicly available practitioner breakdown. Clockwork Recruiting (Christian Spletzer) defines **8 stages**. Retrained Search (Louise Archer) maps **9 steps**. The differences are structural, not substantive — all describe the same underlying workflow.

The consolidated process with realistic timeline benchmarks for a solo boutique in a 200–500 person niche:

**Phase 1: Discovery and strategy (Weeks 1–2).** Sign contract. Conduct the intake workshop with all stakeholders. Draft the Position Prospectus and Executive Scorecard. Define target company list. Delineate off-limits companies. Agree communication cadence. Bradford emphasises that the search partner should **witness firsthand the management style and corporate culture** — not just hear about it.

**Phase 2: Research and identification (Weeks 2–5).** Map the target universe. Identify and profile candidates. Query LinkedIn, proprietary databases, and industry sources. Build organisation charts of target companies. Sound network sources for referrals and calibrations. This is the most labour-intensive phase for a solo operator.

**Phase 3: Outreach and qualification (Weeks 3–7).** Contact prospective candidates. Determine if they meet primary requirements. Gather motivational details — what it takes for them to move. Present an anonymised longlist to the client at approximately week 4 for calibration.

**Phase 4: Assessment and shortlisting (Weeks 5–9).** Conduct deep-dive interviews against the Executive Scorecard. Pre-reference candidates discreetly. Write Candidate Assessment Reports. Present a formal shortlist of **4–6 candidates** with full written assessments.

**Phase 5: Client interviews and selection (Weeks 7–11).** Coordinate interviews. Manage feedback loops. Narrow to 2–3 finalists. Conduct formal reference checks.

**Phase 6: Offer and close (Weeks 9–12+).** Position and negotiate the package. Manage counter-offer risk. Close the candidate. Provide transition and onboarding support.

**Timeline reality check:** Strategic Talent Partners states 90–100 days as typical. 4C Executive (UK) maps 8–14 weeks. Altios cites an industry average of 4–6 months. For the founder's niche — a mapped universe of 200–500 candidates, deep domain expertise, and no competing search firm — **target 8–12 weeks with a client-facing estimate of 10–12 weeks**. JRG Partners reports that **client-side delays account for 30–45% of extended search timelines**, so the biggest risk is not your process but the client's responsiveness.

### The intake workshop that extracts what the client actually needs

Lou Adler's Performance-based Hiring methodology provides the foundational framework. The core principle: **define the work, not the person**. Replace skills-and-experience job descriptions with 5–6 Key Performance Objectives (KPOs) defining tasks, actions, and measurable results. The foundational question: *"What does this person need to accomplish over the next 6–12 months to be considered successful? Given this, what do they need to do in the first 30, 60, and 90 days to ensure those objectives are met?"*

For the founder's planned 60–90 minute stakeholder workshop, the recommended structure maps directly to producing the Position Prospectus and Executive Scorecard:

**Minutes 0–15: Context setting.** Why is this role open now? What business problem does it solve? What happens if it is not filled in six months? This surfaces the economic urgency that justifies the retained fee.

**Minutes 15–40: KPO extraction.** Use Adler's five questions with each stakeholder group. *"What's the major objective or biggest challenge?"* *"What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?"* *"Are there any team or managerial issues that need to be addressed?"* Define 6–8 outcomes the successful candidate must deliver in months 1–12.

**Minutes 40–55: Competency mapping.** What competencies, leadership behaviours, and cultural attributes predict success? Identify deal-breakers. This populates the Executive Scorecard directly.

**Minutes 55–70: Calibration.** Ask stakeholders to name one or two people who would be ideal for this role and discuss why. This reveals unstated preferences and anchors the search to concrete examples.

**Minutes 70–90: Search strategy alignment.** Target company discussion, compensation range reality-check, timeline agreement, and decision-making process — who has veto, how many interview rounds, and what is the latest start date.

Cowen Partners adds a **client coachability assessment** that should be embedded in the intake: is compensation competitive? Can the client commit to a responsive interview process? Will they listen to market data? These questions protect you from mandates that are destined to stall.

### The candidate identification methodology — numbers and ratios

The funnel ratios are consistent across practitioners. For a niche of 200–500 candidates globally:

**Target universe:** 200–500 (the entire addressable market, already partially known to the founder). **Researched and profiled longlist:** 40–80 based on preliminary fit against the Position Prospectus. **Approached and contacted:** 30–50 who meet the specification. **Qualified, interested, and available:** 8–15 after screening conversations. **Shortlist presented to client:** 4–6 with full Candidate Assessment Reports. **Client interviews to finalist:** 2–3. **Placement:** 1.

Fraser Dove International provides calibration benchmarks: **80% of candidates are eliminated at the longlisting stage**. Their target shortlist-to-interview ratio is **80% or higher** — meaning 80%+ of shortlisted candidates should be considered a good match by the hiring manager. If more than 30% of the shortlist is rejected, there is a scoring discrepancy requiring recalibration against the Executive Scorecard.

### How retained candidate presentations differ from contingency CV submissions

The Candidate Assessment Report is a **consultative decision-support document**, not a forwarded CV. Bradford describes it as *"a report that details the candidate's education, career history, honours, and awards. The report also serves as an analysis and appraisal of the candidate's strengths, weaknesses, and appropriateness for the position. Additionally, this is where the recruiter will highlight any key motivators, issues, and deal-making details essential to closing the candidate."*

Clockwork's CAR structure adds: research findings scored against the position specification, a QIA (Qualified, Interested, Available) analysis detailing the candidate's motivation and constraints, red flags and outstanding notes including skills not on the CV, and recommendations for what to probe in interview. Each shortlisted candidate should be mapped against the Executive Scorecard competencies with a **comparative matrix** showing all candidates side-by-side.

### How a solo operator manages the research phase

The founder's niche creates a structural advantage that large generalist firms cannot replicate. With a universe of 200–500 candidates, **the entire addressable market can be mapped proactively** before any specific mandate is signed. Savage advises: *"You should be having 100 cups of coffee a year with the type of talent you might hire in the future."* For a niche this size, the founder should aim to have substantive conversations with 50–100 of the most senior individuals before taking the first mandate.

Practical approaches for compressing research without a team: pre-build the talent map across the entire niche as a strategic asset; use LinkedIn Recruiter or Sales Navigator as the primary identification tool; consider outsourcing initial name generation on a project basis to research firms like Intellerati; and apply a concentric circles model — **inner circle (50–100 people you know personally), middle circle (100–200 you know of), outer circle (200–500 requiring active research)** — with the goal of progressively moving everyone inward over 12–18 months.

Strategic Talent Partners notes that most retained search consultants can handle **15–20 searches per year maximum**. For a solo operator managing both business development and execution, **8–12 mandates per year with no more than 3–4 concurrent** is a realistic capacity ceiling.

### AESC standards and professional practice

AESC's six pillars are integrity, excellence, objectivity, diversity and inclusion, confidentiality, and conflict of interest compliance. Operationally, AESC requires: written engagement terms at the outset covering fees, scope, confidentiality, conflicts, and roles; mutual agreement on off-limits policies and timeline expectations; a comprehensive needs assessment and position description developed in partnership with the client; competency-based interviewing, 360-degree referencing, and due diligence processes; open and frequent communication throughout; and consideration of diversity in every assignment. AESC membership is not required but aligning with their standards provides credibility, particularly with clients who have used larger retained firms previously.

## Section 4 — Client management throughout the engagement

### Maintaining confidence through "recruiter equity"

Greg Savage's central concept for client management is **"Recruiter Equity"** — the client's trust, buy-in, and belief in your ability and judgement. It combines experience and knowledge, giving you the power to advise clients and impact outcomes. Savage writes: *"Building recruiter equity starts with taking the job order. At this point, you will map out your process and sell the quality of your screening and matching. It continues with the 'shortlist pitch', where you will confidently overcome objections and 'sell your candidates in'. Finally, you must challenge the client on their decision to reject your shortlist."* His signature move when presenting candidates: tell the client *"I stake my reputation on these candidates."*

Whitby identifies the retained model's built-in confidence mechanisms: *"Your clients are happy to receive and return your calls, so you waste less time chasing them for feedback. Your clients are receptive to your advice, so you gain greater influence over the hiring process. That often translates into faster decisions and fewer deal-breakers."*

During quiet sourcing periods, Clockwork recommends **Research Coverage Reports** — documents showing which target companies you have mapped and which candidates you have identified — to demonstrate systematic market coverage even before interviews begin. This converts invisible effort into visible progress.

### Status update cadence and format

The practitioner consensus is **weekly written updates during active search phases** (sourcing, outreach, assessment) and **fortnightly updates during slower phases** (initial research, post-shortlist while the client interviews). JRG Partners specifies that a defined communication cadence should be part of every retained proposal.

A weekly status update for a solo boutique should contain: search status summary with a RAG indicator (red/amber/green), activity since last update (approaches made, conversations held, sources consulted), target company coverage showing systematic market mapping, candidate pipeline by stage (identified → approached → in conversation → qualified → ready to present), key market intelligence insights, client actions required with deadlines, and a timeline check against agreed milestones. Keep it to one page. The discipline of producing this weekly report — even when progress feels slow — is what separates retained from contingency in the client's experience.

### Handling scope creep

JRG Partners confirms that *"scope creep or frequent, ill-considered changes to the job description mid-search"* is a primary factor extending searches beyond six months. Curran Daly adds that *"disagreements among the CEO, the board, and HR mid-search can lead to scope creep, stalling the process and damaging your reputation with top-tier talent."*

The recommended response is a three-option reset. First, acknowledge and document the change in writing. Second, present the impact against the original search strategy — what work is completed, which candidates remain relevant, what new work is required, and the timeline impact. Third, offer three options: a formal scope reset treated as a revised search with adjusted timeline and potentially additional fee; a phased approach completing the current search then conducting a second phase; or incorporation of minor adjustments within the existing engagement with documented changes. The recommended script: *"I completely understand that [new insight] has shifted your thinking on this role. Let me put together a brief impact assessment showing how this change affects our target list, the candidates already in conversation, and our timeline. We can then decide together whether we adjust within the current engagement or whether this warrants a reset."*

Best practice: include a **change management clause** in the engagement letter specifying that material changes to title, reporting line, compensation band, or core competencies constitute a scope change triggering a formal review.

### Managing the offer and negotiation stage

The retained consultant's role at offer stage is fundamentally different from contingency. You have been involved from strategy through assessment, your fees are already partially received, and your counsel carries weight without the desperation of a 100%-at-risk fee. Clockwork's principle: *"It's your job as the recruiter to make sure both the client and the candidate have all the information they might need to make an intelligent choice about their future."* You should not be aggressively selling either party. *"If you do find yourself working overtime to sell the candidate on the role or the client on the candidate, that's a sign it won't work out well."*

Practically, this means: throughout the search, gather each finalist's compensation expectations, motivations, and deal-breakers so the offer is crafted to land; position the offer within total value (not just base salary) including equity, benefits, growth opportunity, and role significance; if a gap exists, identify what bridges it (sign-on bonus, review timeline, title, scope); brief the client that a counter-offer from the candidate's current employer is likely at this level; and manage the notice period proactively with regular candidate contact to prevent second thoughts.

### Off-limits obligations — what they mean operationally

Doug Seville (DSML Executive Search) defines it clearly: *"The 'off limits' rule means that, as a retained recruiter, I'm not going to do anything that would jeopardise my relationship with my customer. Unlike a contingent recruiter, who is owed nothing unless a final purchase is made, my customer has bought our services up front and they trust me to always act in the best interests of their organisation."*

Duration standards vary: Godliman Ltd cites **at least one year from completion** as industry standard; JRG Partners cites **1–2 years**; DSML cites **two years** for larger firms. Scope must be explicitly agreed — whole organisation, division only, or location-specific. AESC requires off-limits terms to be defined in writing. Godliman offers the strategic warning: *"Managing the off-limits list is one of the most important aspects of a search consultant's client strategy. Too many off-limits clients will restrict your ability to deliver candidates."*

**Recommendation:** Offer **whole-organisation off-limits for 12 months post-completion** as standard. For mid-market clients of 50–500 employees, this demonstrates commitment without severely restricting the sourcing universe in a niche of 200–500 candidates.

### Handling the unresponsive client

Savage identifies the root cause: *"For clients, it's usually about shifting priorities, poor internal communication, or a lack of accountability in their hiring process. It's hard to hear, but often clients ghost you because they do not take you seriously, or you lack credibility or gravitas."*

The escalation framework runs four levels. **Level 1 (days 3–5):** Combine a substantive search update with a specific question requiring a response — *"I have two strong profiles I'd like to discuss. Could we schedule 20 minutes this week?"* **Level 2 (days 7–10):** Phone call, not email, acknowledging the silence — *"I appreciate you're incredibly busy. I need your input to keep momentum."* **Level 3 (day 14+):** Formal communication reframing around their business need — *"The delay is now impacting our ability to engage the strongest candidates, as they are being approached by other organisations."* **Level 4:** Savage's "tough love" conversation, using his **"ten seconds of courage" framework**: *"Do your planning. Think through your strategy. Then, as the nerves rise, tell yourself you only need ten seconds of courage. Just enough to start. Once you begin, you're in the conversation. And once you're in, you're almost always fine."*

Prevention is superior to cure: agree communication cadence in the engagement letter, set recurring calendar invitations at the outset, and use Clockwork's benchmark profiles early to force engagement before the main search effort begins.

### Post-placement relationship

AESC states: *"The executive search firm's responsibility does not end when the candidate accepts an offer. AESC members are available to assist their clients with onboarding and integration."* The recommended cadence: **personal call to both client and placed executive in week one**, structured check-in at 30 days asking specifically about cultural integration and early wins, formal review at 90 days when most executives form initial views on fit, guarantee milestone check at 6 months confirming mutual satisfaction, and anniversary check-in at 12 months transitioning the conversation from placement support to account development. Clockwork's principle: *"Good search work leads to good business development which leads to good search work."*

### Every document needed before the first mandate

The complete document set divides into 11 client-facing and 11 internal-only items.

**Client-facing documents:** Retained search proposal, service comparison matrix (already designed), firm credentials or capability statement, retained search agreement/contract, Position Prospectus template (already planned), search progress report template, Candidate Assessment Report template, Executive Scorecard (already planned), structured interview guide for the client, reference report template, and offer advisory/compensation benchmarking brief.

### The documents that matter most and what goes in each

The **Retained Search Agreement** must contain: parties, scope of services with exclusivity confirmation, fee calculation, payment schedule with trigger events, expense reimbursement (pre-approved and capped), candidate ownership/introduction period (typically 12 months), replacement guarantee with conditions and duration, off-limits clause with duration and scope, confidentiality obligations, data protection/GDPR compliance, termination provisions with notice period and earned-fees language, governing law, and signatures. The ASA/NAPS model agreement is available free through NPAworldwide and covers all these elements.

The **Candidate Assessment Report** should contain: a two-to-three-sentence executive summary of why the candidate merits consideration, career history with context on scope and achievements at each role, education and qualifications, strengths assessment mapped against the Position Specification, development areas and risks with honest appraisal, key motivators and flight risk factors, compensation expectations (current package and requirements), availability and notice period, and deal-making details — what would close this candidate and what could lose them.

The **Search Progress Report** should contain: RAG status indicator, activity since last update, pipeline overview by stage with numbers, key candidate highlights (anonymised summaries of strongest prospects), market intelligence themes, issues or risks requiring client input, next steps and client actions required, and timeline check against milestones.

### Systems for a solo operator with ADHD constraints

The technology stack must account for executive function challenges: externalise everything from working memory, use visual systems over linear ones, automate administrative friction, and manage energy rather than time.

**Recommended minimum viable tech stack for FPZ CreativAI:**

For CRM and search management, **i-intro®** is purpose-built for solo and SME recruiters transitioning to retained — it includes a candidate delivery platform, behavioural assessments, and video introductions, with 300+ firms using it and training through the Retained Recruiter Academy. If budget is constrained, **Recruit CRM** or **Airtable** configured with Kanban views provides a visual, flexible, colour-codable alternative at lower cost.

For project management, **Leantime** is built specifically for neurodivergent users with goal-connected task management, energy-aware scheduling, and minimal overwhelm design. It is open-source with a free tier.

For sourcing, **LinkedIn Sales Navigator** remains non-negotiable for executive search, paired with **Apollo.io** for contact enrichment and email sequences.

For scheduling, **Cal.com** (open-source) or **Calendly** eliminates back-and-forth booking friction entirely.

For documents and proposals, **Google Workspace** for core documents and **Canva** for the Position Prospectus and branded templates, with **PandaDoc** or **DocuSign** for proposal delivery and electronic signatures.

For automation, **Zapier** connects tools: new Airtable record triggers a Leantime task creation and a calendar reminder. This chain externalises the executive function of remembering next steps.

For accountability, **external structure is critical for a solo ADHD operator**. Options include Mark Whitby's coaching group, the i-intro® community, a peer recruiter, or virtual coworking body-doubling sessions through Focusmate or Flown.

### ADHD-specific operational principles

The single most important system for an ADHD solo operator is **written SOPs for every repeatable process**. SOPs are not bureaucracy — they are an external executive function. When you cannot remember the next step, the SOP tells you. Write one SOP per search phase before the first mandate. Use Kanban boards over to-do lists: ADHD brains process visual information faster. Colour-code everything — urgent in red, important in amber, on track in green — across calendar, pipeline, and documents.

**Energy blocking replaces time blocking.** Schedule deep work (candidate assessment, prospectus writing) during peak energy periods. Schedule admin (invoicing, data entry, CRM updates) during low-energy periods. Use 25–45 minute focus blocks rather than 90-minute sessions if sustained attention is difficult. Pair one low-interest task with one high-interest task to maintain momentum.

The weekly planning ritual creates structure without rigidity: every Monday, review active searches, set three priorities per search, and schedule key actions. Every Friday, spend 15 minutes on structured reflection — what worked, what slipped, what to adjust. The weekly client update call functions as an accountability mechanism: you must have progress to report, which creates a forcing function against procrastination.

### Prioritisation for the first mandate

Where sources conflict on minimum viable setup, the consensus is clear: **start with fewer, well-configured tools rather than a comprehensive stack**. The biggest risk is not under-tooling — it is over-tooling and then abandoning systems because they create cognitive overwhelm.

The recommended build sequence: first, get the Retained Search Agreement and Position Prospectus template professionally designed and legally reviewed. Second, set up a simple visual pipeline tracker in Airtable with Kanban views. Third, create SOPs for each search phase — these are your external executive function and the single highest-value preparatory investment. Fourth, choose one CRM or search platform (i-intro® if budget allows, Airtable if not). Fifth, build remaining templates iteratively as the first mandate progresses rather than trying to perfect everything before launch.

## Conclusion — what the practitioner consensus means for a solo boutique with no retained track record

The practitioner literature converges on three insights that matter most for the founder's situation. First, **retained search is sold through process, not credentials** — Gionta's granular methodology walkthrough, Whitby's recommendation-not-option framing, and Taylor's urgency-vetting approach all work without requiring a retained track record because they demonstrate the value of what you will do, not what you have done. The founder's 300+ contingency placements, reframed through Whitby's lens (*"your case studies don't have to be retained searches"*), provide the proof of outcome quality.

Second, **the niche is the competitive moat**. With a candidate universe of 200–500 globally and no competitor search firm, the founder possesses the single most valuable asset in retained search: a pre-mapped talent market that no generalist firm can replicate. This compresses the research phase, enables comprehensive market coverage on every mandate, and justifies the retained fee because the client literally cannot get this depth of market intelligence elsewhere.

Third, **the transition from contingency to retained is a systems challenge, not a skills challenge**. The founder already knows how to identify, assess, and place senior candidates. What changes is the cadence of client communication, the depth of documentation, the structure of candidate presentation, and the discipline of process management — all of which can be externalised into SOPs, templates, and automated workflows that accommodate ADHD constraints. O'Brien's reframe captures it: *"Recruiters work on vacancies. Management consultants who specialise in talent acquisition work on campaigns."* The founder is not learning a new profession. He is upgrading the operating system.
