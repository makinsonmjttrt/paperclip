# Engaging senior passive candidates for retained executive search

**The single highest-leverage move for a solo retained search founder with no track record is to position every outreach as a market intelligence exchange, not a recruitment pitch.** Senior executives ignore recruiters but engage with credible specialists who can teach them something about their own market. The methodology below — synthesised from practitioner-authored content across retained search firms, the AESC, and individual search partners — provides the complete operational framework to run 20 research conversations that build a candidate network before any mandate exists. Every technique, script, and framework is extracted directly, with sources named inline, and calibrated for a boutique founder operating in a niche of 200–500 global candidates.

## Section 1: Channel selection and first contact

### The phone remains the gold-standard channel at C-suite level

**57% of C-level and VP-level buyers prefer phone as their first point of contact** (DiscoverOrg/RAIN Group data, cited across multiple 2025–2026 sources). Better Headhunting, a UK practitioner resource on retained search methodology, states it plainly: "Most headhunters prefer to have an initial approach call on the phone as it gives them the opportunity to verbally describe the opportunity to candidates." A typical retained search approach call lasts **10–15 minutes**, covering the opportunity, company context, and candidate profile sought.

However, cold calling is increasingly impractical at senior level. Direct office numbers are harder to find, and as Better Headhunting notes: "Many companies — particularly B2C internet companies — don't have easily found office numbers. Increasingly, headhunters email candidates or send them InMails on LinkedIn to book a future call." The practical implication: **phone is the engagement channel; email and LinkedIn are the booking channels.**

The response rate hierarchy for C-suite candidates runs as follows:

| Channel | C-suite response rate | Primary role in retained search |
|---|---|---|
| Warm introduction | 10–15× cold outreach rates | Gold standard — always preferred |
| Phone (scheduled) | ~50% when booked (Social Talent/Jonathan Campbell) | Primary engagement channel |
| Direct email | 6.4% C-suite average; **10.44% with timeline-based hooks** (The Digital Bloom, 2025) | Gateway to phone; follow-up |
| LinkedIn InMail | **6.98% for C-level** — the lowest of any seniority segment (SalesSo, 2026) | Backup channel; pre-approach research |
| Multi-channel sequence | 34.5% (SourceWhale) | Recommended standard methodology |

The case against relying on LinkedIn InMail at C-suite level is stark. SalesSo's 2026 analysis found C-level executives show the lowest InMail response rate of any seniority segment because they are "time-poor and inbox-swamped." Better Headhunting observes that LinkedIn has democratised access to the point where being headhunted "feels less special to candidates than it did 20 years ago." InMail is now strongly associated with contingent recruitment and SDR outreach, not retained search.

The case for email is stronger than most practitioners assume. C-level executives respond **23% more often** than non-C-suite employees (Mailforge, 2026). The critical variable is message quality: timeline-based hooks achieve **10.44% CEO reply rates** versus 4.26% for generic problem statements — a **2.45× spread** on the same channel (The Digital Bloom). Emails of **50–125 words** achieve dramatically higher response rates; longer messages see response collapse.

Warm introductions convert at **10–15× cold outreach rates** (Be Executive Events). Network referrals account for **60% of executive placements** (LinkedIn, cited by iSmartRecruit). The AESC frames this structurally: "Executive candidates respond very differently to an independent third party than they do to direct outreach from an employee of the hiring firm. Top executives cannot afford to let their interest in a new role become public, and they are more likely to trust the confidentiality offered by an experienced third party."

### How boutique firms compensate for lack of brand recognition

Simon Kent, Partner at Wilton & Bain (10 years in boutique executive search, London/New York), provides the most useful practitioner analysis. His assessment of the SHREK firms (Spencer Stuart, Heidrick & Struggles, Russell Reynolds, Egon Zehnder, Korn Ferry): "To appoint a CEO or Board Member of a Fortune 500 company requires a rarefied network and brand to ensure that phone calls to prospective candidates are returned promptly." But he qualifies this sharply: **"With the exception of extremely rarefied C-level searches, it may be a fallacy to state that only SHREK firms can reach the target community for a given search."**

Five structural advantages compensate for missing brand recognition:

**No off-limits restrictions.** Large firms contractually agree not to recruit from client organisations. Vantedge Search notes: "Large firms are likely to be limited in their outreach within key companies in your industry because of non-solicitation agreements with their existing clients." Clockwork data indicates **approximately 30% of retained searches at large firms go unfulfilled**, with off-limits constraints a contributing factor. In a 200–500 person candidate universe, off-limits lists would be devastating — a boutique faces none.

**Partner-led delivery.** Neon River states: "In most cases, the Partners or founders who sell the projects also deliver them, which often leads to higher project quality than with larger firms." Kent notes that SHREK partners bill ~$4M/year and are "heavily leveraged" with junior teams doing core candidate work. When a senior executive receives a call from the actual founder who will run their process — not a junior associate — this compensates for brand deficit with personal gravitas.

**Deep specialisation as credibility proxy.** Kent again: "The best executive search boutiques are competitive in at least two of price, quality, and speed, usually based on a deep understanding of a particular market niche. At their best, the immediate network and insights that these firms have in their markets are unparalleled." For AI leadership in creative production, demonstrable sector knowledge becomes the brand substitute.

**Reputation sensitivity.** Neon River: "They are more reputation-conscious because they lack an established brand; a poor project means no next project." This translates into higher effort per search, which candidates notice.

**State the retained engagement explicitly.** GatedTalent/Talentis: "If she represents a retained executive search firm, you can reasonably assume that she is working on a specific opportunity for a particular client, and she is operating on an exclusive basis." Naming the retained nature of your work immediately separates you from the contingent recruiters flooding their inbox.

### Engineering warm introductions without a direct relationship

Retained executive search has a standard architecture for this: **source calls precede candidate calls.** Krista Bradford (CEO, The Good Search) describes the methodology: "Search firms sound their network of sources for candidate referrals and calibrations. Sources include journalists, professional associations, and other relevant groups."

KeySearch formalises this into a **three-tier contact strategy**: (1) **Information Givers** — contacted first to gather intelligence; (2) **Door Openers** — leveraged for introductions once intelligence is gathered; (3) **Decision Makers** — approached last, when fully prepared with market knowledge and warm connections.

The practical sequence for a solo founder:

Call industry sources — conference speakers, VES board members, SIGGRAPH programme committee members, VC partners investing in creative-tech, university programme directors — not as candidates but as domain experts. Ask: "Who is doing the most interesting work in AI-driven creative production?" This simultaneously generates candidate names and creates a relationship where the source may offer to introduce you.

Each source conversation generates **2–3 new names**. Each of those conversations generates 2–3 more. Within 10–15 source calls, you have a mapped landscape with warm paths to most targets. Hunt Club's entire model operates this way: "30,000+ respected leaders make direct introductions to high-quality talent others can't reach."

Kent identifies a specific technique applicable to boutiques: **backchannel referencing.** Before approaching a target candidate, speak to someone who knows them. Frame it as market research. Once that person has said positive things about the target, ask: "Would you be comfortable introducing me? I'm working on something that might be relevant for them." The introduction must be active and genuine — a Substack-writing life sciences search partner warns: "When you reference a mutual connection, you're not creating rapport — you're revealing that you're following some outdated networking playbook. Stop doing this." Simply name-dropping a mutual connection in a cold message is counterproductive. The introduction must be live.

## Section 2: Message construction for senior profiles

### The first outreach message — what works and why

Christian Spletzer (Clockwork Recruiting) provides the foundational framework: every first message must answer four questions in order — **Who are you? Why are you reaching out? What is this about? How can they reach you?** His tactical instruction: "If you're a small search firm, don't shy away from it — tell them what your specialty is." Clockwork's tested sequencing: **call first, then email.** "If you email first, it gets deleted. If you call first, you'll likely get voicemail, but you can say you'll follow up with an email — that email is more likely to be read."

**Email — live mandate:**

> Subject: AI leadership role — confidential search
>
> [First name],
>
> I lead FPZ CreativAI, a retained executive search practice focused exclusively on senior AI leadership roles in creative production — VFX, animation, and emerging content pipelines.
>
> I'm conducting a confidential search for a [VP/Head of AI] for a [brief, compelling descriptor: e.g., "studio reshaping how feature content is produced using generative tools"]. Your work at [Company] — particularly [specific reference: a shipped product, a keynote, a team they built] — made you one of a small number of people globally whose perspective would be genuinely valuable.
>
> I appreciate you may be entirely content where you are. I'd simply welcome a 20-minute conversation — whether this specific role is of interest or not, I'd value your insight on the leadership landscape in this space.
>
> Would a brief call this week or next suit?

This works because it names their specific work (not generic flattery), signals exclusivity ("one of a small number of people globally"), removes pressure ("whether this specific role is of interest or not"), offers the research-conversation frame, and stays under **150 words**.

**LinkedIn connection message — condensed:**

> [First name] — I run a retained search practice specialising in AI leadership across creative production. I'm mapping the global landscape of senior leaders in this space and your work at [Company] stood out. Would you be open to a brief, confidential conversation? No agenda beyond learning who's shaping this field. Happy to share what I'm seeing in the market in return.

**Phone — cold call opening:**

> "Good [morning/afternoon], [First name]. This is [Your Name] — I run FPZ CreativAI, a retained executive search firm focused on senior AI leadership roles in creative production. I appreciate this is a cold call, and I'll be brief.
>
> I'm leading a confidential search for a [title], and your background at [Company] — specifically [concrete detail] — put you at the top of a very short list. I know you're likely not looking, and that's precisely why I'm calling — the people who are right for this kind of role rarely are.
>
> Would you be open to a 15-minute exploratory conversation this week?"

Lou Adler (Performance-based Hiring) identifies the critical mistake: do **not** open with "Do you have a moment to talk about [position]?" — it invites an instant "No." His principle: add a delay into your opening as a conversation starter.

### How messaging differs without a live mandate

When building relationships before a mandate exists, the message must lead with intellectual curiosity, remove all sales pressure, and use future-facing language. Lou Adler specifically notes: "Adding a delay into your opening is a great conversation starter." His technique: "We're doing workforce planning for next year and a few senior positions are being created. Would you be open to chatting to see if one represented a strategic career opportunity?"

**Email — no mandate, relationship-building:**

> [First name],
>
> I'm building a specialist practice in AI leadership across creative production — VFX, animation, real-time pipelines. Rather than reaching out when I have a specific brief, I'm investing time now to understand who's shaping this space and what the next 2–3 years look like from a leadership perspective.
>
> Your trajectory at [Company] — particularly [specific detail] — suggests you'd have a perspective worth hearing. I'm not calling about a role today. I'd simply value 20 minutes of your time to learn what you're seeing and share what I'm hearing from other leaders in this space.
>
> Would that be of interest?

This positions you as a peer researcher, not a transactional recruiter. In a relationship-driven industry like VFX/creative production, this maps directly to how the industry already operates.

### Handling "I'm not looking" — extracted practitioner techniques

Lou Adler provides the most comprehensive framework (from "On Becoming a Super Recruiter"). His foundational reframe: **"An 'I'm not interested' response does not mean the person is not interested in exploring another situation. It means the person does not want to talk to you. This is a big difference."**

**Technique 1 — Sell the conversation, not the job:**
> "Would you be open to have an exploratory career conversation about whether one of our current or future opportunities represents a good career move?"

Adler's principle: "By definition, passive candidates are not looking for another job, so stop selling them something they don't want."

**Technique 2 — The irrefutable question:**
> "Would you be open to exploring a senior [function] position if it were superior to what you're doing today?"

Adler reports this gets a "Yes" approximately **95% of the time**.

**Technique 3 — The attention reset:**
> Candidate: "I'm not interested."
> You: **"That's exactly why we should talk."**

Adler: "Neither comment makes much sense, so this is a great way to reset expectations."

**Technique 4 — The career strategist frame:**
> "Time is your most valuable asset. Whatever you do in the next 2–3 years will affect the next 5–10. Sometimes there's a bigger risk in staying in the same role rather than changing."

**Technique 5 — The parking lot (for compensation objections):**
> "If the job doesn't represent a career move, it doesn't matter what we pay you. Let's first see if it is, and if so, we can determine if the pay is appropriate."

**Technique 6 — Become someone worth knowing:**
> "I focus exclusively on placing top people in AI leadership roles across creative production. If the current opportunity isn't a good career move, we can at least network until something more appropriate develops."

Adler: "People want to work with people who can help their career growth."

Greg Savage's approach differs in emphasis. His core principle: "Pick up the phone and talk to the candidates. Give them the verbal handshake and build that relationship so that they trust you." His text-first filtering technique: "Got a job. If it's not right for you, my apologies for the interruption. If it is, just press Yes and I'll call you." This filters for interest before investing call time.

**Where Adler and Savage conflict — and the recommendation for a solo boutique founder:** Adler's framework is more structured, script-driven, and designed for high-volume passive candidate engagement in corporate talent acquisition. Savage's approach is more relationship-first, intuition-led, and native to agency search. For a solo founder building a 200–500 person niche network, **Savage's relational approach is the better fit for ongoing relationship-building**, while **Adler's objection-handling scripts are the better fit for the specific moment when a candidate pushes back.** Use both: Savage for strategy, Adler for tactical responses.

### Subject lines that work at VP/C-suite level

Gem's analysis of millions of outreach sequences found subject lines achieving **80%+ open rates** consistently include role/seniority references ("VP," "Executive," "CTO"), action verbs ("build," "scale," "lead"), and enthusiasm signals ("exciting," "growth," "founding"). IQTalent data shows strategic subject lines hit **38–45% open rates** versus the typical 20–22%. Under **30 characters** performs best on mobile (41.6% of emails opened on mobile). Name in subject line increases open rate by **26%** (Campaign Monitor).

**For live mandates:**
- "[First name] — confidential AI leadership search"
- "VP AI role — your name came up"
- "Head of AI — [studio/company descriptor]"

**For relationship-building:**
- "AI leadership in creative production — your perspective"
- "[First name] — mapping AI leaders in [VFX/content/production]"
- "Research: who's leading AI in creative production?"

## Section 3: Building relationships before a live mandate

### Greg Savage's methodology — the core techniques extracted

Greg Savage (44+ years in recruitment; founder of Firebrand Talent Search, People2People, Recruitment Solutions; author of *The Savage Truth* and *Recruit The Savage Way*) provides the most directly applicable methodology for candidate relationship-building.

**"Everyone is a candidate. All the time."** Savage's foundational thesis (articulated in his 2011 blog post at gregsavage.com.au) rejects the active/passive binary entirely: "There are no passive candidates, all candidates are active, it's just a matter of timing. There's no one listening to this podcast who is not going to change jobs at some point. Maybe it's tomorrow, maybe it's in two years." The implication: every senior AI leader you speak to today will move at some point. Your job is to be so embedded in their professional life that when it comes, they speak to you before anyone else.

**The consumer marketing reframe.** Savage explicitly says recruitment has merged with marketing: "Consumer marketers tell us about their car or their washing powder, and they know we're not looking to buy it now but they want us when we come to the purchase, to think of them, and have a relationship with them." Treat your candidate universe like a consumer marketer treats their audience.

**Three calls a day — the daily discipline.** From Savage's November 2024 blog post "One tactic will turbocharge your impact": **"Make three phone calls a day. To people not expecting to hear from you."** Targets: old clients who've moved on, candidates you've worked with but didn't place, someone you met at an event, suppliers in your space, someone in your current candidate pool you promised to follow up with. The critical instruction: **"Don't ask for anything. Just engage, listen, empathise and share."** He adds: "Don't only do this because the market is tough. When the market shifts, and you are flooded with work again, keep doing this. It's an investment, not a gimmick."

For a solo founder managing ~200–500 senior AI leaders, three calls per day touches the entire network roughly once per quarter — precisely the right cadence for warm contacts.

**The four moments of truth in candidate care.** Savage identifies four critical junctures where candidates judge a recruiter (from "4 times candidates will judge you," August 2024): (1) Post-first-meeting — "The candidate has taken time to meet with us. A relationship is established. Communication expectations are set. The recruiter needs to actively engage, whether there is an assignment or role on offer or not. Keep them informed. Advise on the market. Check-in: 'Has anything changed since we last spoke?'"; (2) Post-interview; (3) The offer stage; (4) The "Valley of Death" between offer acceptance and start date. The first is critical for pre-mandate relationships — even without a live role, the post-first-conversation follow-up is where the relationship is cemented or lost.

**Sell differentiation through insights, not pitches.** From "17 selling tips for recruiters now" (August 2023): "Call with insights: a salary survey, information on hiring trends, latest strategies. Whatever is likely to interest the prospect." And: "If a call is better than an email, do it. If a video call is better than a phone call, do it. If a face-to-face is better than a VC, do it."

### The market mapping conversation — a five-step framework

Market mapping is the structured, proactive process of identifying, profiling, and tracking executive talent across an industry before a specific mandate exists. It is the foundational methodology that distinguishes retained search from contingent recruitment. The conversation framework below synthesises practitioner content from TriSearch, MapX, Recruiterflow, and AESC methodology:

**Step 1 — Lead with credibility and context:**
> "Hello [Name], I'm [Your Name] from FPZ CreativAI. We're a specialist search practice focused exclusively on senior AI leadership roles in creative production. I'm not calling about a specific role today. I'm mapping the landscape of AI leadership in creative production and your name came up as someone doing particularly interesting work in this space."

**Step 2 — Frame as mutual exchange, not extraction:**
> "I'm putting together a view of how this market is evolving — who's building what, where the talent concentrations are, what's driving moves at your level — and I'd value your perspective. In return, I'm happy to share what I'm seeing across the market — compensation trends, how teams are being structured, which companies are investing seriously versus experimenting."

**Step 3 — Ask open-ended questions about them.** Savage's principle: "Ask questions about them, their current successes, their concerns, challenges, and views on matters of mutual interest." Examples calibrated for AI creative production leaders: "How are you finding the challenge of integrating generative AI into established creative workflows?" / "What's the biggest constraint on scaling your AI capability — talent, technology, or organisational buy-in?" / "If you were advising someone stepping into a role like yours for the first time, what would you tell them the market doesn't understand yet?"

**Step 4 — Offer something concrete.** Share a data point, trend observation, or market insight: "Interesting — that matches what I'm hearing from three other heads of AI creative I've spoken to this month. The consensus seems to be that..."

**Step 5 — Establish permission for ongoing contact:**
> "Would you be open to staying in touch? I typically reach out quarterly with a market update, and if anything specific comes across my desk that might be relevant to you, I'd want to make sure you hear about it first."

### Follow-up cadence — the segmented model

Recruiterflow's 2026 CRM guide provides the most operationally sound framework, segmenting contacts by receptivity:

| Segment | Definition | Cadence | Message type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actively available | Currently looking or confirmed open | Weekly | Role-specific opportunities, market positioning |
| Passively open | Not looking but willing to talk | Monthly to bi-monthly | Market insights, salary benchmarks, industry developments |
| Not looking, worth keeping warm | Happy in role, no near-term move intent | Quarterly | Thought leadership, event invitations, check-in calls |

The critical principle from Recruiterflow: "Most firms re-engage candidates when they have a relevant role. That means the first contact in a year is a pitch — and pitches from people you haven't heard from in twelve months don't land well." And: **"Cadence without relevance is noise. Sending quarterly newsletters to your entire database isn't relationship management — it's broadcasting. The standard to aim for: every touchpoint should be specific enough that the recipient feels it was sent to them, not to a list."**

TriSearch's research on outreach timing: outreach typically occurs **early morning or evening** when executives check personal messages. Allow **3–4 business days between attempts**, with a maximum of three touches for initial contact. Successful outreach converts **30–40% of priority candidates** into exploratory conversations.

### Converting "happy to chat" into a placeable relationship

The conversion follows a progressive deepening model, synthesised from Savage, Crelate, and retained search methodology:

**Stage 1 — Market intelligence contact** (no mandate). Conversation framed as mutual market exchange. Goal: understand career trajectory, motivations, team structure, market perspective. Deliverable to candidate: a market insight, salary benchmark, or industry observation they find genuinely useful. CRM action: log conversation, note career motivators, current satisfaction level, next likely trigger event.

**Stage 2 — Ongoing value delivery** (between conversations). Share relevant thought leadership content — your own market analysis, curated industry news, or introductions to peers. Comment on and share their LinkedIn posts. Send personalised congratulations on promotions, company milestones, or public achievements.

**Stage 3 — Career conversation** (signal-triggered). When you detect a trigger — company restructuring, funding round, leadership change, tenure milestone, or the candidate reaching out — transition to a deeper conversation. Savage's single most powerful check-in question: **"Has anything changed since we last spoke?"** This question is the bridge between market intelligence and career intention. Begin exploring what Savage calls MTA (Motivation to Accept): what would the right next move look like? What would it take to leave?

**Stage 4 — Mandate match** (live role). When a mandate arrives matching the candidate's profile and motivations, you are reaching out to someone who knows you, trusts your market credibility, has already articulated their career drivers, and views you as an advisor. The call: "We spoke in [month] about [topic]. Something has come across my desk that maps directly to what you told me you were looking for. Can we schedule 45 minutes this week?"

Three trigger signals that indicate a candidate may be more receptive (MapX, Recruiterflow): company restructuring or leadership change; funding rounds or M&A creating new opportunities or instability; **tenure milestones** — executives at 3+ years are statistically more likely to consider moves.

## Section 4: Platform-specific tactics

### LinkedIn — profile, outreach, and content

**Profile optimisation.** Position yourself as a strategic advisor, not a transactional recruiter. Ericka Fisher (VP, JM Search) uses "Building impactful teams and empowering people to change their lives" as her headline. Stephanie Aloia (Director, Keynote Search) uses "Connector, Collaborator, Strategic Talent Partner." The formula: **[Role/Identity] | [Niche Specialism] | [Value to Candidate]**. Example: "Retained Search | AI Leadership in Creative Production | Trusted Advisor to C-Suite Talent." Avoid "recruiter" or "headhunter" as primary identifiers — these read as contingency, not retained.

The About section should be first person, narrative, not a job description. Careery.pro (2026): "The About section is where most recruiter profiles fail hardest. The typical recruiter About reads like a copy-paste from a job posting." Structure: who you serve and why it matters; your unique thesis on AI leadership in creative production; how you work (retained, consultative, confidential); a soft call to action. Sean Campbell (LinkedIn Search & Staffing): "Ensure your summary includes keywords and why you chose this path — describe your experience and career accomplishments rather than buzzwords."

**Connection request vs. InMail.** The data is clear: C-level InMail response rates are **6.98%** — the lowest seniority segment (SalesSo, 2026). However, once connected, follow-up messages have a **25–35% response chance** versus InMail's 10–25%. The recommended escalation from Leonar (2026): (1) send a blank or very brief connection request — your optimised profile does the selling; (2) if not accepted within 7 days, find their direct email and reference LinkedIn; (3) reserve InMails for the final 5% who remain silent. Botdog's analysis of 16,492 requests: **63% of acceptances happen within 24 hours, 88% within 7 days.** If no acceptance within a week, escalate.

**Content as a door-opener.** Executives who share insights regularly are **7× more likely to be approached** with new opportunities (Content Marketing Institute). LinkedIn followers are **2× more likely to respond to your InMail** (Sean Campbell/LinkedIn). Post 2–3× per week on topics your target candidates care about: AI leadership challenges in creative production, compensation trends, how AI teams are being structured, anonymous case studies. Comment meaningfully on target candidates' posts before ever sending an outreach message — this creates familiarity and warms the eventual connection request. Use content as a legitimate reason to reach out: "I'm writing a piece on how creative agencies are structuring AI leadership — would love your perspective."

### Email — finding addresses, frameworks, and compliance

**Finding executive email addresses.** Hunter.io (strong domain search; free 50 searches/month; Starter €49/month for 2,000 searches) identifies email patterns across a company. Apollo.io (275M+ contacts; free tier available; paid from $49/month) combines prospecting with email sequencing. SignalHire (850M+ profiles; 95%+ email accuracy; from $39/month) excels at finding personal emails and mobile numbers for hard-to-reach C-suite. Cognism (90%+ coverage of director-level and above in the EU; GDPR/CCPA compliant) is strongest for UK/European executive data. Manual methods: domain pattern guessing (verify with Hunter), Google dorking (`site:company.com "@company.com" "name"`), conference speaker lists, patent filings, and published papers which often include direct email addresses.

**Cold email framework for C-suite.** The Ian Koniak framework (ex-Salesforce) works well for retained search adaptation: (1) warm and personal — lead with a sincere, specific compliment; (2) shared values or struggle; (3) research-backed insight citing something specific about their company; (4) a sharp point of view with direct linkage to why you're reaching out; (5) soft CTA, strong conviction. Key data: best send time is **6–9 AM** in the recipient's timezone, **Tuesday–Thursday mornings** (Woodpecker/Clickass). Follow-ups: Day 3–4 (add new value), Day 7–8 (shorter), Day 14 (final, respectful close). **42% of all campaign replies come from follow-ups**, yet 48% of senders never send a second message (Martal.ca, 2026).

**GDPR compliance (UK).** The appropriate lawful basis for executive search is **Legitimate Interest**, not consent. The EDPB acknowledges that the power imbalance between employer and candidate makes "freely given" consent problematic (TechGDPR; Workable). You must conduct a **Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA)** with three tests: (1) Purpose — is there a genuine retained search mandate or credible research purpose? (2) Necessity — is contacting this individual necessary? (3) Balancing — do the individual's rights override your interest? Article 14 requires you to **inform candidates within 30 days** of first processing that you hold their data, why, and their rights. ICO recommends retaining unsuccessful candidate data for approximately **6 months maximum**. Maintain a recruitment-specific privacy notice on your website.

**CAN-SPAM compliance (US).** Cold email to US contacts is legal under CAN-SPAM — no prior consent required. Key requirements: accurate headers, non-deceptive subject lines, valid physical postal address, clear opt-out mechanism, honour opt-out within 10 business days. Penalties: up to **$53,088 per violating email**. Configure SPF, DKIM, and DMARC on your sending domain. Keep spam complaint rates below 0.3%.

### Phone — cold calling, gatekeepers, and voicemails

Cold calling works at C-suite level. **63% of C-level executives prefer to receive a phone call as first contact** (AI-Bees, citing DiscoverOrg). **82% of buyers accept meetings from salespeople who cold call** (RAIN Group, 2026). Unlike sales calls, a retained search call carries inherent career value — you are offering opportunity, not selling product.

**Gatekeeper navigation.** Greg Woodward (PhoneBurner) advocates the "Drastic Contrast" technique: sound fundamentally different from every other cold caller by speaking with a downward inflection (conveys confidence) rather than the upbeat, slightly nervous pitch-readiness of SDRs. Treat executive assistants as resources: "I'm hoping you can help me — I'd like to have a brief conversation with [Executive] about a confidential leadership matter. Could you help me find a good time?" Call at gatekeeper-free times: **before 8:30 AM** (executives arrive before assistants), **12:00–1:30 PM** (lunch), **after 5:30 PM** (assistants have left). **Tuesday–Thursday** are optimal days.

**Voicemail.** Danny Cahill (AccordingToDanny.com) provides the practitioner-standard framework. His evolved model uses **DICR**: Declare (who you are), Create Interest (bullet points about the opportunity), Request an Outcome (call to action). Sweet spot: **18–30 seconds**. Each voicemail left increases callback chances by **11%** (Yesware). **80% of calls go to voicemail** — treat this as your most-practised pitch. **50%+ of voicemails are now transcribed** to email/text (Cahill) — front-load value because they may read before listening.

Adapted voicemail:

> "[Name], this is [Your Name] — I run a retained search practice focused on senior AI leadership roles in creative production. I'm reaching out about a confidential [VP/Director] opportunity with a [brief descriptor]. I think your background is an excellent fit. You can reach me at [number — said slowly]. Again, that's [number]. I'll also drop you an email."

Based on the SourceWhale data (18% → 34.5% with multi-channel hyper-personalisation) and **287% engagement increase** from combining channels:

| Day | Action | Channel |
|---|---|---|

## Section 5: Practice plan for 20 research conversations

### Before each conversation — 60–90 minute preparation

**Candidate intelligence (30 minutes).** Full LinkedIn profile review: current role, tenure, reporting line, team size, career arc. Company context: growing, contracting, restructuring, recently acquired, or undergoing AI transformation? Publications, conference appearances, patents — have they spoken at SIGGRAPH, VES events, NAB, or Curious Refuge? Social signals: recent LinkedIn posts, podcast appearances. Mutual connections: identify any shared contacts who could provide context or a warm introduction. Trigger events (per MapX methodology): company restructuring, leadership changes, funding rounds, M&A, **time-in-role 4+ years**.

**Industry context (15 minutes).** Latest news on their employer. Competitive landscape: what are peer companies doing with AI in creative production? Prepare one **"insight gift"** — a specific data point, trend observation, or development you can share that demonstrates niche expertise.

**Conversation objective (15 minutes).** Define whether this person is a potential candidate, a source who can refer others, or both (per AESC: distinguish between candidate recruitment calls and candidate sourcing calls). Prepare three tailored questions specific to their background. Set a "minimum viable outcome": even if they are never a candidate, what referral, market insight, or relationship deposit can you secure?

### The conversation itself — the four-phase framework

**Phase 1 — The Opening (2–3 minutes).** Lou Adler's principle: "Don't sell the job, sell the conversation." Since these are pre-mandate research conversations, position as a credible niche specialist conducting market intelligence:

> "[Name], this is [Your Name] from FPZ CreativAI. I appreciate you taking the call. I'm a specialist headhunter focused exclusively on the intersection of AI and creative production — VFX, virtual production, generative content. I'm currently mapping the leadership landscape in this space and your name keeps coming up as someone doing genuinely interesting work at [Company]. I'm not calling with a specific role today — I'm conducting research into how AI leadership is evolving across the sector, and I'd value fifteen minutes of your perspective. Would now work, or would another time suit better?"

**Phase 2 — The Exploration (12–18 minutes).** Adapt Clockwork Recruiting's QIA framework (Qualified, Interested, Available) to **PIR: Perspective, Intelligence, Referrals.** Start with their world: "Before I get into what I'm seeing across the market, I'd love to hear from you. How is AI actually changing the creative leadership function at [Company]?"

Three question tracks to select from:

*Track 1 — Their role and challenges (Perspective):* "What does your AI team structure look like today versus two years ago?" / "How are you balancing the tension between technical AI talent and creative production instincts?"

*Track 2 — Market intelligence:* "Where are the real skill gaps in AI leadership within VFX/virtual production right now?" / "If you were hiring your own successor, what profile would you look for that barely exists today?" / "Which companies are furthest ahead in integrating AI into their creative pipeline?"

*Track 3 — Network and referral seeding:* "Who else in the space is doing work you admire at this intersection?" / "Are there events or communities where you find the best thinking on this?" / "If you were advising someone entering this niche as a search specialist, who would you say I absolutely must speak to?"

The **80/20 rule**: they talk 80%, you talk 20%. AESC Best Practices: "Listen to the Candidate and take copious notes. Over the course of the call, the Candidate will tell the Associate a lot about themselves, including what they like and want professionally."

**Phase 3 — The Soft Assessment (woven throughout, 3–5 minutes).** Note but do not explicitly probe: career trajectory and ambition signals (are they hinting at being stuck, under-challenged, or ready for something new?); cultural markers (collaborative vs. hierarchical, risk appetite); mobility indicators (mentions of family, location, equity vesting, satisfaction cues).

**Phase 4 — The Close (3–5 minutes).** Achieve three things: express genuine gratitude, offer reciprocal value, establish a specific reason for re-contact.

> "[Name], this has been incredibly valuable. You've given me a much sharper picture of how [specific insight] is playing out in practice. I'd like to do three things if you're open to it.
>
> First, I'm compiling a short briefing on AI leadership trends in creative production — compensation benchmarks, emerging role structures, where talent is moving. I'd like to send you a copy when it's ready. Would that be useful?
>
> Second, as I continue these conversations, I may come across insights or introductions relevant to you — would you be open to me reaching out?
>
> Third, I'd value keeping you in my network. Can I connect on LinkedIn?
>
> And if you think of anyone else I should be speaking to, I'd welcome the introduction."

The **briefing document** creates a concrete, value-driven reason for follow-up. The **permission to re-contact** converts a one-off call into an ongoing relationship. The **referral ask** is positioned as an afterthought, reducing pressure.

### Post-conversation follow-up protocol

**Within 2 hours:** Complete CRM entry — contact details, key insights, assessment notes (tier A/B/C), referral names, trigger signals, follow-up commitments. Send thank-you email:

> Subject: Great speaking with you — [specific topic]
>
> [Name],
>
> Thank you for taking the time today. Your perspective on [specific point they made] was genuinely insightful and has shaped how I'm thinking about the AI leadership landscape in creative production.
>
> As promised, I'll share the market briefing once compiled. If any of the people or trends we discussed prompt further thoughts, I'd welcome the conversation.
>
> I've sent a LinkedIn connection request. Looking forward to staying in touch.

**Same day:** LinkedIn connection request with personal note referencing the conversation.

**Within 5 working days:** Contact any referred individuals, mentioning the referrer by name. Send a brief note to the referrer confirming you connected.

**Week 4–6:** Send the promised market briefing document. Include a personal note referencing something specific from the conversation.

**Month 2–3:** Share a relevant article, event announcement (SIGGRAPH, NAB, VES), or industry insight. Brief, personal, non-transactional.

**Ongoing:** Set alerts for job changes, company announcements, or conference appearances. Re-engage with: "Saw you moved to [New Company] — congratulations. I'd love to hear how the AI leadership landscape looks from your new vantage point."

**Qualitative signals to monitor:** Are candidates sharing information they wouldn't share with a generalist recruiter? Are referrals to other senior people coming unprompted? Are candidates asking about your work and perspective? Are conversations naturally extending beyond 30 minutes?

### Strategic sequencing — who to call first

**Conversations 1–5 (Week 1–2): "Calibration."** Start with the most accessible, mid-seniority contacts — Director-level individuals at mid-tier studios or post-production houses where barriers are lowest. These calls calibrate your language, test your questions, and refine your framework. Lower stakes if you make early mistakes. Directors often have broad visibility into VP/C-suite leaders and provide upward referrals. Greg Savage's "Ten seconds of courage" principle (January 2026 blog) applies here: "Do your planning, consider your strategy, and then, as the nerves and doubt surface, tell yourself that all you need is ten seconds of courage to start."

**Conversations 6–10 (Week 2–3): "Intelligence deepening."** VP-level leaders at larger creative production companies, studios with established AI initiatives, and 1–2 adjacent-niche leaders (gaming, broadcast, immersive media). You now have five conversations' worth of insights and can reference patterns: "I've been speaking with several AI leaders in the space and a consistent theme is [X] — does that resonate?" Push for C-suite introductions and sharpen compensation benchmarking data.

**Conversations 11–15 (Week 3–4): "Authority conversations."** C-suite and senior VP leaders at the most prominent organisations — major VFX studios, leading AI-native creative tools companies, heads of emerging AI divisions at studios/streamers. Include 1–2 high-profile individuals from VES leadership, SIGGRAPH programme committees, or Curious Refuge faculty. By now you have 10 conversations' worth of market intelligence and can speak with genuine authority.

**Conversations 16–20 (Week 4–5): "Network reinforcement."** Second-round conversations with Tier A contacts from earlier tranches; referral contacts you haven't yet reached; deliberate gap-fills (APAC-based leaders, startup founders, non-obvious talent from adjacent sectors). Second conversations with strong contacts deepen relationships into genuine advisory partnerships. AESC notes: "Research continues to develop potential Candidates throughout the entire Search Process."

### The market briefing — your signature deliverable

Across all 20 conversations, you are simultaneously building a market briefing document. This serves as your follow-up value delivery, your business development tool for prospective clients, your credential when approaching retained search mandates, and evidence of your niche authority. Structure it as: (1) executive summary on the state of AI leadership in creative production; (2) emerging role archetypes; (3) compensation benchmarks (anonymised, aggregated); (4) talent supply observations — where leaders are concentrated, mobility patterns; (5) key skills gaps; (6) recommendations for organisations building AI leadership capability. This single document, built from 20 real conversations, becomes the most credible piece of collateral your practice can produce.

## Conclusion

The operational thesis is straightforward: **retained search at the senior level is a relationship business dressed in process clothing.** The process (market mapping, multi-channel sequencing, CRM discipline) exists to systematise relationship-building, not replace it. For a solo boutique founder entering a niche of 200–500 global candidates, the maths are favourable — 20 well-executed research conversations, each generating 2–3 referrals, covers a significant fraction of the addressable market within 5 weeks.

Three insights cut across all the practitioner content reviewed. First, the research-conversation frame is not a tactic but a genuine operating model — the best retained search practitioners are always in research mode, and every conversation generates intelligence that compounds over time. Second, the phone is non-negotiable at this level despite being the channel most practitioners avoid; Savage, Adler, and Vlastelica all converge on this. Third, the gap between a boutique founder with deep niche knowledge and a SHREK firm with a global brand is far narrower at the individual candidate level than most people assume — what matters is whether the person on the other end of the call can teach the candidate something about their own market that they didn't already know.
