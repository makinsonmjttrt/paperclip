# FPZ executive search: a viable niche with no credible competitor

**The single most commercially significant finding in this research is a complete competitive vacuum.** No recruitment or executive search firm globally operates with credibility at the intersection of AI leadership and creative production. AI search firms (True Search, Harnham, Digitalent) recruit ML engineers and CAIOs for tech companies but have zero creative production context. Creative search firms (The Talent Business, Grace Blue) place CCOs and agency CEOs but have no AI expertise. VFX recruiters (PXL Talent, Talent Outpost) understand production floors but operate contingent-only and lack AI knowledge. FourPointZero's six years of creative production network depth, combined with a retained search methodology, would occupy genuinely uncontested territory. The addressable market is real — **DNEG alone invested $225M in AI in 2024-25** — but narrow enough that first-mover advantage is decisive. This report provides the evidence base for a go/no-go decision.

## 1. The AI leadership roles actually being hired right now

**The most commercially useful insight: creative production's AI leadership layer is forming right now, unevenly, and most companies haven't made their first senior AI hire yet.** This is greenfield territory for a search firm.

Across five sub-sectors, clear structural patterns emerged in how organisations hire AI leadership:

**VFX studios** are the most active sub-sector for standalone AI leadership roles, but even here, only DNEG has a genuinely new AI-specific executive title. DNEG posted for a **Head of Machine Learning** — a brand-new role scoped to multimodal generative models, reporting into their $200M-funded Brahma AI division. This is the first time a major VFX studio has created a dedicated senior AI leader outside the traditional CTO function. Cinesite created a **TechX unit** with a Generative AI Lead (Andrew McNamara) and a Generative AI 2D Supervisor. Digital Domain's AI work (Masquerade facial capture, Charlatan face-swapping) sits under CTO **Hanno Basse**, while ILM's AI strategy is absorbed into SVP **Rob Bredow's** existing remit. Framestore, Weta FX, and Scanline VFX showed no evidence of standalone AI leadership roles. **MPC (Technicolor) began closing operations in early 2026; Jellyfish Pictures suspended operations in March 2025.** Neither is hiring.

**Creative software companies** have the most mature AI leadership structures. Adobe leads with **Alexandru Costin** (VP of Generative AI), **Emily McReynolds** (Head of Global AI Strategy), and **Meera Srinivasan** (Sr. Director, AI/GenAI/Agentic Solutions). Autodesk is building its **Flow Studio** team (the renamed Wonder Dynamics acquisition) under a Senior Director role. Epic Games has **Vladimir Mastilović** (VP, Digital Humans Technology). Foundry and SideFX embed AI development within engineering management, without dedicated AI executive titles.

**AI-native creative platforms** don't hire "AI leaders" because they *are* AI companies. Runway ($5.3B valuation, 140 employees), Pika, Luma AI, and Midjourney are founder/CTO-led. Their hiring needs are for experienced operating executives who can scale — COOs, GTM leaders, VP Engineering — not "Head of AI." Runway's announcement of "new leaders to support our next phase of growth" (February 2026) signals precisely this.

**Creative agencies** lag significantly. The only confirmed agency-level Chief AI Officer appointment was **James Nicholas Kinney** at INVNT (December 2025). BBDO, Wieden+Kennedy, Droga5, Uncommon, and Mother showed zero public AI leadership roles. AI hiring in agencies happens at holding company level (WPP, Publicis, Omnicom), not individual agency level.

**Production companies** (RSA, Anonymous Content, Partizan, MJZ) showed **zero evidence** of AI executive hiring at any level. This sector remains untouched.

### Established vs emerging titles

| Status | Titles | Where found |
|--------|--------|------------|
| **Established** (pre-2024) | CTO, SVP Creative Director, VP Engineering | Digital Domain, ILM, Autodesk, Epic Games |
| **Emerging** (post-2024) | Head of Machine Learning, Generative AI Lead, VP Generative AI, Global Chief AI Officer, Head of Global AI Strategy | DNEG, Cinesite, Adobe, INVNT |
| **Not yet created** | Chief AI Officer at VFX studio, Head of AI at creative agency | No VFX studio or named agency has filled this |

### Structural differences by sub-sector

VFX studios house AI under existing CTOs or create standalone R&D units (Cinesite's TechX). Software companies distribute AI across product, strategy, and marketing leadership with multiple VP/Director roles. AI-native platforms have flat, founder-led structures where AI is implicit in every role. Agencies absorb AI into existing creative technology functions. This structural variation has direct implications for search briefs — the same "Head of AI" title means fundamentally different things across sub-sectors.

## 3. How creative production companies actually buy senior talent

**The most commercially useful insight: retained executive search is rare in creative production, which means the market is underserved but also requires education. The critical opening is that AI leadership hiring breaks established patterns — it demands cross-industry sourcing that internal TA and network-based hiring cannot deliver.**

Major VFX studios default to **internal talent acquisition supplemented by personal networks**. Framestore maintains a dedicated recruitment team with a Head of Recruitment. ILM routes through Disney's corporate TA. DNEG has used specialist VFX recruiters (Talent Outpost confirmed via testimonial from DNEG's VP of Animation Technology). The VFX industry is small enough that most senior hiring happens through SIGGRAPH, VES events, and personal referrals.

When external recruitment is used, it is overwhelmingly **contingent or project-based**. Specialist VFX recruiters (PXL Talent, Talent Outpost, DAX, Motus VFX, Rachelle Lewis Talent) operate at HOD and senior production staff level. Only **PXL Talent** and **Talent Outpost** explicitly claim executive search capability in VFX, with Talent Outpost offering a tiered model including retained search (claiming 93% 24-month retention rate).

For true C-suite appointments at major studios, companies typically use **parent company TA** (Disney for ILM, Fimalac for Framestore) or **generalist entertainment search firms** (Korn Ferry, Spencer Stuart). At creative agencies, **Grace Blue** and **The Talent Business** handle CEO/CCO appointments via retained search.

**The AI leadership hiring gap is structural.** The "Head of AI at a VFX studio" role requires a candidate who spans two separate talent markets — AI/ML researchers from tech and senior production leaders from creative. Internal TA teams and VFX networks can access one pool but not the other. AI search firms can source ML talent but cannot assess creative production credibility. This structural mismatch is the precise commercial opportunity for FPZ.

### Named search firms currently active in adjacent territory

- **Grace Blue/Sinecure.ai** — retained creative leadership search, acquired by AI platform January 2024
- **The Talent Business** — retained creative executive search, 8 global offices, 400+ placements/year, Tech & Innovation practice
- **PXL Talent** — VFX/animation executive search and recruitment, most credible VFX specialist globally
- **Talent Outpost** — VFX/animation recruitment with retained exec search tier, DNEG client
- **Searchlight** — media/entertainment search, sponsors BAFTA Guru Live
- **True Search** — dedicated AI practice (10+ years), no creative production expertise
- **Harnham** — data/AI recruitment specialist, no creative production expertise
- **Scion Executive Search** — media/TV/film, has gaming/animation staffing arm

No firm was identified that specifically services the intersection of AI leadership and creative production at retained executive search level.

## 4. Seventeen competitors mapped, and the gap nobody fills

**The most commercially useful insight: the competitive landscape splits cleanly into four quadrants — creative search firms (no AI expertise), AI search firms (no creative context), VFX recruiters (no exec search capability), and generalist search firms (no specialist depth). Nobody occupies the centre.**

### Creative executive search firms (understand creativity, not AI)

**Grace Blue** positions as "the standard in executive search for consumer, media, marketing." Its Sinecure.ai acquisition (January 2024) gives an "AI" narrative, but this is about their search *technology*, not AI industry expertise. Practices serve agencies, consumer brands, PE, sports, and media — zero creative production/VFX focus. **Threat level: MEDIUM** — the Sinecure.ai brand adjacency could confuse clients, but they lack production depth.

**The Talent Business** is the closest structural competitor. They claim "the world leader in executive search for businesses fueled by innovation and creativity" and have a Tech & Innovation practice. Their thought leadership (Cannes Lions, The Drum, AdAge) is strong. But their creative focus is advertising/marketing leadership — not VFX, post-production, or creative technology pipelines. They have **no published content on AI transformation of creative production**. **Threat level: HIGH** — most likely firm to expand into this space, but would need 2-3 years to build credible production networks.

**Major Players** is the UK's largest creative talent agency with 30+ years and 500,000+ talent pool. They have zero AI content on their website, no retained search methodology, and their "production" specialism means project management and studio management — not VFX or creative production. **Threat level: NONE** for exec search.

### AI/tech search firms (understand AI, not creative production)

**True Search** has a dedicated AI practice led by Mark Bai with 10+ years' history, placing generative AI, deep learning, and computer vision executives. They are the 6th largest exec search firm. But they have zero creative production or entertainment industry expertise — their consumer practice serves CPG/retail. **Threat level: MEDIUM** — infrastructure to pivot exists, but no creative network.

**Harnham** claims to be the "world's biggest data and AI talent recruitment company" with exec search capability. No creative, VFX, or entertainment specialisation whatsoever. **Threat level: LOW.**

### VFX specialist recruiters (understand production, not exec search)

**PXL Talent** (Sophie Maydon, London) is the most credible VFX specialist, attending SIGGRAPH and World VFX Day. They claim exec search capability, but are a small boutique without AI expertise. **Threat level: MEDIUM** — most likely to compete if they added AI knowledge.

**Talent Outpost** (Tiffany Feeney, US-based) has strong studio-floor credentials (DreamWorks, Blue Sky alumni) and a retained search tier. No AI positioning. **Threat level: LOW-MEDIUM.**

### Generalist search firms (too large, too broad)

**Korn Ferry** has a Media & Entertainment practice (recently hired Amanda Pitt from Grace Blue for UK/EMEA). They handle CEO searches at Disney/Netflix scale but would not typically engage at VFX studio level. **Spencer Stuart, Heidrick & Struggles, Russell Reynolds, and Egon Zehnder** all have TMT or media practices, but operate at enterprise level and lack VFX/creative production depth. **Threat level: LOW** — these firms serve a different market tier.

### The genuine gap

**Zero recruitment firms contribute thought leadership on AI's transformation of creative production.** Aquent publishes content about AI skills creatives need, but from a staffing perspective. Grace Blue mentions AI in relation to media owners. The industry conversation about AI in creative production (in VFX Voice, The Wrap, The Drum) features studio executives, researchers, and journalists — not a single recruiter. The entire thought leadership podium is empty.

## 5. A talent pool of roughly 50–250 people, thin but searchable

**The most commercially useful insight: the candidate pool is small enough to justify retained search fees (you can't find these people through job boards or contingent processes) but large enough to sustain a practice if scoped across adjacent verticals.**

### Pool size estimation

The global pool of people who combine senior AI/ML expertise with creative production understanding breaks into three tiers:

- **Tier 1** (could immediately fill "Head of AI at ILM/DNEG"): approximately **50–75 people globally**
- **Tier 2** (could grow into the role within 12 months): approximately **75–125 people**
- **Tier 3** (adjacent talent from pure tech AI requiring industry adaptation): approximately **200–500 people**

This estimate is triangulated from named individuals at major studios, conference speaker lists (SIGGRAPH, FMX, VIEW), AI-native creative company leadership rosters, and the known size of VFX/creative tech workforces. A 2024 study mapped **42,000+ VFX professionals across 175 studios** — senior AI/ML leadership represents a fraction of a percent of this total.

### Named talent mapping (publicly available)

Key individuals identified include **Joe Marks** (CTO, Weta FX — career path: CMU ML Center → Disney Research → Weta), **Danny Cohen-Or** (Chief Scientist, MARZ — Tel Aviv University professor recruited for AI/face-editing), **Hanno Basse** (CTO, Digital Domain), **Felipe Pesantez** (ML Engineer spanning DNEG, Framestore, MPC, Weta), **Aaron Hertzmann** (Principal Scientist, Adobe Research, SIGGRAPH 2025 keynote), and **Mike Haley** (SVP Research, Autodesk, overseeing Flow Studio). AI-native creative company leaders include Runway's founding team, Pika's **Demi Guo and Chenlin Meng** (Stanford/Google Brain alumni), and MARZ's co-founders.

The dominant career path into these roles is **academic ML research → corporate R&D → creative industry**, with a secondary path of **VFX pipeline engineering → ML specialisation → AI leadership**. Very few people have made the reverse journey from creative production into AI leadership.

### Candidate behaviour and response rates

LinkedIn InMail response rates at C-level average only **6.98%** — the lowest of any seniority tier. Technology sector rates run higher at **22–28%** due to greater digital comfort, with best-in-class campaigns reaching 35–42% through personalisation. Messages under 400 characters see a **22% response boost**. Multi-step sequences with 2-3 follow-ups can push response rates to 20–30%.

**41% of engineering leaders are currently looking to leave their role** (CTO Craft 2024 survey). However, creative production cannot typically match big tech compensation. Career decision drivers for this population, ranked: access to cutting-edge technology, creative freedom, company/project prestige, equity upside, team quality, and remote/hybrid flexibility (98% of tech leaders work hybrid or remote).

### Geographic clusters

**London** (DNEG HQ, Framestore HQ, Foundry, Flawless), **Los Angeles** (ILM, DNEG, Digital Domain, Framestore, Sony Imageworks), **Vancouver** (ILM, DNEG, MPC, Image Engine), **San Francisco** (ILM Presidio, Pika, Luma AI), **New York** (Runway, Framestore), **Toronto** (MARZ), **Montreal** (DNEG, Cinesite — though talent is dispersing after Quebec's May 2024 VFX tax credit cap), and **Wellington** (Weta FX). MARZ operates remote-first across 8+ countries. Net tech talent inflows to Europe halved from 52,000 (2022) to 26,000 (2024), signalling ongoing Europe-to-US brain drain.

### UK vs US differences that affect search execution

UK non-competes are weakly enforced and may soon be capped at 3 months. California bans non-competes entirely. UK C-suite notice periods typically run **6–12 months** with garden leave — meaning UK hires have a significantly longer time-to-start than US equivalents, where at-will employment enables 2–4 week transitions. This has direct implications for search timelines and client expectation-setting.

## 6. Eight client types ranked by search viability

**The most commercially useful insight: the three highest-viability client types are advertising holding companies (WPP, Publicis, Dentsu), creative/VFX SaaS platforms (Adobe, Autodesk, Foundry), and AI-native creative companies post-Series C (Runway, ElevenLabs, Luma AI). Major VFX studios rank fifth due to historically low search-firm usage, despite high AI urgency.**

### Tier 1: Highest viability

**Advertising holding companies** (WPP, Publicis, Dentsu, Omnicom) have exceptional financial capacity, extreme AI urgency, and are established retained search buyers. WPP's Elevate28 plan reinvests **£500M into AI-enabled capabilities**. Publicis committed **€300M over 3 years** to its CoreAI platform. These companies have procurement-managed PSLs of search firms. The challenge is complex procurement and preference for established firms — entry may require targeting individual agency brands rather than group-level. **Viability: HIGH. Risk: long sales cycles, PSL barriers.**

**Creative/VFX SaaS platforms** combine tech company budgets with existential AI urgency. Adobe (market cap $230B+) launched **Firefly Foundry** (October 2025) with dedicated teams of AI scientists and creative specialists — precisely the hybrid leadership a FPZ search would target. Autodesk (~$60B market cap) is building its Flow Studio team. Foundry (owned by $60B Roper Technologies) is releasing Nuke 17.0 with major AI features. Mid-size platforms like Foundry and SideFX are **highly accessible to boutique specialists** — their VFX/creative context requirements give a specialist firm genuine competitive advantage over generalist tech search firms. **Viability: HIGH. Best immediate targets: Foundry, SideFX, Maxon.**

**AI-native creative companies** post-Series C are flush with cash and scaling rapidly. Runway raised **$315M in February 2026** at a $5.3B valuation, targeting $300M ARR. ElevenLabs raised **$500M at $11B valuation**, eyeing IPO. Luma AI closed a **$900M Series C** in November 2025. These companies need experienced operating executives who understand creative production workflows — exactly the candidate profile FPZ can source. However, they typically hire through VC networks and Silicon Valley search firms (Riviera Partners, True Search). FPZ's differentiator would be sourcing leaders who understand creative production, which tech search firms cannot do. **Viability: HIGH with caveats. Best targets: Runway, ElevenLabs, Luma AI.**

### Tier 2: Strong viability

**Broadcasters and streamers** have enormous resources and accelerating AI urgency. Netflix acquired **InterPositive** (Ben Affleck's AI filmmaking startup) for reportedly up to $600M. Disney invested **$1B in OpenAI** and launched a 200+ character partnership with Sora. Netflix's SEC filing (Q4 2025) explicitly states it is "very well positioned to leverage advances in AI." BBC and Channel 4 have active AI strategies. These companies use retained search regularly, but tend to engage major firms (Heidrick, Spencer Stuart). **BBC and Channel 4 are the most accessible UK-based targets** and may be more likely to engage a specialist boutique. **Viability: HIGH. Risk: preference for large search firms for major hires.**

**Major VFX studios** have the highest AI urgency and the deepest relevance to FPZ's network, but historically resist paying premium retained search fees. VFX operates on tight margins. DNEG is the exception — **$2B+ valuation, $200M AI investment, actively building AI leadership**. Framestore is well-resourced under Fimalac ownership. ILM benefits from Disney's resources. The commercial argument: AI leadership hiring breaks the pattern of network-based VFX hiring because candidates span two separate talent markets (AI/ML and creative production). Internal TA cannot source both. **Viability: MEDIUM-HIGH. #1 specific target: DNEG.**

### Tier 3: Limited viability

**Games studios** (EA, Ubisoft, CD Projekt Red) have resources and moderate AI urgency, but their AI needs are broader than creative production — procedural generation, NPC behaviour, automated testing. The cinematic production angle is a subset. **Viability: MEDIUM.**

**Creative agencies** individually (BBDO, W+K, Uncommon, Mother) have limited budgets for retained search, moderate AI urgency, and hire through industry networks. AI hiring happens at holding company level. **Viability: MEDIUM-LOW.**

**Mid-size VFX/animation studios** (Blue Zoo, Aardman, Outpost VFX) cannot typically afford £50K+ retained search fees and hire AI capability through internal promotion or network referrals. **Viability: LOW.**

## 7. The positioning claim nobody else can make

**The most commercially useful insight: zero recruitment firms are contributing thought leadership on AI's transformation of creative production. The entire industry conversation features studio executives, researchers, and journalists — never a recruiter. The first search firm to occupy this space credibly will own it.**

### What competitors actually say

**The Talent Business:** "The world leader in executive search for businesses fueled by innovation and creativity." Strong thought leadership on creative leadership broadly (Cannes Lions, AdAge, The Drum). Zero content on AI transformation of creative production.

**Grace Blue:** "We set the standard in executive search in the consumer, media, marketing and communications sector." The Sinecure.ai acquisition gives an AI narrative, but this is AI-as-search-tool, not AI-as-industry-expertise. Zero creative production or VFX focus.

**Major Players:** "The original marketing and creative talent agency." Zero AI content anywhere on their site. Their "production" specialism is creative services production management, not VFX/creative production.

**Aquent:** The only major creative recruiter talking about AI, but their positioning is "creatives need AI skills" and "we use AI to recruit" — not "we understand how AI transforms creative production leadership."

**AI search firms (True Search, Harnham, Digitalent, Talentfoot):** All recruit ML engineers and CAIOs for tech/enterprise. Zero creative production context. They find people who build AI tools — not people who deploy AI to transform how creative content gets made.

**VFX recruiters (PXL Talent, Talent Outpost):** Deep production-floor understanding but zero AI positioning, no published thought leadership, and limited exec search methodology.

### The white space is structural, not cosmetic

The gap is not merely a marketing opportunity — it reflects a genuine capability gap. No firm can currently:
- Assess candidates for both AI/ML technical depth and creative production credibility
- Source across both talent pools (tech AI researchers and creative production leaders)
- Advise clients on how to structure AI leadership roles that work within creative production culture
- Navigate the cultural mismatch between AI engineers from tech companies and creative production environments

### The single most ownable positioning statement

**"The executive search practice built from inside creative production, for the leaders navigating AI transformation of how creative work gets made."**

This works because: "Built from inside creative production" differentiates from AI search firms parachuting in and from creative search firms who operate from an advertising perspective. "Navigating AI transformation" differentiates from creative recruiters ignoring AI entirely. "How creative work gets made" anchors specifically to production processes — the exact intersection nobody occupies.

### Proof points that make it defensible

FPZ needs five things to make this positioning credible:

1. **Track record evidence** — six years of named placements in creative production companies, even if pre-AI roles, proving network depth
2. **Published thought leadership** — 3-4 articles in VFX Voice, The Drum, or Creative Review on AI leadership challenges in creative production (the podium is currently empty)
3. **A proprietary assessment framework** — a named, documented methodology for evaluating candidates at the AI/creative production intersection
4. **An inaugural market report** — "State of AI Leadership in Creative Production" based on 10-15 executive interviews; nothing comparable exists
5. **Industry advisory endorsements** — quotes from studio heads or VFX supervisors validating FPZ's understanding of the AI transformation challenge
