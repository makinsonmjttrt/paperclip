# Signature Moves: Martyn Makinson
*Last updated: 2026-04-02*

Recurring structures and phrases that feel authentically his. These are the patterns that make a post sound like Martyn and not like a LinkedIn ghostwriter.

---

## Move 1: The Contrast Close

**What it is:** A short claim followed immediately by "not [inferior alternative]." One sentence. No lecture.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "Real salary data from completed mandates, not market estimates."
> "How these businesses actually run, not just who works in them."

**The pattern:**
> "[What I provide], not [what everyone else provides]."

**Rules for using it:**
- Use maximum once per post.
- The contrast must be specific. "Real expertise, not guesswork" is too vague. "Compensation data from completed mandates, not industry surveys" is right.
- Never follow it with an explanation. The contrast does the work.

---

## Move 2: The Pre-Emptive Candour Signal

**What it is:** Voluntarily disqualifying himself before the client has to. States a limitation or condition before being asked. This is his most distinctive tone marker.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "I'd rather tell you early than waste your time."
> "I'll be candid about which opportunities are worth your time and which aren't."
> "No obligation to be actively looking to have a conversation."

**The pattern:**
> "[I'll tell you the truth], even if that truth is [not commercially convenient for me]."

**Why it works:** Signals confidence, not weakness. Only someone who doesn't need to oversell says this.

**Rules for using it:**
- Use maximum once per post.
- Must be specific. "I'll be honest with you" is too vague. "If your brief isn't positioned to attract the people you actually want, I'll tell you that upfront" is specific.
- Never pair it with a follow-up sales push. Let it breathe.

---

## Move 3: The Quiet Boast

**What it is:** A credential stated plainly, immediately followed by a constraint that adds more credibility than the credential itself.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "Over 300 appointments in seven years, most of the board-level work under NDA. Happy to talk about the ones we can."

**The pattern:**
> "[Impressive number/claim]. [Reason you can't fully verify it]. [Low-pressure invitation]."

**Why it works:** The NDA clause signals that the real work is bigger than what's visible. The "happy to talk about the ones we can" is an open door, not a pressure.

**Rules for using it:**
- Use sparingly. Once per bio or once per introductory-type post.
- The constraint must be genuine (NDA, confidentiality, client request), not manufactured.
- The invitation at the end must be genuinely low-pressure.

---

## Move 4: The Short Declarative Break

**What it is:** A statement so short it functions as a paragraph by itself. Used after a medium-length explanation to land the conclusion.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "Retained and engaged mandates only."
> "I don't advertise them."
> "It's moved on a bit since then."

**The pattern:** Say the point in 4-6 words. Full stop. New paragraph.

**Rules for using it:**
- The short declarative lands a point — it doesn't introduce one.
- It follows the explanation, never precedes it.
- Use 1-2 per post, not more.

---

## Move 5: The Conditional Pivot

**What it is:** Segments the audience mid-content, then speaks directly to each segment.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "If you're making a senior hire, I'll give you an honest read on the talent market..."
> "If you're a senior leader considering a move, I work with a small number of candidates at any one time..."

**The pattern:**
> "If you're [audience A], [direct value statement for A]."
> "If you're [audience B], [direct value statement for B]."

**Rules for using it:**
- Use at the end of a post when there are two clear audience types (client and candidate).
- Maximum two audience segments.
- Each segment gets one sentence of value. No elaboration.
- Never phrase it as a question: "Are you making a senior hire?" is not his register.

---

## Move 6: The Industry Insider Frame

**What it is:** Positions the observation as coming from inside the industry, not from watching it.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "Seven years in this industry means we understand how these businesses actually run, not just who works in them."
> "From what I see in briefs week to week..." (editorial brief voice note, not yet in published copy)

**The pattern:**
> "[Time/relationship in the industry] means [specific thing I know that outsiders don't]."

**Rules for using it:**
- The insider observation must be genuinely specific, not generic ("I know the industry well").
- Pairs well with Move 1 (contrast close).
- Never claim to be an insider and then make an observation that any outsider could make.

---

## Move 7: The One-Line Human Moment

**What it is:** A single personal observation, usually at the end of a bio or post, that reveals something human without performing vulnerability.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "I've been interested in technology since my first Commodore VIC-20. It's moved on a bit since then."

**The pattern:**
> "[Genuine personal reference]. [Understated observation about how far it's come]."

**Rules for using it:**
- Use once per bio or once per longer content piece. Not in short posts.
- Must be genuinely his own reference (Commodore VIC-20 is his history, not a borrowed detail).
- The understatement ("it's moved on a bit") is the voice marker — never inflate it into "how extraordinary the journey has been."

---

## Move 8: The Problem-Framing Statement

**What it is:** States the client's problem in their language before offering the solution. No preamble. Goes straight to the problem as they'd articulate it.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "These are inaugural appointments. There's no internal benchmark for what good looks like, the global talent pool is limited, and the cost of a misaligned hire at this level is significant."

**The pattern:**
> "[The problem stated flatly]. [Why it's different from other hiring problems]."

**Rules for using it:**
- The problem must be stated from the client's perspective, not from Martyn's perspective.
- Never follow the problem statement immediately with a pitch. Let the problem land.
- Use for posts targeting hiring decision-makers. Not relevant for candidate-facing content.

---

## Move 9: The Flat Opening Statement

**What it is:** The very first sentence of a post. A plain declarative about what he does, what happened, or what he sees in the market. No rhetorical device. No question. No build-up.

**Examples from his writing:**
> "I place C-suite and senior leadership into creative production businesses."
> "AI skills have overtaken every other category in creative job postings." (from editorial brief)

**The pattern:**
> "[Subject] [verb] [object]." Full stop.

**Rules for using it:**
- The opening sentence should be the most specific thing in the post.
- If the opening sentence could apply to any recruiter in any sector, rewrite it.
- No emotional language in the opening. Emotion (if any) comes later.

## Structural Templates

**Template A: Most Common (Observation → Insight → Close)**
```
[Line 1: Flat opening statement — specific data or observation]

[Lines 2-4: Explain what it means from inside the market]

[Lines 5-6: Why this matters to the reader specifically]

[Line 7: Short declarative close OR conditional pivot to audience]

[Optional line 8: Low-pressure invitation or specific intelligence question]
```
Total: 100-200 words.

**Template B: Case Study (Situation → Shift → Lesson)**
```
[Line 1: Time-anchored situation stated plainly]

[Lines 2-4: How the situation evolved, stated without drama]

[Line 5-6: Short declarative summary of what changed — Triple Declarative works here]

[Line 7: Bridge to broader market point ("Not a one-off.")]

[Lines 8-9: The generalised insight and who it applies to]

[Optional: Specific closing question]
```
Total: 150-200 words.

**Template C: Intelligence List (Observation → Numbered Items → Source)**
```
[Line 1: "[Number] [things] I [see/track] [context]"]

[Line 2: Brief source framing ("I track this through briefs and offers...")]

[Items 1-5: Bold label + 1-2 sentences each]

[Closing: Third-party source that supports the pattern]

[Optional: Specific closing question]
```
Total: 200-250 words. Use sparingly — not every week.

## Move 10: The Triple Declarative

**What it is:** Three consecutive ultra-short sentences, each one landing a separate but related point. Functions as a rhythm break that makes the reader stop.

**Example from Writer agent post (day-06, secondary evidence):**
> "Different role. Different seniority. Different budget."

**The pattern:**
> "[Short noun/adjective phrase]. [Short noun/adjective phrase]. [Short noun/adjective phrase]."

**Why it works:** The repetition of short stops creates emphasis without exclamation marks. It's the written equivalent of counting on your fingers.

**Rules for using it:**
- Maximum once per post.
- All three items must be parallel in structure.
- Each item must be a genuinely separate point, not a synonym.
- Do not follow it with an explanation. It is the conclusion, not the setup.

---

## Move 11: The Shifting Brief (Case Study Opener)

**What it is:** Opens with a real client situation stated in plain past tense, then walks through how it evolved. No dramatisation. The story reveals the insight rather than being told the insight.

**Example from Writer agent post (day-06, secondary evidence):**
> "Last quarter we ran a search that changed three times before we placed anyone."

**The pattern:**
> "[Time reference] [we/I] [did the thing]. [What we expected.] [What actually happened.] [What we learned.]"

**Rules for using it:**
- Use for posts where market intelligence is best shown through a real situation.
- The story must end with a specific, generalizable insight, not a generic takeaway.
- "Not a one-off." is an acceptable bridge from the story to the broader point. Short. No explanation needed.
- Do not use for posts targeting candidates — the "we" positions Martyn as practitioner, not peer.

---

## Move 12: The Numbered Intelligence List

**What it is:** A structured list with numbered items and bold headers when the post is sharing a specific taxonomy or ranked intelligence. Not a listicle. Used when the structure itself is part of the value.

**Example from Writer agent post (day-08, secondary evidence):**
> "Five human skills are commanding a salary premium in AI-era creative teams."
> *(followed by numbered bold items)*

**The pattern:**
> "[Specific number] [things I track/see] [context]."
> **1. [Bold short label]** — [one or two sentences of specific explanation]

**Rules for using it:**
- The opening line must state the exact number. Not "several" or "a few."
- The opening line must come from Martyn's direct observation ("I track this through the briefs and offers I handle") not a third-party source.
- Maximum 5 items. Fewer if each item requires more than 2 sentences.
- Each bold label must be a specific named thing, not a vague category. "Creative direction under ambiguity" is right. "Creativity" is wrong.
- Do not use bold lists for posts where the argument flows as prose. Reserve for pure intelligence delivery.

---

## Notes for Writer Agent

- If a post is missing at least one of Moves 1-4, it probably doesn't sound like Martyn.
- Move 2 (Pre-Emptive Candour) is the most uniquely his. If a post has it, readers who know Martyn will recognise it immediately.
- The biggest risk is writing competent-but-generic content that passes the do-not-use checklist but lacks these positive markers.
- When in doubt: shorten. Cut the explanatory middle. Keep the declarative opening and the short close.
- Moves 10-12 are based on secondary evidence (Writer agent output, not Martyn's own hand). They appear compatible with his voice but are lower confidence. Use when appropriate; do not force them.
